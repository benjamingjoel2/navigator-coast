Stand at the top of Alfama in the early morning and the city seems to pour away from you. Roofs of faded terracotta tumble towards the river, washing hangs between windows, and somewhere below a café shutter rattles up. Then the light arrives, bouncing off the Tagus and catching the white towers of São Vicente de Fora on the next hill. It is the oldest view in Lisbon, and it still stops people mid-step.

Alfama, Mouraria and Graça sit side by side on the slopes beneath São Jorge Castle. Together they are the Lisbon of postcards and of fado, but they are also lived-in neighbourhoods, with corner groceries, schoolchildren and neighbours talking across the lanes. The best way to understand them is slowly, on foot, with comfortable shoes and no fixed timetable.

## Alfama: the old heart

Visit Lisboa calls Alfama [the oldest and most traditional neighbourhood in Lisbon](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/alfama), a place of cobbled lanes and steep, gruelling inclines. It is not exaggerating about the slopes. Streets here are often stairways, and many of them have carried the same names for centuries: the tourism board notes that Cruzes da Sé is referenced from 1690 and Beco do Quebra-Costas has existed since 1565.

Start at the bottom, at the Sé, Lisbon's cathedral. According to [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/alfama), it dates from 1147, and visitors can see its treasury and the Roman ruins that lie beneath it. From there, the climb begins in earnest.

<Pair a="alf-rua-adica-1" b="alf-rua-adica-2" captionA="Rua da Adiça, a cobbled lane between whitewashed houses in Alfama." captionB="Stone steps climbing the same street, a few paces further on." />

Do not try to follow a map too closely. Alfama's street plan predates any idea of a grid, and the pleasure is in drifting: a tiny square with a fountain, a tiled shrine set into a wall, a view of the river that appears without warning between two houses and disappears again. If you get lost, head downhill to find the river or uphill to find the castle.

## Viewpoints: Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol

Two terraces near the top of Alfama give the classic panorama. [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/alfama) points to the Miradouro de Santa Luzia, with views over Alfama and the Tagus, and to Portas do Sol, a square with outdoor cafés and higher viewpoints nearby. The pergola and tile panels at Santa Luzia make a natural place to pause before the final climb.

Come early in the morning or late in the afternoon. At midday the light flattens the roofs and the terraces fill with tour groups; at either end of the day the river glows and the crowds thin.

## São Jorge Castle

The castle crowns the hill, and its walls are visible from almost everywhere in the old town. Opening hours change with the season: the castle's [official schedule](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/) gives longer summer hours from March to October and shorter winter hours from November to February, and lists closures on 1 January, 1 May and 24, 25 and 31 December. Wall walks and towers can close earlier than the grounds, depending on daylight.

Tickets can be bought on the day or online; the [ticket page](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/choose-your-ticket/) sets out the options. Buying ahead is worth it in high season, when the queue at the gate can be long. Once inside, walk the ramparts slowly. The views stretch across the Baixa to the river and over the rooftops you have just climbed.

## Mouraria: where fado was born

Drop down the castle's northern slope and you are in Mouraria. Its name is a history lesson. As [Visit Lisboa explains](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/mouraria), Mouraria is where the Moors were allowed to live after the Christian reconquest of Lisbon in 1147. It is also known as the birthplace of fado, and the place where Severa, a mythical figure in the world of fado, lived and died.

<Figure id="alf-rua-mouraria" caption="Shopfronts and old façades along Rua da Mouraria." />

Mouraria feels different from Alfama: less polished, more mixed, with small grocers and family restaurants alongside new cafés. The lanes are just as steep and just as tangled. Wander up from the lower streets towards the castle walls and you pass through some of the quietest corners of the old city.

If the climb to Graça looks daunting from here, there is help. Carris, Lisbon's transport company, operates the [Graça funicular](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/funiculars/), which connects Mouraria and Graça.

## Graça: terraces above the city

Graça sits higher still, and its reward is perspective. Visit Lisboa singles out the [Miradouro da Graça](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/graca) for one of the best views of Lisbon, and describes the shade of the church and its pine trees as giving the terrace a certain air of spiritual refuge. Climb a little further to the Miradouro da Senhora do Monte and the whole city spreads out below, from the castle hill down to the river.

<Figure id="alf-senhora-do-monte" size="full" caption="Evening light from the Miradouro da Senhora do Monte, with the castle hill and the river beyond." />

The famous 28 tram climbs through these streets too: the tourism board suggests [riding it to Graça](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/graca) and enjoying the view from the miradouro. The route has had a number of temporary changes, so check [Carris's service changes page](https://www.carris.pt/en/travel/alteracoes-de-servico/) before you plan around it. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers city transport tickets and passes.

## The flea market and the Pantheon

On Tuesdays and Saturdays, the Campo de Santa Clara fills with stalls. The [Feira da Ladra](https://www.visitlisboa.com/pt-pt/locais/feira-da-ladra), Lisbon's flea market, runs from 09:00 to 18:00 on those days, behind the monastery of São Vicente de Fora. Visit Lisboa traces the market back to 1272 and notes that it moved around the city before settling here. Expect old tiles, books, records, kitchenware and a great deal of glorious junk.

At the end of the square stands the white dome of the Panteão Nacional. The building began life as the church of Santa Engrácia; according to the [Panteão Nacional](https://www.panteaonacional.gov.pt/), the unfinished building was classified as a National Monument in 1910, and a law of 1916 decided it would become the National Pantheon.

## Fado in the evening

Fado belongs to these hills, and hearing it here is a different experience from hearing it anywhere else. A good place to start is the [Museu do Fado](https://www.museudofado.pt/en), on Largo do Chafariz de Dentro at the foot of Alfama. It describes itself as an interactive museum about fado, which is inscribed as Cultural Heritage of Humanity, and it opens Tuesday to Sunday.

For an evening performance, most fado houses combine dinner with music. Two examples in Alfama:

- **[Clube de Fado](https://www.clubedefado.pt/)**, on Rua de São João da Praça in the shadow of the cathedral, was founded in 1995 by the guitarist Mário Pacheco, according to its own site. Dinner and live fado are combined, with performances starting each evening.
- **[Mesa de Frades](https://mesadefrades.pt/)**, on Rua dos Remédios, occupies an 18th-century royal chapel that was later a warehouse and a tavern. Its original tile panels survive on the walls, and the space was restored in 2017.

Book ahead, arrive on time and stay quiet while the singer performs. It is a matter of respect, and the room will make that clear if you forget.

## Where to stay

Staying up here puts you in the middle of things, with the trade-off of hills and, in June, late-night noise. [memmo Alfama](https://www.memmohotels.com/alfama/), a small hotel on Travessa das Merceeiras near the cathedral, is one central option with a terrace over the rooftops. Our comparison of [Lisbon and Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) looks at the city's other neighbourhoods if you would rather sleep somewhere flatter.

## When to go

These quarters are at their most exuberant in June, when the [Festas de Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/pt-pt/eventos/festas-de-lisboa-1) honour Santo António with street parties, music and grilled sardines in Alfama, Castelo and Mouraria, among other neighbourhoods. Visit Lisboa lists the 2027 edition for June, still to be confirmed. It is joyful, crowded and loud well into the night.

For walking, spring and autumn are kinder, with softer light and cooler afternoons on the climbs. ## A suggested walk

A loose route for a full day, with plenty of room for detours:

| Stop | What to do |
|---|---|
| Sé (cathedral) | Start at the bottom; see the treasury and Roman ruins |
| Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol | Morning views over Alfama and the river |
| São Jorge Castle | Ramparts and gardens; allow a couple of hours |
| Mouraria | Lunch in the lower streets, then wander |
| Graça funicular | Ride up to Graça |
| Miradouro da Graça and Senhora do Monte | Late-afternoon light over the city |
| Alfama | Back down for dinner and fado |

On a Tuesday or Saturday, add the Feira da Ladra in the morning, then work across to the castle from there.

## Before you book

- Check the castle's current [opening times](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/) for your season, and buy tickets ahead in summer.
- Look at [Carris's service changes](https://www.carris.pt/en/travel/alteracoes-de-servico/) before relying on tram 28 or the funiculars.
- Plan flea-market visits for a Tuesday or Saturday, and fado-museum visits for any day except Monday.
- Book fado dinners in advance, especially at weekends.
- If you are visiting in June, book accommodation early and expect festival noise at night.
- Wear shoes with grip. The limestone cobbles get slippery, particularly after rain.
