Early morning on the Portuguese coast has a particular sound: the low, regular thud of swell arriving from far out in the Atlantic, and the squeak of wetsuits being pulled on in car parks above the beach. Portugal faces the open ocean along almost its entire length, and that exposure gives it waves through much of the year, from gentle whitewater for a first lesson to reef breaks that host the world's best surfers.

The question is not whether you can surf in Portugal. It is where, and when, and at what level. Here is how the main areas compare.

## Ericeira: Europe's first surfing reserve

Ericeira, a whitewashed fishing town on the coast north-west of Lisbon, has built its identity around surf. Visit Lisboa describes it as [the first surfing reserve in Europe](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/ericeira-world-surfing-reserve), recognised as both a national and a World Surfing Reserve.

<Figure id="surf-ribeira-dilhas-aerial" caption="Surfers waiting for a set over the reef at Ribeira d'Ilhas, just north of Ericeira." />

What makes the area special is the density of quality waves along a short stretch of coast. Most of them are reef and point breaks over rock, which means they are at their best for intermediate and advanced surfers who can read a lineup and handle a rocky entry. Ribeira d'Ilhas, in a valley just north of town, is the best known; Visit Lisboa lists it as part of the reserve and as the venue for a [round of the Liga MEO Surf](https://www.visitlisboa.com/pt-pt/eventos/liga-meo-surf-ericeira), Portugal's national championship.

Beginners are not left out. The sandier beaches in and around town are where most lessons take place, and the local surf schools choose spots day by day according to swell and tide. If you are learning, let the instructors decide; that local knowledge is what you are paying for.

Ericeira works well as a base without a car, and the town has its own pleasures between sessions: steep lanes, fish grills, sunset from the cliffs. Our [Ericeira guide](/stories/ericeira-guide/) covers the town, the beaches and getting there from Lisbon.

## Between Ericeira and Peniche

North of Ericeira the coast becomes wilder and less developed. High cliffs alternate with long beaches at the mouths of small valleys, and the towns are spaced out. Santa Cruz is the best known of these, with a long beach broken by rock outcrops.

<Figure id="surf-santa-cruz-cliffs" caption="Late light on the cliffs and shore break at Santa Cruz, on the open coast between Ericeira and Peniche." />

This stretch rewards surfers with a car and some patience. It is generally less crowded than either Ericeira or Peniche, and on the right swell and wind some of these beaches are excellent. It is also more exposed, with fewer facilities, so it is not the place for a first unsupervised session.

## Peniche: waves from two directions

Peniche sits on a rocky peninsula that pushes out into the Atlantic, and that geography is the key to its surf. Beaches on either side of the peninsula face different directions, so when the wind is wrong on one side it is often better on the other. That makes Peniche one of the most consistent places in Portugal to find a rideable wave.

<Pair a="surf-peniche-aerial" b="surf-peniche-surfer" captionA="The Peniche peninsula from above, with reefs and white water on every side." captionB="Heading out at Peniche, with a rocky islet on the horizon." />

The headline wave is Supertubos, the hollow beach break south of town. The regional tourism board's [Peniche surf guide](https://centerofportugal.com/article/peniche-a-surfers-paradise) gives it the top difficulty rating of five out of five, and describes the Rip Curl Pro Portugal held there as one of the most prestigious competitions. The World Surf League's Championship Tour stop is listed on the WSL site at [Supertubos, Peniche](https://www.worldsurfleague.com/events/2025/ct/322/meo-rip-curl-pro-portugal/main); the 2025 event ran in March, so check the WSL calendar for the current year if you want to watch.

For everyone else, the same guide points beginners to Praia Norte and notes that learners can surf beside Lagide at the small beach known as Prainha. The bay running round to Baleal, on the north side of the peninsula, is where many surf camps and schools gather.

## Nazaré: for watching, not surfing

Just north of Peniche, Nazaré is a different proposition entirely. Its Praia do Norte is famous for some of the biggest waves ever surfed, created by an undersea canyon off the coast. According to [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-big-waves), the canyon is at least 5,000 metres deep and about 230 km long, the big-wave season begins in November and ends in February, and a big-wave challenge has been held there since 2011.

This is not surfing for visitors; it is a spectacle. The place to watch is the Forte de São Miguel Arcanjo on the headland, where the [Surfer Wall](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-surfer-wall) displays boards ridden by big-wave surfers. Our [guide to Nazaré's big waves](/stories/nazare-big-waves/) explains when the giant days are likely and how to watch safely.

## Lisbon's train-line beaches

If you are staying in Lisbon and want to surf without hiring a car, the beaches west of the city along the Cascais railway are the obvious choice. Carcavelos, a long sandy beach below an old fort, is a stop on CP's [Cais do Sodré to Cascais line](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/comboios-urbanos-lisboa-cascais-1), and has a busy surf scene with schools and board hire.

<Figure id="surf-carcavelos-fort" caption="A wave peels in front of the fort at Carcavelos, a train ride from central Lisbon." />

The waves here are generally gentler than on the open west coast, which suits beginners and improvers, but they need a decent swell to work well, and the beach gets crowded on summer weekends. South of the Tagus, the long beaches of Costa da Caparica offer more space. Our [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) guide explains how Lisbon's suburban trains and ticketing work.

## The Algarve's west coast

Most people associate the Algarve with calm coves, but its western edge, from Sagres north towards the Alentejo border, faces straight into the Atlantic and picks up swell much more readily. Beaches such as Arrifana, Amado and Bordeira are long, open and backed by cliffs or dunes, with surf schools and a relaxed, outdoorsy feel.

The appeal is variety within a short drive. Sagres sits on the corner where the south-facing and west-facing coasts meet, so you can choose the side that suits the day's wind and swell. The [Pousada Sagres](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-sagres) in the village describes itself as about ten minutes' walk from the beach, with views of the Sagres fortress and Cabo de São Vicente. Our [Sagres and Cape St Vincent](/stories/sagres-and-cape-st-vincent/) guide covers the area beyond the beaches.

## When to go

As a broad rule, the bigger swells come in the colder months; Nazaré's giant-wave season, which [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-big-waves) puts at November to February, is the extreme end of that pattern. Summer tends to bring smaller, gentler surf and warmer days. That makes summer the easier season for complete beginners and autumn the favourite of experienced surfers, with spring somewhere in between.

Whatever the month, check the forecast. [IPMA's coastal forecasts](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) give hourly wave height, swell period, wind and water temperature for named surf beaches, including Ribeira d'Ilhas. The Atlantic stays cool year-round, so a full wetsuit is standard for most of the year.

## Staying safe

Portuguese beaches can be powerful places. The UK government's [Portugal safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) warns that waves can be unpredictable, that some beaches have a strong undertow and rip currents, and that hidden rocks or shallow depths can cause serious injury. It also notes that supervised beaches use a flag system, with red meaning you must not enter the water.

For surfers, that translates into a few habits. Do not paddle out alone at an unfamiliar break. Ask locally about rocks and currents before your first session. Respect the lineup and right of way, especially at crowded reef breaks around Ericeira and Peniche. And if you are a beginner, take lessons rather than renting a board and guessing.

## Before you book

- Be honest about your level, then choose the area: Carcavelos, Baleal and the Ericeira town beaches for learning; the reefs of Ericeira and Supertubos only once you are confident.
- If you want to watch the pros at Supertubos, check the [WSL event page](https://www.worldsurfleague.com/events/2025/ct/322/meo-rip-curl-pro-portugal/main) and calendar for this year's dates.
- For a Lisbon-based trip, check the current [Cascais line timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/comboios-urbanos-lisboa-cascais-1) for Carcavelos.
- Watch the [IPMA coastal forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) in the days before you travel, and be ready to change beaches.
- Pack or hire a full wetsuit; the Atlantic here is cool even in summer.
- Learn the beach flags and respect them.
