Lisbon wakes up slowly. The first trams grind uphill out of the Baixa, the light comes off the Tagus and catches the white limestone pavements, and by the time the cafés have their tables out, the city's seven hills have turned from grey to gold. A week later you will be standing on a granite quayside in Porto watching the same sun go down behind a different river.

That is the shape of this trip: two cities, one train ride between them, and a day in the hills of Sintra. It is the itinerary we suggest most often to people coming to Portugal for the first time with seven nights to spend, and it works because it does not try to do too much.

## The plan at a glance

| Day | Where | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Lisbon | Alfama, Baixa, Chiado, Belém |
| 3 | Sintra | Palaces and gardens, back to Lisbon for the night |
| 4 | Lisbon | A slower day: viewpoints, markets, a fado evening |
| 5 | Train north | Lisbon to Porto, afternoon in the Ribeira |
| 6–7 | Porto | Old centre, Gaia's Port lodges, the river mouth at Foz |

Four nights in Lisbon and three in Porto is the right split. Lisbon is bigger and Sintra eats a full day; Porto is compact and rewards you for wandering. If you are still torn about which city deserves more time, our comparison of [Lisbon and Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) goes into the differences in more detail.

## Days 1 and 2: Lisbon's old hills and the river

Start high. Alfama, the oldest neighbourhood, tumbles down the hill below the castle in a maze of stairways, laundry lines and tiny squares, and it is best explored on foot and without a plan. Climb to the viewpoints at Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol for the view over the rooftops to the river, then let gravity take you down towards the Baixa, the flat grid of streets rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake.

<Pair a="wk1-lisbon-tram" b="wk1-sintra-pena" captionA="A yellow tram on one of Lisbon's steep old-town streets." captionB="The red and yellow walls of Pena Palace, high above Sintra." />

The trams are part of the fun, though on the busiest routes you may spend more time queueing than riding. For getting around, the Lisbon metro's [ticket page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/) explains the navegante occasional card, which can be topped up with tickets or money and is valid across the member operators; there are also 24-hour tickets, including one that covers Carris buses and trams, the metro and CP's suburban trains to Sintra and Cascais. Check the current options there rather than relying on what a friend paid last year.

In the Baixa, stop for something sweet at [Confeitaria Nacional](https://www.confeitarianacional.com/) on Praça da Figueira. The pastry shop has been on the square since it opened in 1829, and it says it introduced the bolo-rei, the Christmas crown cake, to Portugal in 1875.

On day two, go west along the river to Belém. Visit Lisboa notes that the Jerónimos Monastery was [proclaimed a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/Heritage), and Belém Tower shares the listing. Give the monastery's cloister an hour, walk the waterfront to the tower, and do not be in a hurry. Our guide to [Belém in a day](/stories/belem-in-a-day/) has a fuller route, including what to see if the queues are long.

## Day 3: Sintra

Sintra is the day trip everyone makes, and for good reason. CP notes that its cultural landscape was [classified by UNESCO in 1995](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/sintra-heritage), and the hills above the old town are scattered with palaces, castles and romantic gardens built by kings and wealthy dreamers in the 19th century.

Getting there is easy. The Sintra line runs from Lisbon's Rossio station, which CP's history of [the Sintra line](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/institucional/the-sintra-line) describes as now serving a purely suburban role; the current Lisbon–Sintra timetable is on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf).

Most of the big monuments, including Pena Palace, the Moorish Castle, Monserrate and the National Palace in the town centre, are run by [Parques de Sintra](https://www.parquesdesintra.pt/en/), which has an online ticket office. [Quinta da Regaleira](https://www.regaleira.pt/en/), with its gardens and initiation well, is managed separately and sells timed tickets online; its site notes a maximum grace period of one hour after your booked entry time. Pick two or three sites, not five. The hills are steep, the paths between the palaces are longer than they look on a map, and the afternoon crowds are real. If you want to plan the day in detail, our [Sintra day trip guide](/stories/sintra-day-trip/) does exactly that.

Before you catch the train back, call in at [Piriquita](https://www.piriquita.pt/) in the old town. The bakery was founded in 1862 and is known for two local sweets: the travesseiro, a puff-pastry pillow filled with egg and almond cream, and the queijada de Sintra.

## Day 4: Lisbon, slowly

Keep day four loose. You have earned a morning with no tickets to show anyone. Wander up through Chiado and Bairro Alto to the São Pedro de Alcântara viewpoint, cross to the Príncipe Real neighbourhood for its garden and shops, or take the ferry across the river for the view back at the city.

In the evening, listen to fado. The music belongs to Alfama and Mouraria, and a small, quiet room where the guitar is not competing with the cutlery is worth seeking out. Book ahead if you have a particular place in mind.

If you are still deciding where to base yourself, our guide to [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) compares the main neighbourhoods. For a short trip, staying central (Baixa, Chiado or the lower edge of Alfama) saves you a lot of uphill walking at the end of the day.

## Day 5: The train to Porto

The fast way north is CP's Alfa Pendular, which links Lisbon and Porto and carries on to Braga. CP's [Alfa Pendular page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/alfa-pendular-train) describes the trains' facilities, including power sockets at every seat, free wi-fi and a café-bar carriage, and you can buy tickets online. The Intercidades trains on the same route are a little slower. Fares and journey times change with the timetable, so check them on CP's site when you book, and book early if you are travelling on a Friday or Sunday.

Arrive in time for a long afternoon in the Ribeira, the riverside quarter where tall, narrow houses in every shade of ochre and blue are stacked up the hill above the quay. Cross the upper deck of the Dom Luís I bridge on foot, if you have the legs for it, for the classic view back over the old town.

## Days 6 and 7: Porto and the Port lodges

Porto's historic centre carries [UNESCO World Heritage status](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB), and the city still feels like a working place rather than a museum. Start your first full day at the cathedral terrace, walk down to São Bento station to see the blue-and-white tiled entrance hall, and head up to Rua das Carmelitas.

There, [Livraria Lello](https://www.livrarialello.pt/en) has been in its building since 1906. Entry is by ticket, which you can book online, and each ticket works as a voucher towards the price of a book. Go early in the day if you want to see the famous staircase without a crowd on it.

For lunch, the [Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/) is the city's historic market, with produce stalls, small shops and restaurants. The market's site lists its opening hours (it is closed on Sundays) and posted a storm closure in November 2026, so check its news before you go.

Spend the second afternoon across the river in Vila Nova de Gaia, where the Port wine houses age their wines. Most offer tours and tastings; [Graham's](https://www.grahams-port.com/) says its lodge was built in 1890 and runs guided visits of its museum, lodge and bottle cellar followed by a tasting, bookable online. Our guide to the [Port lodges of Gaia](/stories/port-lodges-of-gaia/) covers the others and how to choose between them.

<Pair a="wk1-porto-dusk" b="wk1-porto-ribeira" captionA="The Ribeira waterfront lights up as the sun goes down." captionB="Old Porto rising from the river on a clear day." />

On your last evening, take a bus or tram out to Foz do Douro, where the river meets the Atlantic. Walk along the seafront as the light goes, and eat grilled fish within sight of the waves.

If you have an extra day, the obvious addition is the Douro Valley, a train ride upriver from São Bento. Our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) explains how to do it properly.

## When to go

Spring and early autumn are the sweet spots: warm enough for long days on foot, without the full heat and crowds of July and August. Winter is mild in Lisbon and wetter in Porto, and you will have the viewpoints largely to yourself. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) has more on what to expect.

## Before you book

- Check entry rules before you travel. GOV.UK's [Portugal entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/entry-requirements) page explains the EU's new Entry/Exit System, the 90-days-in-180 rule for visa-free visitors and the passport validity rules.
- Book the Lisbon to Porto train on [CP's Alfa Pendular page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/alfa-pendular-train) as soon as your dates are fixed.
- Buy Sintra tickets in advance from [Parques de Sintra](https://www.parquesdesintra.pt/en/) and [Quinta da Regaleira](https://www.regaleira.pt/en/), and plan your train around your entry slot.
- Book Livraria Lello and any Port lodge visit online a few days ahead in busy months.
- Fly into Lisbon and out of Porto (or the reverse) if you can, so you are never backtracking.
- Pack shoes with grip. Both cities are steep, and polished limestone and granite get slippery in the rain.
