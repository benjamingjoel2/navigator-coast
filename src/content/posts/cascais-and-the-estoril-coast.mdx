The train leaves Cais do Sodré with the river right outside the window. For most of the journey the Tagus is so close you could almost lean out and touch it: container cranes, then the white bulk of the monuments at Belém, then the estuary widening until the far bank slips away and the water in front of you is simply the Atlantic. Forty minutes after leaving the city you step out at Cascais to the cry of gulls and the smell of salt.

That ride is half the pleasure of a day on the Estoril coast. The other half is what waits at the end of it: a seaside promenade strung between small sandy coves, a fishing harbour that still works, a cluster of good museums, and, just beyond the last houses, a stretch of wild Atlantic shore that feels a long way from any capital.

## The Cascais line

The railway between Lisbon and Cascais is one of the four lines that make up CP's [Lisbon urban network](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/lisbon), and it is the easiest way to reach the coast. Trains start at Cais do Sodré, on the riverfront west of the Baixa, and run along the shore through Belém, Oeiras, Carcavelos, the Estoril stations and Monte Estoril to Cascais.

On CP's [weekday timetable valid from 8 November 2026](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/cascais-8nov-uteis), the full run takes around 40 minutes, and through the middle of the day there is a train roughly every 20 minutes in each direction. The [weekend and holiday timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/cascais-8nov-fds) keeps a similar daytime rhythm, thinning to every half hour in the evening. Trains keep running late, so there is no need to rush dinner. Timetables do change, so check the current PDFs on CP's site before you travel.

For tickets, CP's [sales points page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/sales-points-lisbon-urban-trains) lists ticket offices and vending machines at stations, plus on-board sales only where a station has no other way to buy. Check the current fare when you buy. If you are coming for the museums, CP and Cascais also run a [combined Museum Quarter ticket](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/museum-quarter) that covers 24 hours of travel on the Cascais line and entry to four Cascais museums, sold at CP ticket offices on the line.

A tip for the outward journey: sit on the left-hand side, facing forward. That is the river side, and the view is the whole point. For more on Portugal's trains and how the urban networks fit together, see our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

## Estoril and the seafront promenade

You do not have to ride all the way to the end of the line. Many people get off at São João do Estoril or Estoril and walk the last stretch to Cascais along the seafront promenade, the paredão, which hugs the shore between a string of small beaches. It is flat, paved and busy with walkers, runners and families, and it brings you into Cascais beside the town beaches.

Estoril still carries itself like the seaside resort it has long been: palm trees, gardens running down to the station and villas with towers and battlements built for the view. Look out for the castellated houses that stand right above the sea; they are among the most photographed buildings on the coast.

The grand dame of the resort is the [Palácio Estoril Hotel](https://www.palacioestorilhotel.com/), in Estoril. Its own website describes nearly a century of hospitality and a Royal Gallery, created in February 2011 in honour of the Spanish, Italian, French, Bulgarian and Romanian royal families who stayed at the hotel. It is worth knowing about whether or not you stay, simply because it explains so much about the tone of this stretch of coast.

## Cascais: harbour, beaches and the old town

Cascais itself is compact enough to cover on foot. The station sits a few minutes from the bay, where small fishing boats ride at anchor below a row of grand seafront houses. Just along the shore are the little town beaches, such as Praia da Rainha: pockets of golden sand tucked between rocks and the backs of whitewashed buildings.

<Figure id="cas-rainha-beach" caption="Early morning on a small cove of sand and rock below the old town of Cascais." />

Behind the bay, the old centre is a tangle of pedestrian lanes paved in black-and-white calçada, the patterned stone mosaic you will see all over Portugal. It is the place to wander, find a pastry and let the day slow down. For an ice cream, [Santini](https://www.santini.pt/) is the obvious name: the geladaria says it has been in the family since 1949, when Attilio Santini first opened its doors, and it lists a shop in Cascais.

If you want to see what local cooks are buying, head inland to the [Mercado da Vila](https://www.visitcascais.com/en/partners/mercado-da-vila), the town market on Rua Padre Moisés da Silva. Visit Cascais describes its stalls of fresh seafood, fruit, vegetables and local produce, along with food festivals and themed fairs. Our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) will help you decode the fish counter.

On the western side of the bay rises the Cidadela, the old maritime fortress. Part of it is now the [Pestana Cidadela Cascais Pousada & Art District](https://www.pestana.com/en/hotel/fortress-cascais), a hotel inside the citadel walls. Pestana describes the citadel as growing from a tower dating back to the late 15th century, and the hotel includes an Art District of galleries and artists' studios that forms part of the town's Museum Quarter.

## The Museum Quarter

Cascais has gathered its museums into a single neighbourhood, the Bairro dos Museus, around the gardens and the lighthouse at the western end of town. The building most visitors come for is the [Casa das Histórias Paula Rego](https://bairrodosmuseus.cascais.pt/list/museu/casa-das-historias-paula-rego), on Avenida da República. According to [Visit Cascais](https://www.visitcascais.com/en/partners/casa-das-historias-paula-rego), it was designed by the architect Eduardo Souto de Moura, opened in 2009 and holds paintings, drawings and prints by Paula Rego and her husband, Victor Willing. Even from outside, its red concrete and pyramid-shaped towers are unforgettable.

CP's Museum Quarter ticket names the Casa das Histórias alongside the Centro Cultural de Cascais, the Palácio da Cidadela de Cascais and the Casa Duarte Pinto Coelho. Check each museum's opening days before you build a day around them; museums in Portugal commonly close one day a week.

## Boca do Inferno

From the museum gardens, keep walking west along the coast road. In a few minutes the tidy town gives way to cliffs, and you reach the [Boca do Inferno](https://www.visitcascais.com/en/partners/boca-do-inferno), the Mouth of Hell. Visit Cascais describes it as a rock formation carved by the sea, where Atlantic waves crash through an open arch, and notes the coastal walkway that links it to the centre.

On a calm summer day it can seem almost peaceful. Come after a winter storm, though, and you will understand the name: the swell booms into the hollow below the cliffs and throws spray high above the viewing platforms. Stay behind the railings. These are real cliffs above a real ocean, and the rock edges are not a place for photographs.

## Further west: Guincho and the wild coast

Beyond Boca do Inferno the coast turns north and changes character completely. The sheltered coves give way to open Atlantic beaches, dunes and headlands within the Sintra-Cascais Natural Park.

The best known is [Guincho Beach](https://www.visitcascais.com/en/partners/guincho-beach), which Visit Cascais places inside the park, with white dunes set against the hills of the Serra de Sintra. It is a beach for long walks, watching the surf and staying for the sunset; it is not the gentle, sheltered water of the town coves, so swim only where lifeguards are on duty and the flags allow it.

<Figure id="cas-guincho-dusk" caption="Waves rolling onto the sand at Praia do Guincho as the sun sets over the Atlantic." />

If you want to stretch your legs, Visit Cascais publishes a waymarked [Guincho coastal route](https://www.visitcascais.com/en/partners/route-guincho-coastal): a circular walk of 9.9 km from Malveira da Serra, rated easy, that takes in the rugged shore past Forte do Guincho with the Sintra hills behind. Without a car, you will need a taxi or a local bus to reach the coast or the trailhead, so plan your return before you set out.

Cascais also makes a natural pairing with Sintra, just over the hills. Many visitors combine the two over two days; our [Sintra day trip guide](/stories/sintra-day-trip/) covers the palaces and how to get there, and [Lisbon or Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) explains why the Cascais coast is one of the big arguments for basing yourself in the capital.

## When to go

Summer is beach season, and the town coves fill up fast at weekends. Portugal's government announced that the 2026 [bathing season in Cascais](https://portugal.gov.pt/gc25/comunicacao/noticias/epoca-balnear-arranca-em-cascais-e-madeira-sob-maior-exigencia-na-protecao-do-litoral) began on 1 May, earlier than in many parts of the country, and advised bathers to use supervised, identified beaches.

Outside summer, the coast is quieter and in many ways better for a train day: the promenade is pleasant for walking, the museums are calm, and a stormy sea makes Boca do Inferno far more dramatic. Our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons in more detail.

## Before you book

- Check the current Cascais line timetable PDFs on [CP's site](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/cascais-8nov-uteis) for the day you travel, including weekends and holidays.
- Buy your ticket at the station before boarding; on-board sales apply only where a station has no other way to buy.
- If you plan to visit several Cascais museums, compare the [Museum Quarter ticket](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/museum-quarter) with separate train and museum tickets.
- Check museum opening days in advance, and keep an eye on the weather if Boca do Inferno or Guincho is the main event.
- Swim only at supervised beaches during the bathing season, and treat the open Atlantic beaches with respect.
