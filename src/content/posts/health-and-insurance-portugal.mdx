Every Portuguese town has one: a green cross, lit up and often blinking, hanging over a narrow shopfront on the main street. Step inside and you will usually find a long wooden counter, a wall of tidy drawers and a pharmacist who has heard every holiday ailment going, from sunburn to blisters to the stomach that did not agree with the third plate of clams.

Most trips to Portugal pass without needing anything more serious than that. But a little preparation before you go makes the rare bad day far easier: knowing which number to call, what your health card actually covers, and why it is not the same thing as travel insurance. This is our plain guide to all three.

## In an emergency: 112

The number to remember is **112**. The UK Foreign Office's [health advice for Portugal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/health) tells travellers to dial 112 and ask for an ambulance in an emergency. It is the same European emergency number you may already know from home, and it works from any phone.

The same page adds a step people often forget: if you are taken to hospital or referred to a clinic, contact your insurer or its medical assistance company as soon as you can. Insurers generally want to know early, particularly if treatment is going to be expensive or you may need help getting home.

Save the 112 number and your insurer's 24-hour assistance line in your phone before you leave, and keep a paper copy of your policy number in your wallet. On a cliff path in the Algarve or a ridge in the Azores, that small piece of admin is worth a great deal.

## When it is not an emergency: SNS 24

For anything short of an emergency, Portugal's national health service runs a phone advice line, **SNS 24**. Its [own site](https://www.sns24.gov.pt/en/) describes it as the digital front door of the SNS, with a line that advises callers and directs them to the most suitable health unit for their symptoms.

The numbers differ by region. According to the [GOV.UK health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/health):

| Where you are | SNS 24 number |
|---|---|
| Mainland Portugal | +351 808 24 24 24 (press 9 for English) |
| Madeira and Porto Santo | +351 800 24 24 20 |
| Azores | +351 808 24 60 24 |

It is a good first call for a child with a temperature at night, a reaction to an insect bite or a fall that probably is not serious but might be. The nurse can tell you whether to wait it out, see a pharmacist, go to a health centre or head to hospital.

## Pharmacies

For minor problems, the farmácia is your friend. The [GOV.UK advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/health) notes that most medicines in Portugal are sold only in pharmacies, though some non-prescription products turn up in supermarket and shopping-centre health shops. It also points travellers to the pharmacy association's [pharmacy finder](https://www.farmaciasportuguesas.pt/catalogo/pesquisar-farmacias), which is useful for locating one nearby when you are somewhere unfamiliar.

Outside normal hours, pharmacies take turns to stay open. Look for the notice in a pharmacy window, which normally lists the nearest one on duty that night, or search the finder.

### Bringing your own medicines

This is where travellers get caught out. The Foreign Office says that medicine rules differ in Portugal, that there are strict rules on dispensing some medicines, including antibiotics, and that **UK prescriptions are not recognised** there. Its advice is to:

- bring enough of any regular medication for the whole trip;
- keep it in its original packaging, with the prescription label;
- carry a few days' supply in your hand luggage, in case a checked bag goes astray.

If you do run out, the same page says you can go to a health centre or a hospital A&E for a repeat prescription, but you may have to pay. Travellers from outside the UK should check their own government's advice, as the principle is the same: do not count on a home prescription being filled.

## GHIC and EHIC: what they cover

If you are insured in an EU country, the free **European Health Insurance Card** is your passport to Portugal's public health system. The EU's [Your Europe guide](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/health/unplanned-healthcare/temporary-stays/index_en.htm) says the card gives you treatment that cannot wait until you get home, on the same terms as people insured in the country you are visiting. It is free from your own health insurer, and the EU warns against websites that charge for it.

UK residents use the **Global Health Insurance Card** instead, or a valid UK-issued EHIC. The NHS [GHIC page](https://www.nhs.uk/using-the-nhs/healthcare-abroad/apply-for-a-free-uk-global-health-insurance-card-ghic/) says the card is free, lasts up to five years, and covers necessary state healthcare such as emergency treatment, A&E and care for long-term or pre-existing conditions. It also notes that some treatment, such as dialysis or chemotherapy, should be arranged in advance, and that you may still be charged where local residents would pay. Apply only through the official NHS service; the NHS, too, warns that unofficial sites may charge a fee.

## What the cards do not cover

This is the important part. Both the EU and the UK are blunt that the card is **not a substitute for travel insurance**.

According to the EU's [guide](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/health/unplanned-healthcare/temporary-stays/index_en.htm), the EHIC does not cover private healthcare, planned treatment, or rescue and repatriation, and it applies only to public-system providers. The [GOV.UK health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/health) lists the gaps for UK travellers: changes to travel or accommodation, medical repatriation to the UK, non-urgent treatment and private clinics. The NHS adds mountain rescue to the list.

In practice, that matters more than people expect. A tourist taken to a private clinic, a flight home on a stretcher, or a rescue from a remote trail will not be paid for by the card.

## Choosing travel insurance

The Foreign Office's general guidance on [travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance) is sensible wherever you live:

- **Buy early.** It suggests arranging cover soon after you book, so that cancellation protection starts straight away.
- **Declare everything.** Tell the insurer about any existing health conditions, including tests or treatment you are waiting for. The guidance warns that failing to declare something may invalidate the policy.
- **Check your activities.** Make sure the policy covers what you actually plan to do. Adventure sports often need specialist cover or an add-on.
- **Read the exclusions.** Many policies do not cover incidents after heavy drinking.

Think about the trip you are taking. A city break in Lisbon and Porto carries different risks from a week of [walking Madeira's levadas](/stories/levada-walks-for-beginners/), surfing the west coast, or [hiking in the Azores](/stories/azores-hiking/), where a twisted ankle on a remote path may mean a rescue and a long transfer. If you plan to surf, canyon, dive or take a boat out whale watching, check the policy names it.

If you need a **Schengen visa** to visit Portugal, insurance is not optional. The European Commission's page on [applying for a Schengen visa](https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen-borders-and-visa/visa-policy/applying-schengen-visa_en) lists medical insurance covering emergency care, hospitalisation and repatriation among the documents you need. Check the exact requirements with the consulate handling your application.

## Everyday health risks

Portugal is not a high-risk destination, but a few things are worth knowing.

**Sun and heat.** Summer in the south and the interior can be fierce. Check the [IPMA weather warnings](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) for heat alerts and the [UV index forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev.uv/) before a beach day or a long walk, plan the hardest exercise for the morning, and carry more water than you think you need. GOV.UK also notes that heat, late nights and changes in diet can affect how some medicines work. Our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons in more detail.

**Bites and stings.** TravelHealthPro's [Portugal page](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/portugal) advises avoiding insect and tick bites, notes that bats may carry bat lyssavirus, and says any bat bite or scratch needs prompt local medical assessment, even if it looks minor. For [Madeira](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/madeira-portugal), it notes a risk of dengue, carried by mosquitoes that mainly bite during the day, so pack repellent.

**Vaccinations.** TravelHealthPro says travellers should be up to date with routine vaccines and boosters. Check with your doctor or a travel clinic well before you go.

**The sea.** Atlantic beaches can be rough. Swim at lifeguarded beaches where you can, respect the flags, and heed cliff warnings: our guide to [Algarve beaches](/stories/algarve-beaches/) explains the coast.

## Before you book

- Apply for a free GHIC or EHIC through the official [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/using-the-nhs/healthcare-abroad/apply-for-a-free-uk-global-health-insurance-card-ghic/) or your national insurer, and check it is in date.
- Buy travel insurance when you book, declare any conditions, and confirm it covers your planned activities and repatriation.
- Save 112, the right [SNS 24](https://www.sns24.gov.pt/en/) number for mainland Portugal, Madeira or the Azores, and your insurer's emergency line.
- Pack enough of your regular medicines for the whole trip, in original packaging, with a few days' supply in hand luggage.
- Check the [GOV.UK health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/health), or your own government's equivalent, close to departure, as advice can change.
- For first-time visitors, our [first-timer's guide to Portugal](/stories/first-timers-guide-to-portugal/) covers the rest of the practicalities.
