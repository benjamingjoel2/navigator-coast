Stand on the rim above Sete Cidades on São Miguel and the cloud can pour over the crater edge like milk, hiding one lake, then the other, then lifting all at once to show you both. Stand on a levada path above Funchal and you get a different kind of drama: a narrow channel of water cut into a near-vertical green slope, with the Atlantic glittering a long way below.

Both archipelagos are Portuguese, volcanic and spectacularly green. Both are mid-Atlantic and reward walkers. But they feel very different on the ground, and the right one for you depends on what kind of trip you are after.

## The short answer

- **Choose Madeira** if you want one base, reliable mild weather on the south coast, a lively small city, dramatic mountain and levada walks, and a trip that is easy to organise.
- **Choose the Azores** if you want wilder, emptier landscapes, crater lakes and pastureland, serious whale watching, and the adventure of island-hopping. You will need to accept that the weather will shape your plans.

## Landscape

Madeira is one steep island, plus quieter Porto Santo. It is all ridges, ravines and terraces, with a high central massif and the laurel forest, the Laurissilva, clinging to the wetter north. Funchal climbs its amphitheatre of hills above the harbour. Everything feels vertical.

The Azores are nine islands scattered across the ocean, and each has its own character. São Miguel has crater lakes, tea fields and hot springs. Pico is dominated by the volcano that gives it its name. Faial looks across the channel at Pico. Flores, far to the west, is all waterfalls and sea cliffs. The landscape is gentler and more pastoral than Madeira's, with hydrangea hedges, stone walls and cows grazing to the cliff edge. It is also emptier.

## Weather: changeable versus microclimates

IPMA's 1991–2020 climate normals show the difference clearly. At [Funchal](https://www.ipma.pt/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_FUNCHAL.pdf), the average January high is 20.0°C and the August high is 26.8°C, with about 574 mm of rain a year and roughly 53 days with at least 1 mm. At [Ponta Delgada](https://www.ipma.pt/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_PONTA_DELGADA_OBSERVATORIO.pdf) on São Miguel, January averages 17.5°C and August 26.0°C, but annual rainfall is about 988 mm, spread over around 122 days with at least 1 mm.

In practice, that means the Azores rarely get cold, but they are often wet, windy and changeable. You can have four seasons before lunch, and a crater-lake viewpoint can be clear one hour and socked in the next. The trick is to stay flexible: keep two or three plans for each day and pick one after you look at the sky.

Madeira's story is about altitude and aspect rather than season. [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/weather-in-madeira/) explains that the south is usually warmer and drier, while the north and the mountains are more exposed to wind and rain, and that you can be in fog up high while the coast is sunny. Funchal's figures describe the sunny south. Up at the peaks or on the north coast, pack as you would for the Azores. The same page puts sea temperatures at around 18°C in winter and 24°C in summer.

## Walking

### Madeira: levadas, booking and fees

Madeira's walking is built around the levadas, the irrigation channels that contour the hillsides, and the steeper veredas, or footpaths, that climb to the peaks. The regional government's forestry and nature institute, IFCN, manages a list of **classified walking routes**. Visit Madeira publishes the [full list with each route's current status](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/notice-to-walkers-and-list-of-walks/), marked open, restricted or closed. When we checked in November 2026, several routes, including some around Rabaçal and the accessible Caldeirão Verde path, were marked closed.

The rules changed in 2026. According to [IFCN's FAQ on fees and reservations](https://ifcn.madeira.gov.pt/en/atividades-de-natureza/percursos-pedestres-recomendados/faq-s-taxas-percursos-pedestres-classificados/frequently-asked-questions-fees-classified-walking-routes.html):

- Each classified trail managed by IFCN is booked by entry time slot, and booking is **only online, through the SIMplifica portal**.
- Registration is mandatory for everyone, including people who are exempt from paying.
- Non-resident visitors pay a fee per trail, with combined day and multi-day options. Check the current amounts on that page rather than relying on anything you read elsewhere.
- Children aged 12 and under do not pay, though they still need to be registered.
- Slots close automatically when they reach capacity. A trail that is closed cannot be booked at all.

The practical upshot is that you should book popular trails a few days ahead and build your plans around the slots you get.

**Levada safety.** Many levada paths are narrow, sometimes unfenced, and can involve long, dark tunnels and sheer drops. Visit Madeira's walkers' notice sets out the basics:

- Check the route's status before you start.
- Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back.
- Allow time to finish before nightfall.
- Carry extra food and water, wear suitable clothes and shoes, and take a phone.
- In heavy rain or strong wind, don't go on; turn back the way you came.

[Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/weather-in-madeira/) also warns that rain, wind or fog can lead to temporary trail closures. We would add a head torch for the tunnels, and a firm rule never to walk a closed route, however inviting it looks.

### The Azores: a trail network across nine islands

The official [Azores Trails](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/alerts) site, run by the regional tourism body, lists marked routes on every island. You can filter them by island, difficulty and length, and the site keeps an **alerts page** of temporarily closed trails. When we checked in November 2026, closures included routes on Santa Maria, São Miguel, São Jorge and Flores. Check it the night before and again in the morning, since storms can close paths quickly.

The big one is Pico mountain, Portugal's highest peak. The regional government's information on the [Casa da Montanha](https://siaram.azores.gov.pt/centros-interpretacao/casa-da-montanha/Casa-da-Montanha.pdf) describes it as a compulsory stop: its main job is to register and control every ascent. Treat it as a serious mountain day and turn back if the weather changes.

## Whale watching

The Azores are the stronger choice here. [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/experience-the-azores/whale-watching) says whale and dolphin watching is possible all year. Spotted dolphins are more common in summer, blue whales are most often seen at the end of winter, and sperm whales are frequent in summer. The same page sets out the code of conduct. It is forbidden to chase, disturb or feed cetaceans, to swim with whales, or to have more than three boats at the same spot. Boats must travel alongside the animals at constant speed and keep more than 50 metres away, or 100 metres if there are calves. Some trips offer swimming with resident dolphins, but never with whales.

Madeira also has whale and dolphin trips. Its [regional regulation](https://ifcn.madeira.gov.pt/images/Doc_Artigos/ActNatureza/ObserVertMarinhos/Legislacao/dlrn152013m.pdf) requires operators to be authorised by IFCN and limits how many boats may be close to the animals at once. Either way, choose a licensed operator and expect trips to be cancelled when the sea is rough.

## Getting there and getting around

### Madeira

Madeira is simple. You fly into the island's international airport, east of Funchal, and stay on one island. Visit Madeira's [guide to getting around](https://visitmadeira.com/en/blog/visit-madeira/everything-you-need-to-know-about-getting-around-madeira/) says you can explore by public bus, but that many viewpoints and trailheads are not near a stop and routes need planning. A car gives you more freedom. Be ready for roads that are steep, with sharp bends and occasional narrow sections in the mountains. Drive them in daylight, and give yourself time.

### The Azores

The Azores take more planning. Islands are linked by inter-island flights and by Atlânticoline ferries. [Atlânticoline's timetable](https://www.atlanticoline.pt/horarios/) shows connections between ports on Faial, Pico, São Jorge, Terceira, Graciosa, Flores and Corvo. The Horta–Madalena crossing between Faial and Pico is the easiest hop to fit into a trip. Check sailings for your exact dates, because the lines and frequencies vary through the year and bad weather can cancel crossings. On each island, a hire car is the practical way to reach trailheads and viewpoints.

### Combining both

It is possible to do both in one trip. Azores Airlines flies between Ponta Delgada and Funchal, and from the 2026 summer season it added a [direct Terceira–Funchal route](https://www.azoresairlines.pt/en/node/1916). That route runs weekly, with a second weekly flight between June and September. A two-week trip could split roughly a week on Madeira with a week on São Miguel, or on Terceira plus the central islands, but check the current schedule before you build an itinerary around it.

## Where to base yourself

**Madeira.** Funchal is the obvious base, with the most restaurants, buses and evening life, and the sunniest weather. Its drawback is that the best walks in the north and west mean early starts and long drives. For a walking-focused trip, consider splitting your stay: a few nights in Funchal, then a few on the north coast or in the west, closer to the trailheads.

**The Azores.**

- **São Miguel** is the best first visit. It has the most flights, crater lakes at Sete Cidades and Lagoa do Fogo, and Furnas with its hot springs. Ponta Delgada makes a practical base.
- **Pico and Faial** work as a pair, linked by the short ferry. Stay in Horta or Madalena. Climb the mountain or walk Pico's lava-field coast, then look back across the channel from Faial.
- **Flores** is for travellers who want waterfalls and cliffs almost to themselves. Allow extra days, because bad weather can disrupt flights in and out.

## Before you book

- For Madeira, check the classified-trail status page and book trail slots on SIMplifica before you travel.
- For the Azores, check the Azores Trails alerts page the night before each walk.
- Build spare days into any Azores itinerary that includes flights or ferries between islands.
- Confirm current Atlânticoline sailings and Azores Airlines schedules for your exact dates.
- Pack waterproofs and warm layers for both archipelagos, whatever the season.
- If you plan to climb Pico, allow a flexible weather window and register at the Casa da Montanha.
- Book whale watching with a licensed operator and accept that sea conditions have the final say.
