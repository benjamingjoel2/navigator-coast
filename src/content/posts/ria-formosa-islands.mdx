The boat leaves Olhão's waterfront with a low grumble, threads between moored fishing boats and then the town simply falls away. Within minutes you are out in a maze of channels, sandbanks and salt marsh, the water shading from green to pale gold where it runs shallow over the sand. Herons stand in the mud like fence posts. Ahead, a thin line of dunes marks the edge of the lagoon, and beyond it, the open Atlantic.

<Figure id="rf-lagoon-boat" size="full" caption="A fishing boat at anchor on still water in the lagoon near Olhão." />

These are the Ria Formosa islands, a string of sandy barrier islands strung along the coast between Faro and Tavira. Culatra, Armona and Deserta are the three most people visit, and each has a different personality: a working fishing island, a low-key holiday island, and one with almost nothing on it at all. You can see one in a morning, but they are at their best when you give them a whole day and let the tides set the pace.

## What the Ria Formosa is

The lagoon is protected as the Parque Natural da Ria Formosa. The park's own visitor guide, published by the nature authority ICNF with Turismo do Algarve, describes it as [stretching for 60 km along the Algarve coast](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467), from the Ancão peninsula to Manta Rota, and covering around 18,400 hectares across the municipalities of Loulé, Faro, Olhão, Tavira and Vila Real de Santo António. Most of that is the lagoon system itself: a chain of islands and sandy peninsulas running roughly parallel to the shore and holding back a sheltered world of salt marsh, mudflats, islets and salt pans.

It is a busy world, too. The same guide notes that more than 20,000 waterbirds gather here in winter, many of them migrants from northern Europe, and that the lagoon is a breeding ground for species such as the little egret, Audouin's gull, the Kentish plover and the little tern. It is also seahorse country. Two species live in the Ria Formosa, and the ICNF says it once held the largest seahorse community in the world; numbers have fallen sharply over the past two decades, and two refuge areas were created in 2020 to try to stop the decline.

If you are coming from Lisbon or Porto, the eastern Algarve is easy to reach by rail. CP's [Algarve line](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line) runs between Lagos and Vila Real de Santo António, and Faro and Olhão both sit on it. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers the trains, coaches and driving in more detail.

## Getting to the islands

There are no bridges to Culatra, Armona or Deserta. You arrive by boat, and that is half the point.

From Olhão, regular passenger boats run to three stops on the islands. According to a 2021 opinion from Portugal's transport regulator, the AMT, [three year-round lines](https://www.amt-autoridade.pt/media/4564/parecer-71_ds_dapp_22jul_docapesca.pdf) connect Olhão with Armona, Culatra and Farol, with the Farol boat calling at Culatra on the way. The regulator gave the crossing to Armona as roughly 25 minutes, to Culatra about 35 minutes, and to Farol about 45 minutes. Timetables change through the year, with more sailings in summer, so check the times posted at the Olhão pier on the day and, above all, note the last boat back.

Deserta is reached from Faro instead. The ICNF guide notes that its walking trail starts at the island's jetty, the Pontão da Ilha Deserta, and that the boat leaves from Faro. Several private boat services run from the city, and many combine Deserta with a loop through the channels.

## Culatra: a fishing island

Culatra is the most lived-in of the three. Its main village is a working fishing community, with nets drying in the sun, boats hauled up on the sand and low houses facing the lagoon. There are no roads to speak of: just sand lanes, a few concrete paths and boardwalks across the dunes to the ocean beach on the far side.

At the western end is Farol, the second boat stop, named for the lighthouse that stands over a cluster of holiday houses. From either village a short walk brings you to the seaward beach, a long, open strand backed by dunes. It feels far wilder than the crossing time suggests.

This is the island to come to if you want a sense of the lagoon as a place where people still make a living. The Algarve's tourism board, in its [guide to the region's cooking](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf), singles out the Ria Formosa as one of the most prized sources of the coast's clams, cockles, razor clams and oysters. Lunch here, in season, is likely to involve shellfish. Our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) has more on the Algarve's dishes, including the cataplana.

## Armona: the easy island

Armona is the closest island to Olhão, which makes it the simplest to visit on a whim. The boat drops you on the lagoon side, where a cluster of small houses and cafés gives way to a sandy path running across the island to the ocean beach. On the lagoon side the water is calm and shallow, a good option for paddling when the Atlantic side is lively.

It is a relaxed, unshowy place, popular with Portuguese families in summer and almost hushed out of season. If you only have half a day, Armona is the one.

## Deserta: almost nothing at all

Deserta, also called Ilha da Barreta, lives up to its name. The ICNF guide notes that it holds the southernmost point of mainland Portugal, Cabo de Santa Maria, and that the only buildings are fishing shelters and a beach support. The rest is sand, dune and sky.

The island has a short, marked trail. ICNF gives it as a [3 km circular route](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467) taking about two hours, graded easy, mostly on a raised boardwalk built to keep feet off the fragile dune plants. In spring and summer look out for the sea daffodil, which the guide describes as having showy white flowers, and along the shore for gulls, terns and wintering waders feeding on the mud at low tide.

<Pair a="rf-barrier-beach" b="rf-deserta-boardwalk" captionA="The barrier islands are long, thin ribbons of sand between lagoon and ocean." captionB="On Deserta, a boardwalk keeps walkers off the fragile dune plants." />

Bring everything you need. There is little shade, and the walk out to Cabo de Santa Maria is exposed to sun and wind.

## The lagoon side: birds, salt and tides

The islands are only half the story. On the mainland, the park's interpretation centre at Marim, just east of Olhão, has a [visitor trail](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467) through salt marsh, dunes, shellfish beds, pine woods, Roman ruins and a tide mill. The ICNF guide adds that the chameleon, which in Portugal lives only on the Algarve coast, can be found there. Closer to Faro, the Ludo trails cross salt pans where, the guide says, flamingos can be seen at certain times of year.

Everywhere, the tide matters. Twice a day the water floods across the flats and drains away again, changing the shape of the channels, exposing sandbanks and drawing birds down to feed. It is worth watching from one spot for an hour: the lagoon at low water and high water can look like two different places.

Winter is the best season for birds, and our [guide to the Algarve in winter](/stories/algarve-in-winter/) covers the quieter months in more depth. For beaches, the bathing season with lifeguards in the Algarve opened on [15 May in 2026](https://portugal.gov.pt/gc25/comunicacao/noticias/epoca-balnear-arranca-em-cascais-e-madeira-sob-maior-exigencia-na-protecao-do-litoral); outside it, most island beaches are unguarded.

## Visiting lightly

This is a protected, fragile landscape, and the islands' dunes are what hold them together. The ICNF's code of conduct is short: stay on the existing trails, do not pick plants, and leave nothing but footprints. Walk on boardwalks where they exist, keep away from nesting birds, and take your rubbish back to the mainland with you.

On the ocean beaches, the usual Atlantic rules apply. The UK government's [safety advice for Portugal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) warns that some beaches have a strong undertow and rip currents, that supervised beaches use a flag system (a red flag means you must not enter the water), and that you should take extra care where there are no lifeguards or flags. Sun on the sand is fierce even in spring; check [IPMA's UV index forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev.uv/) before you set out.

## Where to base yourself

Olhão is the natural base for Armona and Culatra, Faro for Deserta. Tavira, further east, is an elegant alternative with its own island, Ilha de Tavira, and the Barril trail; our [Tavira guide](/stories/tavira-guide/) covers the town. In the centre of Tavira, the [Pousada Convento Tavira](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-tavira) occupies a former convent; its own description notes that some rooms were adapted from the convent's old cells and that an archaeological site with remains of an Almohad-era neighbourhood was uncovered when the building was converted. For more on the regional capital, see our guide to [Faro's old town and lagoon](/stories/faro-old-town/).

## Before you book

- Check the boat times at the Olhão pier, or with the operator in Faro for Deserta, on the day you travel, and plan around the last boat back.
- Go with the tide in mind: low water is best for birds on the flats, and the lagoon looks very different at high water.
- Pack water, food, sunscreen, a hat and something warm for the boat. Facilities on the islands are limited, especially out of season.
- Outside the bathing season, assume beaches are unguarded and swim only in calm conditions.
- Stay on trails and boardwalks, keep dogs under control near birds, and take all rubbish home.
- Travelling by train? Check the current Algarve line timetable on [CP's site](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line) before you go.
