Lisbon gives itself away slowly. On the first afternoon it is all noise and gradient: trams grinding uphill, tiles flashing in the sun, the river appearing and disappearing at the ends of streets. By the second evening, sitting on a viewpoint as the light goes gold over the Tagus, you start to feel how the city fits together. Two days is not long, but it is enough to see the essentials and to know exactly why you will come back.

This plan keeps the walking sensible, groups sights by neighbourhood, and leaves room for long lunches. Day one covers the historic centre and the hills; day two heads west along the river.

## Arriving and getting around

If you fly in, the Metro is the simplest way into town. Metro Lisboa's [history page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/company/know/brief-history/) records that the Red Line was extended to the airport in July 2012, and from there you can change for the rest of the network. The Metro [runs from 6:30 to 1:00](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/travel/timetables-and-frequency/) every day, including weekends and public holidays.

For fares, Metro Lisboa's [ticket page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/) explains the options: a reusable Navegante occasional card that you load with single tickets or pay-as-you-go "zapping" credit, valid across the member operators, or a contactless bank card tapped at the gates, which costs a little more per ride. One card per person per journey either way.

If you plan to visit several museums and monuments, look at the [Lisboa Card](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisboa-card), which bundles free public transport with free entry to a long list of sights.

## Day one: Baixa, the castle and Alfama

### Morning in the Baixa

Start where the city meets the river, in the Baixa. Its straight, level streets run down to the great riverside square of Praça do Comércio, also called Terreiro do Paço. The [Pousada de Lisboa](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-lisboa), on the square itself, describes the building as more than two centuries old, raised after the 1755 earthquake and later home to government ministries. The square was the seat of royal and administrative power, and it still feels like it, with arcades on three sides and the Rua Augusta Arch leading back into town.

<Pair a="l48-rua-augusta-arch" b="l48-rooftops-se" captionA="The Rua Augusta Arch on Praça do Comércio, with a tram passing beneath the arcades." captionB="Over the Baixa rooftops to the twin towers of the Sé, Lisbon's cathedral." />

For breakfast, walk up Rua Augusta to Praça da Figueira. [Confeitaria Nacional](https://confeitarianacional.com/) has been there since 1829, and its own history says it introduced Bolo Rei, the Christmas king cake, to Portugal in 1875. It is a good place for your first pastel de nata of the trip.

### Up to the castle

From the Baixa, climb towards the [Castelo de São Jorge](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/) on its hilltop. The castle opens daily from 9:00, closing at 21:00 from March to October and at 18:00 from November to February, with last entry half an hour before closing; it is closed on 1 January, 1 May and 24, 25 and 31 December. Access to the walls and towers can end earlier than the grounds. Go in the morning, before the heat and the crowds, and walk the ramparts for the widest view in the city.

### Alfama in the afternoon

Below the castle, Alfama spills down towards the river. Visit Lisboa calls it [the oldest and most traditional neighbourhood](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/alfama) in Lisbon, and on foot it is a maze of steep, cobbled lanes, washing lines, small squares and staircases that seem to lead nowhere. The Miradouro de Santa Luzia and the neighbouring Portas do Sol viewpoint look out over the rooftops to the Tagus. Our deeper guide to [Alfama, Mouraria and Graça](/stories/lisbon-alfama-mouraria-graca/) has routes for getting gently lost.

At the bottom of the hill, the [Museu do Fado](https://www.museudofado.pt/en) on Largo do Chafariz de Dentro tells the story of Lisbon's own music, its singers and guitarists. It opens from Tuesday to Sunday, 10:00 to 18:00, with last entry at 17:30, and closes on Mondays. An hour there makes the evening that follows mean far more.

### Evening: fado

Alfama is the natural place to hear fado, and Visit Lisboa recommends its fado houses for the evening. Book ahead if you can, eat slowly, and expect silence when the singing starts.

## Day two morning: tram 28 and Chiado

If you would rather ride than climb, Carris [route 28E](https://www.carris.pt/viaje/carreiras/28e/) runs from Martim Moniz through Graça, past the Sé and the Portas do Sol viewpoint, down through the Baixa to Chiado, and on to Estrela and Campo de Ourique. It is the classic Lisbon tram ride, and it is busy. Board early, near the Martim Moniz end, and you have a better chance of a seat.

<Figure id="l48-bica-funicular" size="column" caption="The Bica funicular's steep, cobbled track between Rua de São Paulo and Largo do Calhariz, below Bairro Alto." />

A note on Lisbon's other famous climbers. At the time of writing Carris lists the [Bica funicular](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/funiculars/) as out of service and the [Santa Justa Lift](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/lift/) as temporarily closed, so check before planning a route around them.

Hop off at Chiado for coffee. [A Brasileira](https://ovalordotempo.pt/marcas/a-brasileira-do-chiado/), on Rua Garrett, opened in 1905, and the bronze statue of the poet Fernando Pessoa, a regular, sits on its terrace. From Chiado, Bairro Alto is a short walk uphill; by day it is quiet, with record shops and small studios, and by night Visit Lisboa describes it as the starting point for an evening that drifts downhill [towards Bica and Cais do Sodré](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/bairro-alto).

## Day two afternoon: Belém and the river

Head west to Belém by tram 15E or the Cascais-line train from Cais do Sodré.

The [Jerónimos Monastery and the Tower of Belém](https://mosteirojeronimos.torrebelem.gov.pt/en) have been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 1983. The monastery's cloister, carved with ropes, shells and foliage, is reason enough to come. Both sites are closed on Mondays, so plan your days around that, and buy tickets in advance in high season.

<Figure id="l48-cloister" caption="The two-storey cloister of the Jerónimos Monastery in Belém." />

Next door, [Pastéis de Belém](https://pasteisdebelem.pt/en/history/) has been baking its custard tarts since 1837. Its own history tells how, after the religious orders were dissolved in 1834, the monastery's recipe ended up being sold in a shop attached to a sugar refinery nearby, and the bakery has kept that recipe secret ever since.

<Figure id="l48-nata-coffee" size="column" caption="A pastel de nata dusted with cinnamon, with a coffee on the side: the essential Lisbon pause." />

For more time in the district, our [day in Belém](/stories/belem-in-a-day/) covers the Padrão dos Descobrimentos, the Coach Museum and more. Then walk back east along the river past [MAAT](https://www.maat.pt/en/plan-a-visit), which closes on Tuesdays, towards Alcântara, where LX Factory sits beneath the Ponte 25 de Abril. We cover that stretch in detail in our guide to [LX Factory and Lisbon's riverside west](/stories/lx-factory-and-alcantara/).

### Sunset and dinner

End the day back at Cais do Sodré. The [Time Out Market](https://www.timeout.pt/time-out-market-lisboa/pt), in the old Mercado da Ribeira on Avenida 24 de Julho, is a food hall with dozens of counters and bars, open daily from 10:00 to midnight, and an easy option if everyone wants something different. Or walk up to one of the viewpoints for the last of the light over the river.

## Where to stay for a short visit

With only two nights, stay central so you can walk home. The Baixa and Chiado are the most practical; Alfama has the atmosphere; Príncipe Real and Lapa are quieter. The Pousada de Lisboa sits right on Praça do Comércio, and [York House](https://www.yorkhouselisboa.com/), on Rua das Janelas Verdes near the National Museum of Ancient Art, occupies a former seventeenth-century Carmelite convent. Our guide to [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) compares neighbourhoods in more depth.

## If you have a third day

Use it for [Sintra](/stories/sintra-day-trip/), with its palaces and forested hills, an easy train ride from the city.

## Before you book

- Check opening days carefully. Jerónimos, the Tower of Belém and the Museu do Fado all close on Mondays, and MAAT closes on Tuesdays.
- Check the [castle's hours](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/) for your dates; they change between summer and winter.
- Decide between a Navegante card, contactless or a [Lisboa Card](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisboa-card) before you land.
- Check the Carris pages for the current status of the [funiculars](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/funiculars/) and the [Santa Justa Lift](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/lift/).
- Book a fado evening and tickets for Belém's monuments ahead in summer.
- Pack shoes with grip. Lisbon's polished stone pavements are slippery on the hills, especially after rain.
