Évora announces itself slowly. The plains roll in from every side, cork oaks and wheat and olive groves, and then the walls appear: a ring of old stone around a low hill of whitewashed houses, their window frames picked out in ochre and yellow. Inside, the lanes narrow and twist, the shadows cool, and every few corners open onto a square, a convent, a Roman column or a café table set out under arches.

It is a city made for walking and for staying the night. Day-trippers from Lisbon see the highlights; those who stay see Évora at dusk, when the stone glows and the squares fill with locals, and in the hush of early morning before the shutters go up.

## Why Évora matters

Évora's historic centre has been a [UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora), and Visit Alentejo puts it plainly: the [entire city is recognised](https://www.visitalentejo.pt/en/alentejo/culture/unesco-world-heritage/world-heritage-list/), with a history running back to Roman times. Its list of highlights reads like a primer in Portuguese history: the Roman temple, the cathedral, the Casas Pintadas (painted houses), the Cadaval Palace, the 16th-century university and Praça do Giraldo.

The city is also preparing for a big year. The regional tourism board notes that Évora was named [European Capital of Culture for 2027](https://visitalentejo.pt/en/blog/easy-day-trips-from-evora-european-capital-of-culture-for-2027), so expect more events, and more visitors, through 2027.

If you are fitting Évora into a longer trip, our [ten-day Portugal itinerary](/stories/ten-day-portugal-itinerary/) gives it a night between Lisbon and the Algarve.

## The acropolis: temple and cathedral

Start at the top of the hill. The granite columns of the Roman temple, often called the Temple of Diana, stand on their podium in a small square, with gardens behind and white walls around. It is one of those sights that looks almost too perfect in photographs and still stops you in your tracks in person.

<Figure id="evo-temple-acropolis" caption="The Roman temple on Évora's hilltop, with the Pousada dos Loios on the right and the plains beyond." />

A few steps away is the cathedral. According to the [cathedral chapter's own history](https://www.evoracathedral.com/index.php/historia/catedralse/), it was built between about 1280 and 1350 in a Romano-Gothic style, stands on the city's highest point beside the temple, and is described as the largest cathedral in Portugal. Its roof is ringed with battlements, in the manner of medieval fortress-churches.

Do not skip the climb. The [cathedral's site](https://www.evoracathedral.com/) calls the roof terrace the highest point in the city and a must-visit: from up there you look over the walled centre and the newer districts to the plain, with Arraiolos, Evoramonte and the Serra de Ossa on the horizon. The same site lists opening hours, ticket prices and Mass times, which are worth checking before you go.

Between the cathedral and the temple stands the [Pousada Convento Évora](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-evora), also known as the Pousada dos Loios. Pousadas de Portugal describes it as a former convent whose rooms are the old monks' cells, almost all different from one another, set around cloisters with an outdoor pool. It is the obvious choice if you want to wake up inside the monument zone.

## Praça do Giraldo and the lanes

From the acropolis, wander downhill to Praça do Giraldo, the city's main square. CP's [Évora guide](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora) describes its arcades, cafés and marble fountain, and this is where Évora gathers: for coffee in the morning, for an ice cream in the late afternoon, for an evening stroll once the heat has gone.

<Pair a="evo-rooftops-view" b="evo-white-lane" captionA="Terracotta roofs and white walls of Évora, with the plain beyond, seen through a gap in the old stonework." captionB="A whitewashed lane in the historic centre, with a stone tower rising behind." />

The best way to see the rest of Évora is to let the lanes take you. Look for the arched passages, the tiled saints over doorways, the wrought-iron balconies and the little squares where a single orange tree shades a bench. The old town is compact enough to explore entirely on foot, and slow enough that you will want to.

For museums, the city runs a joint pass. The [Évora Ticket](https://www.evoraticket.pt/), from the Rede de Museus de Évora, covers sites in the city's museum network; check the current list of included places and the opening hours before you plan your day.

## The Chapel of Bones

Évora's most famous sight is also its most unsettling. The Capela dos Ossos sits inside the Church of São Francisco, and the church's [own page on the chapel](https://igrejadesaofrancisco.pt/capela-dos-ossos/) explains its purpose: built in the 17th century to prompt reflection on the brevity of human life, its walls and pillars are covered with several thousand bones and skulls taken from the convent's burial grounds.

<Figure id="evo-chapel-of-bones" size="column" caption="Skulls and bones set into the walls of the Capela dos Ossos, beneath the painted vault." />

Look up, too. The vaulted ceiling carries frescoes dated 1810, with biblical scenes and the instruments of Christ's Passion, and on the wall opposite the exit is a tile panel by the architect Siza Vieira, contrasting death with the miracle of life. The church is a private religious body; its [hours and prices page](https://igrejadesaofrancisco.pt/igreja/horarios/) lists the days it closes and notes that state-museum discounts do not apply.

## Beyond the walls

Évora makes an excellent base for the central Alentejo. Outside the city, the Almendres Cromlech is, in the words of Visit Alentejo, a [Neolithic-era monument](https://visitalentejo.pt/en/blog/easy-day-trips-from-evora-european-capital-of-culture-for-2027) of many stone pillars standing upright in a circle, set in the countryside. Go early or late in the day, when the low sun throws long shadows from the stones.

<Figure id="evo-almendres-cromlech" caption="Standing stones of the Almendres Cromlech among oak trees outside Évora." />

The same Visit Alentejo article suggests other easy day trips: Arraiolos, known for its hand-stitched carpets, and Estremoz, which sits high above vineyards and olive groves with its fortifications and medieval castle. East of Évora, the hilltop village of Monsaraz and the great lake of Alqueva make a superb overnight extension; see our guide to [Monsaraz and Alqueva](/stories/monsaraz-and-alqueva/).

Wine is never far away. The Alentejo wine commission's page on [Cartuxa](https://vinhosdoalentejo.pt/en/wine-tourism/fundacao-eugenio-de-almeida-adega-cartuxa) explains that the Fundação Eugénio de Almeida's winery, the Adega da Cartuxa at Quinta de Valbom, lies about 2 km from the historic centre near the Cartuxa Convent, in a former Jesuit house, and that wine has been made there since the late 19th century. Guided tours finish with a tasting of wines and olive oils; book through [Cartuxa's own site](https://www.cartuxa.pt/).

## What to eat

The Alentejo kitchen is generous, rustic and built around pork, bread, olive oil and herbs. CP's [Évora page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora) lists the classics you will meet on menus here: grilled black pig, pork with clams, migas, tomato soup, dogfish soup and açorda, the bread-based dish fragrant with coriander and garlic. For dessert, look for sericaia with plums.

Lunch is the main event in the Alentejo, long and unhurried, and many kitchens close in the afternoon. For more on regional dishes and how to order them, see our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

## When to go

Évora is an inland city, and summer is fierce. IPMA's [1991–2020 climate normals for Évora](https://www.ipma.pt/opencms/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_EVORA.pdf) put average highs above 33°C in July and August, with most days in those months topping 30°C. Winter days are mild, with January highs averaging about 14°C, but nights can be cold.

Spring and autumn are the sweet spots, when the plains are green or golden and walking the lanes at midday is still a pleasure. Our [guide to the best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) goes month by month.

## Getting there

The train is the easy option from Lisbon. CP's [Évora page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora) notes that the station is close to the centre and walkable, and that tickets can be bought online up to 60 days ahead, through the CP app or at stations. Our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers the Intercidades services, coaches and the case for hiring a car.

A car is not needed in Évora itself, where everything is within walking distance. Hire one when you leave, if you plan to explore the cromlech, the wineries and the villages further east.

## Before you book

- Check the current train times and fares on [CP's site](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora), and book ahead for weekends.
- Stay at least one night; the city is at its best after the day visitors leave.
- Check opening hours and closing days for the [cathedral](https://www.evoracathedral.com/) and the [Church of São Francisco](https://igrejadesaofrancisco.pt/igreja/horarios/) before you plan your day.
- Avoid sightseeing in the midday heat in July and August, as [IPMA's climate data](https://www.ipma.pt/opencms/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_EVORA.pdf) suggests; start early and rest after lunch.
- If you want a winery visit, book it in advance and decide who is driving before the first glass.
