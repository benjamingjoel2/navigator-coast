The road gives out first, then the tarmac, and the last kilometre is a dirt track between olive trees. At the end there is a whitewashed farmhouse with a band of blue paint around the doors, a fig tree in the yard and nothing else in sight but fields running to the horizon. In the evening the only sounds are sheep bells and the cicadas. Breakfast, the next morning, is likely to include something made on the estate.

That is the promise of staying on a quinta, and in Portugal it is a promise often kept. Across the country, farms, wine estates, manor houses and whole villages have been restored to take guests. Some are simple and family-run; some are among the most polished places to stay in the country. This guide explains what the different names mean, and where to look in three of the best regions for it.

## Quinta, herdade, monte, solar: a short glossary

The words describe places more than types of accommodation.

- **Quinta** is a farm or country estate. In the Douro it usually means a wine estate; around Lisbon and in the north it can be a manor with gardens.
- **Herdade** is the Alentejo's word for a large rural estate, often of cork oaks, olives, cereals and vines.
- **Monte** is the farmhouse at the heart of an Alentejo estate, typically low, whitewashed and set on a rise.
- **Solar** or **paço** is a manor house, often with a family coat of arms over the door.
- **Casa de campo** simply means a country house.

## The official categories

Behind the romantic names sit some precise legal categories, and they are worth knowing because they tell you what kind of stay to expect. Turismo de Portugal's [overview of tourist accommodation types](https://business.turismodeportugal.pt/pt/Planear_Iniciar/Como_comecar/Empreendimento_Turisticos/Paginas/default.aspx) defines two that matter most here.

**Turismo no espaço rural (TER), or rural tourism.** These are establishments in rural areas that provide accommodation while preserving, restoring and valuing the architectural, historic, natural and landscape heritage. They are classified in three groups: *casas de campo* (country houses), *agroturismo* (stays on working farms) and *hotéis rurais* (rural hotels).

**Turismo de habitação.** These are family-run establishments in old private houses which, for their architectural, historic or artistic value, represent a particular period, such as palaces and manor houses. They can be in the countryside or in towns.

The distinction is practical. A *turismo de habitação* stay usually means a handful of rooms in a historic family home, often with the owners on site. A *hotel rural* is closer to a small hotel. *Agroturismo* puts you on a farm that is still working. You can look up classified establishments in Turismo de Portugal's [register of tourist establishments](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_ET.aspx), and many smaller farm cottages are registered instead as local accommodation, which has its [own register](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AL.aspx).

One long-running network is [Solares de Portugal](https://www.solaresdeportugal.pt/en), the booking site linked to TURIHAB, the turismo de habitação association based in Ponte de Lima. It groups its houses into *casas antigas* (historic houses), *quintas e herdades* (farms and estates), *casas rústicas* (rustic houses) and *casas no campo* (country houses).

## The Alentejo: herdades and montes

The Alentejo is the classic place for a farm stay. It is a land of wide horizons, cork oak and olive groves, white villages on hilltops and long, hot summers, and its estates are big enough that you may not see another guest all day.

<Pair a="qta-cork-oak" b="qta-alentejo-village" captionA="A cork oak in the Alentejo plain, its trunk stripped of bark." captionB="Whitewashed walls and a tiled roof in Monsaraz." />

Évora makes an obvious anchor. CP's page on [the Alentejo and Évora](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora) notes that the city has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986 and that the station is close to the centre. Many estates lie within an hour's drive, so you can mix a night or two in the city with a longer stay in the countryside.

**Where to stay.** Near the hilltop village of Monsaraz, [São Lourenço do Barrocal](https://www.barrocal.pt/) calls itself a hotel and *monte alentejano*: a farm estate with its own vineyards, cellar and olive-dotted fields, whose restaurant uses produce from the land, and with views across to Monsaraz. Further south, in Albernoa near Beja, [Herdade da Malhadinha Nova](https://www.malhadinhanova.pt/) is a 744-hectare estate with 80 hectares of vineyard on schist slopes. Its rooms are spread between a country house, Monte da Peceguina, and several separate houses on the estate.

## The Douro: wine quintas

In the Douro Valley, a quinta almost always means a wine estate, and staying on one is the best way to see the valley at its quietest. You wake among the vines, often with the river below, and tastings are a short walk away. If you are planning a trip, our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) itinerary covers the train, the villages and tasting safely.

**Where to stay.** [Quinta do Vallado](https://www.quintadovallado.com/en/) says it was built in 1716 and is one of the oldest quintas in the valley; it runs a wine hotel at the estate, as well as a second property in the Douro Superior. Near Pinhão, [Quinta de la Rosa](https://www.quintadelarosa.com/) describes itself as a family-owned winery and offers rooms, some with river views, alongside its restaurant. [Quinta Nova de Nossa Senhora do Carmo](https://www.quintanova.com/), a family wine estate with a long history of making port, began producing Douro table wines in 2005 and has a Relais &amp; Châteaux winery house with rooms.

<Pair a="qta-douro-riverside" b="qta-schist-village" captionA="A white quinta house on the bank of the Douro, below terraced vineyards." captionB="Piódão, a village of schist houses in the mountains of central Portugal." />

Harvest, from late summer into early autumn, is the most atmospheric time to stay, and the busiest. Book early, and expect some estates to limit visits while they are picking.

## Central Portugal: manor houses and mountain villages

The Centro is less well known for farm stays, and that is part of its appeal. Here the options range from grand manor houses in wine country to restored stone villages in the mountains.

**Where to stay.** In Penalva do Castelo, 25 km from Viseu, [Casa da Ínsua](https://www.casadainsua.pt/) is a baroque mansion, known for its gardens, that has been converted into a five-star hotel. The estate also produces Dão wines, apples (grown there since the 18th century, according to its website) and DOP Serra da Estrela cheese from its own flock of sheep. Further east, in the historic village of Marialva, [Casas do Côro](https://www.casasdocoro.pt/) is a country hotel made up of restored houses in the traditional Beira style, with a restaurant serving wines from its own vineyards.

In the mountains of central Portugal, the [Aldeias do Xisto](https://www.aldeiasdoxisto.com/en) (Schist Villages) network brings together 28 villages, grouped around the Lousã and Açor mountains, the Zêzere and the Tejo-Ocreza area. Many of their stone houses have been restored as guesthouses and holiday cottages, which the network lists on its own booking platform. It is excellent walking country and a world away from the coast.

## What to expect

A rural stay is a different rhythm from a city hotel, and it helps to plan for it.

- **You will almost certainly need a car.** Few estates are near a station or a regular bus. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire and tolls.
- **Meals vary.** Some houses serve dinner on request; others expect you to drive to the nearest village. Ask before you book, especially for your arrival night.
- **Seasons matter.** Inland Alentejo summers are very hot, and Centro mountain winters are cold. Spring and autumn are lovely in both. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) has the detail.
- **Food is part of the point.** Expect regional cooking and the estate's own wine, oil or cheese. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal, region by region](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) will help you know what to order.

## Before you book

- Check whether the property is a hotel-style stay or a few rooms in a family home, and what that means for meals, check-in times and service.
- Look up its classification or registration in Turismo de Portugal's [register of tourist establishments](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_ET.aspx) or [local accommodation register](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AL.aspx).
- Ask for directions in advance: satellite navigation often struggles with farm tracks.
- Confirm whether dinner is available, and where the nearest restaurant and shop are.
- In the Douro, book well ahead for the September and early October harvest.
- Plan for a car, and decide who is driving before any tasting.
