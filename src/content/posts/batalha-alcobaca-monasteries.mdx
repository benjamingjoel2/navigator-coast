Two monasteries, a short drive apart, built by two different religious orders for two very different reasons. One is a thank-offering for a battle, all lace-like pinnacles and flying buttresses in honey-coloured limestone. The other is a Cistercian abbey of almost severe simplicity, its church a long, tall, white tunnel of stone. Seeing them on the same day is one of the best lessons in medieval architecture Europe has to offer.

<Figure id="batalha-exterior" size="full" caption="Pinnacles, buttresses and tall Gothic windows on the Monastery of Batalha." />

Both are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, both are in the Centro region north of Lisbon, and both are easy to combine. Here is how we would spend the day.

## Morning: Batalha

### A promise kept in stone

Batalha means "battle", and the monastery is a promise kept. On 14 August 1385, at the [Battle of Aljubarrota](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/batalha), Portugal defeated Castile, and King João I had vowed to build a great monastery if he won. The monastery's own [history page](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/historia) records that construction began under royal commission in 1387, two years after the battle, and that the king gave it to the Dominican order in 1388.

The work went on for well over a century, under a succession of master builders the monastery names one by one: Afonso Domingues, who planned the church; Huguet, who designed the Founder's Chapel and the great west portal; and later Mateus Fernandes and João de Castilho, who brought the exuberant Manueline style of King Manuel I's reign.

### What to see

**The church** is the first thing you enter: tall, narrow and filled with coloured light from its stained glass.

**The Founder's Chapel**, just off the nave, was begun around 1415 and finished in 1435. The monastery says João I commissioned it [as his burial place](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/historia), and it holds the tombs of the king and his English queen, Philippa of Lancaster, and of their sons. Its spire was destroyed in the earthquake of 1755.

**The Chapter House** is roofed by a vault that the monastery credits to Huguet. Since 1921 it has held the monastery's memorial to the Unknown Soldier, with the remains of two Portuguese soldiers who fought in the First World War.

**The Unfinished Chapels** are the most haunting part of the visit. Begun in 1437 as a second royal mausoleum, commissioned by King Duarte, they were [never completed](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/historia): you walk into an octagon of richly carved chapels and pillars that stop abruptly, open to the sky.

<Figure id="batalha-unfinished-chapels" caption="The Unfinished Chapels at Batalha, never roofed and still open to the sky." />

The monastery was declared a National Monument in 1907 and inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1983.

### Practical details

The [visit page](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/visita) lists opening hours of 09:00 to 18:00 from 16 October to 31 March (last entry 17:30) and 09:00 to 18:30 from 1 April to 15 October (last entry 18:00). It closes on 1 January, Easter Sunday, 1 May and 25 December. Tickets can be bought online or at the ticket office, which is at the back of the monument. The page also notes changes to access and the visit route after Storm Kristin, so check it before you go.

### The battlefield

If you have a car and an extra hour, the battle itself is a short drive away. The [Battle of Aljubarrota Interpretation Centre](https://fundacao-aljubarrota.pt/interpretation-center/), run by the Fundação Batalha de Aljubarrota, presents the battle on the São Jorge battlefield, where you can also see the Chapel of São Jorge that the commander Nuno Álvares Pereira commissioned in 1393. The foundation says it works with the monastery to offer visitors an integrated experience.

## Lunch: on to Alcobaça

Alcobaça is a short drive south-west of Batalha. Arrive in time for lunch in the town, which spreads out around the vast square in front of the monastery.

### Convent sweets

Alcobaça is serious about pastry. Turismo Centro de Portugal calls the convent sweets [absolutely mandatory](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/alcobaca), singling out the cornucópias, cone-shaped pastries filled with soft egg yolk and sugar. [Pastelaria Alcôa](https://www.pastelaria-alcoa.com/), on Praça 25 de Abril facing the monastery, says it has been making traditional convent sweets in copper vessels since 1957, following the tradition of the region's Cistercian monks.

<Pair a="alcobaca-facade" b="alcobaca-cloister" captionA="The ornate front of the Monastery of Alcobaça, facing the town square." captionB="The cloister at Alcobaça, one of the monastery's medieval spaces." />

The region is also one of Portugal's main fruit growers, and Turismo Centro de Portugal singles out [the Alcobaça apple](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/alcobaca). For more on regional food, see our guide to [what to eat in Portugal, region by region](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

## Afternoon: Alcobaça

### A Cistercian giant

Alcobaça is older than Batalha by more than two centuries. The monastery's [UNESCO page](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/unesco) explains that it was founded in 1153 by Afonso Henriques, the first king of Portugal, through a donation to Bernard of Clairvaux, and describes it as one of the largest and best-preserved Cistercian monasteries in Europe. It was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1989.

The ornate front you see from the square gives little hint of what lies behind it. Step inside and the medieval abbey takes over.

### What to see

**The church** is the great surprise. Its three naves were built at almost the same height, which the monastery describes as [unprecedented at the time](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/unesco). The result is a long, tall, bright space, very plain, that pulls your eye straight down to the altar.

**The tombs of Pedro and Inês** face each other across the transept. The story behind them is Portugal's great tragic romance: Pedro, heir to the throne, loved Inês de Castro, who was murdered on his father's orders. The monastery calls the tombs [examples of the finest Gothic funerary sculpture](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/unesco), and Turismo Centro de Portugal notes that Inês's tomb carries a Last Judgement and Pedro's a Wheel of Life.

<Figure id="alcobaca-tomb" caption="A carved crouching figure supporting the tomb of Inês de Castro, beneath scenes in relief." />

**The monastic buildings** are what make Alcobaça exceptional. The UNESCO page lists the medieval spaces that survive: the cloister, the lavabo, the sacristy, the chapter house, the parlour, the monks' room, the refectory and the dormitory.

**The kitchen** is the one everyone remembers. Turismo Centro de Portugal says it was [built between 1720 and 1752](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/monastery-of-alcobaca-unesco-world-heritage), with a 26-metre chimney carried on cast-iron columns, blue-and-white tiles and a channel of spring water running through the room.

<Figure id="alcobaca-windows" size="column" caption="Sunlight through tall arched windows in the monastery at Alcobaça." />

### Practical details

Turismo Centro de Portugal lists [opening hours of 09:00 to 18:00 from October to March](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/monastery-of-alcobaca-unesco-world-heritage), with last entry at 17:30, and 09:00 to 19:00 from April to September, with last entry at 18:30. It notes that the monastery closes on 1 January, Easter Sunday, 1 May, 20 August and 25 December. Tickets are sold through the Museus e Monumentos de Portugal [ticketing site](https://mmp.bymeoblueticket.pt/pt/event/14718/mosteiro-de-alcobaca). Check the current hours on the [monastery's website](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/) before you travel.

## Getting there and around

**By car**, the day is simple. Batalha's visit page gives the route from Lisbon as the A1 north towards Leiria, then west to Batalha, and notes a [free car park](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/visita) about 50 metres from the ticket office. Alcobaça is then a short drive away, and you can return to Lisbon in the evening.

**By bus**, the monastery's [visit page](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/visita) says Rede Expressos runs daily services from Lisbon's Sete Rios terminal, taking about two hours, and that Batalha's bus terminal is a five-minute walk from the ticket office. Doing both monasteries in a day by bus takes more planning, so check connections between Batalha and Alcobaça before you set out, or consider staying a night.

Our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire, tolls and long-distance buses in more detail.

## Where to stay

If you would rather not rush, spend the night in Alcobaça. **[Montebelo Mosteiro de Alcobaça Historic Hotel](https://montebelohotels.com/montebelo-mosteiro-de-alcobaca-historic-hotel/pt/home)** occupies the monastery's former Rachadouro Cloister, in a project by the architect Eduardo Souto de Moura, with an events hall in the old monastery library. Staying overnight also lets you add the Convent of Christ the next day; see our guide to [Tomar and the Convent of Christ](/stories/tomar-and-the-templars/).

## Making a longer trip of it

Batalha and Alcobaça fit neatly into a wider route through central Portugal, between Lisbon and Coimbra or Porto. If you are planning a first trip, our [first-timer's guide to Portugal](/stories/first-timers-guide-to-portugal/) will help you decide how much of the Centro to fit in.

## Before you book

- Check the opening hours of [Batalha](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/visita) and [Alcobaça](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/) for your dates, and note Alcobaça's closure on 20 August.
- Read Batalha's visit page for any route changes after Storm Kristin.
- If you are travelling by bus, confirm Rede Expressos times and plan the Batalha to Alcobaça leg in advance.
- Start early: Batalha first, Alcobaça after lunch, is the most comfortable order.
- Wear comfortable shoes; both monasteries involve a lot of standing and stone floors.
