The bica arrives in a thick white cup, you drink it standing at the counter in three sips, and then comes the small moment of truth: do you tap a card, or dig for coins? In Portugal the answer is usually "either", but not always. A city-centre supermarket will take any card going. A tiny tasca in a village, a market stall or a kiosk on the beach may prefer cash, and the man selling roast chestnuts on a Lisbon corner in November certainly will.

Money in Portugal is easy once you know the handful of things that are different. Here is what we tell friends before they go.

## The currency

Portugal uses the euro. The European Central Bank explains that two series of [euro banknotes](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/banknotes/html/index.en.html) are in circulation: the first with seven denominations from 5 to 500 euros, and the newer Europa series with six, because the ECB stopped issuing the 500 note. In practice you will mostly handle 5, 10, 20 and 50 euro notes. Large notes can be awkward to change in small cafés, so break them in supermarkets or bigger shops. The ECB's [security features page](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/banknotes/current/security/html/index.en.html) shows how to check a note by feel, look and tilt.

## Cards: widely taken, with exceptions

Debit and credit cards, contactless payments and phone wallets are part of daily life in Portugal. The Multibanco network, which runs the country's shared cash machines and card terminals, says its [shop terminals accept international cards](https://www.multibanco.pt/) as well as contactless and mobile wallets.

So in hotels, supermarkets, chain cafés, museums, most restaurants and shops in Lisbon, Porto and the resort towns, a card is fine. Where it can come unstuck:

- **Small, family-run places**, especially in the countryside and on the islands. Some do not take cards at all, and some prefer cash for small amounts.
- **Markets, kiosks and street sellers.**
- **Card types.** Visa and Mastercard are the safest bets. Some outlets do not take American Express or Diners Club.

Our habit is simple: assume a card will work in the city, and glance at the door or ask before ordering anywhere small. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) will take you to plenty of places where it is worth asking.

## Cash machines

Cash machines are easy to find in towns: look for the MB (Multibanco) logo. The network says it has [more than 12,000 ATMs](https://www.multibanco.pt/) across the mainland and the islands, open 24 hours.

Before you rely on them:

- **Ask your own bank** what it charges for withdrawals abroad, and whether it has a daily limit. Fees are set by your card issuer, not by Portugal.
- **Take out sensible amounts** rather than lots of small withdrawals, if your bank charges per transaction.
- **Use machines inside or attached to banks** during opening hours if you can. If a machine keeps your card, you want someone to talk to.
- **Know who to call.** Multibanco lists a 24-hour line for lost or stolen cards on its website; save your own bank's emergency number too.

How much cash to carry? Enough for a day or two of coffees, market snacks, the odd taxi and a cash-only lunch. In the Alentejo interior or a rural corner of the Azores, carry a bit more.

## Pay in euros, not your home currency

When a card terminal or cash machine offers to charge you in your home currency, it is offering a conversion at its own rate. We always choose **euros** and let our own card provider do the conversion. It is usually the better deal, and at least it is the one you can check in advance.

For EU-issued cards there are clear rules. The EU's [Your Europe guide to payments](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/financial-products-and-services/payments-transfers-cheques/index_en.htm) explains that euro card payments and withdrawals in other EU countries must cost the same as domestic ones, that traders cannot add a surcharge for using a particular consumer card (three-party schemes such as American Express and Diners Club, and business cards, are not covered), and that you must be told about conversion fees before you agree to a purchase. If your card comes from outside the EU, your bank's own terms apply, so read them before you go.

## MB WAY, and why locals pay by phone

You will see "MB WAY" signs on counters and in shop windows. It is a Portuguese payment app linked to [participating banks](https://www.mbway.pt/), used to pay in shops by QR code or NFC, send money to friends, split bills and even withdraw cash without a card. It is everywhere in Portugal, from market stalls to small cafés.

For most visitors it is not an option, because it runs through banks that take part in the service. Do not worry if a sign says MB WAY: those businesses usually take cards or cash as well. Ask.

## Paying for transport

Contactless cards have made getting around the cities easier.

- **Lisbon metro.** You can [tap a contactless bank card](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/) at the gates. Each card covers one person per journey, so everyone needs their own. The alternative is a reusable navegante occasional card, loaded with tickets or credit.
- **Lisbon buses and trams.** Carris [accepts bank cards and contactless at the validator on board](https://www.carris.pt/en/buy/occasional-journeys-2/), as well as cash; on-board tickets are valid only on the vehicle where you buy them.

Long-distance trains and coaches sell tickets online and in apps. ## Motorway tolls

If you drive, tolls are where a card can fail you. Canada's travel advice warns that [international credit or debit cards are not always accepted](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/portugal) at highway tolls. Some motorways also have electronic-only tolls, with no booths at all. According to [PT Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/en), foreign-registered vehicles on those roads must have an activated means of payment, such as a bank card linked to the number plate or a prepaid Tollcard, and there are small administrative fees.

If you hire a car in Portugal, ask the rental company how tolls are handled before you drive away: whether the car has a device fitted, and how and when you will be charged.

## Tipping

We treat tipping in Portugal as a courtesy rather than an obligation. Our own practice is to leave small change for coffee and drinks, and to round up or leave a modest tip in restaurants when the service has been good. Tips are easiest in cash: card terminals do not always offer the option.

## Shopping and VAT refunds

If you live outside the EU, you may be able to reclaim VAT on goods you take home. Ask in the shop whether it offers tax-free shopping and keep the paperwork. At Lisbon airport, the airport lists a [VAT refund service](https://www.aeroportolisboa.pt/en/service-categories/reembolso-do-iva) for departing passengers; check where to find it, and leave time for it on the day you fly.

## Bringing cash into Portugal

Most people will never come near the limit, but it is worth knowing. You must declare [cash of EUR 10,000 or more](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/carrying-cash/index_en.htm), or the equivalent in other currencies, when you enter or leave the EU. Our guide to [Portugal's entry requirements](/stories/portugal-entry-requirements/) covers the other border rules.

## Before you book

- Tell your bank you are travelling, and check its fees for card payments and cash withdrawals abroad.
- Take two cards from different providers, kept in different places, plus a little cash in euros for your first day.
- Choose euros, not your home currency, when a terminal or cash machine asks.
- Give each traveller their own contactless card if you plan to tap in on Lisbon's metro.
- If you are hiring a car, ask how tolls will be charged before you leave the rental desk.
- For rural trips and island walking days, carry more cash than you would in the cities.
