A January morning in Tavira. The river is pewter under a low sky, the fishing boats are tied up two deep, and the café by the old bridge has its doors open anyway, because by eleven the sun will be out and the terrace will be warm enough for a coffee in shirtsleeves. Then, around four, the clouds roll back in off the Atlantic and you are glad of the jumper you nearly left behind.

That, in a sentence, is the Algarve in winter: mild, changeable, quiet, and a very different place from the one on the summer postcards. For some travellers it is the best time of year to be here. For others it will be a disappointment. Here is how to tell which camp you are in.

## What the weather is really like

The best guide is not a brochure but the climate record. IPMA, Portugal's national weather service, publishes 30-year averages for its station at Faro airport, and the [1991–2020 Faro climate normal](https://www.ipma.pt/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_FARO.pdf) tells a fairly clear story:

| Month | Average high | Average low | Average rainfall | Days with 1 mm+ of rain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| November | 19.6°C | 11.9°C | 75.5 mm | 6.6 |
| December | 17.1°C | 9.7°C | 90.0 mm | 7.0 |
| January | 16.3°C | 8.3°C | 49.3 mm | 6.0 |
| February | 17.0°C | 8.8°C | 41.9 mm | 5.4 |

A few things stand out. Daytime highs in the mid to high teens are pleasant walking weather, but evenings are cool, and many older Algarve houses are built to stay cool rather than warm. Frost is essentially absent at Faro, with zero days below freezing on average. And December is the wettest month of the year on that record, wetter than November or January.

Rain here tends to arrive in bursts rather than as constant drizzle, so on most rainy days there are still long dry spells. But you should plan for at least a couple of washed-out days in a week-long trip and keep an indoor option in your back pocket. For context, July's average high at the same station is 29.4°C, which is exactly why winter is the season for walking.

We would not plan a winter trip around swimming. Some people do take a bracing dip on a bright afternoon, but we could not find an official sea-temperature average to quote, and the lifeguard season is a summer affair: the government's 2026 schedule had the [bathing season opening on 15 May in the Algarve](https://portugal.gov.pt/gc25/comunicacao/noticias/epoca-balnear-arranca-em-cascais-e-madeira-sob-maior-exigencia-na-protecao-do-litoral). In winter, assume no one is watching the water.

## Shorter days, slower rhythm

The other thing winter changes is the light. Around the solstice the days are noticeably short, and the sun sits low over the sea all afternoon, which is wonderful for photographs and less wonderful for long walks that start late. Plan the main outing of the day for the morning, carry a head torch if you are on a cliff path, and treat a lingering lunch as part of the itinerary rather than a delay.

## What's open and what isn't

Winter is low season, and it shows. Resort strips built mostly for summer visitors can feel shuttered, some beach restaurants close or cut their hours, and boat trips depend on both demand and sea conditions. If a particular trip, restaurant or attraction is the reason you are going, check that it is running on your dates before you book anything else.

The towns that people actually live in all year are a different matter. Markets, cafés, churches and museums keep going, and you get them without queues. This is the strongest argument for choosing your base carefully (more on that below).

## Walking: the real reason to come

If summer belongs to the beach, winter belongs to the trails.

### The Seven Hanging Valleys

Lagoa's municipality describes the [Percurso dos 7 Vales Suspensos](https://www.cm-lagoa.pt/conhecer/percursos/sete-vales-suspensos) as a 5.7 km nature trail linking Praia de Vale Centeanes with Praia da Marinha. It follows an almost continuous line of limestone cliffs, broken by little valleys whose streams end above sea level, which gives the walk its name. In summer the heat and the crowds can make it hard going; on a clear winter morning it is the Algarve coast at its most dramatic, with ochre rock, turquoise coves and gulls riding the updraft.

Treat the edges with respect. The UK government's [travel advice for Portugal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) warns that falling rocks are a hazard, particularly in the Algarve, and that the authorities can fine people who ignore cliff-erosion signs. After heavy rain, give overhangs and cliff tops an even wider berth.

### The Via Algarviana

For something longer and inland, the [Via Algarviana (GR13)](https://viaalgarviana.org/menu/65/gr13---via-algarviana) runs for about 300 km across the hills from Alcoutim on the Guadiana river to Cabo de São Vicente, divided into 14 sectors. You do not need to walk all of it. A single sector gives you cork oak, rockrose, whitewashed hill villages and a quiet you will not find on the coast. The trail organisation's site has stage details and planning information.

### The Ria Formosa

On the eastern side, the lagoons, salt pans and barrier islands of the Ria Formosa Natural Park are at their liveliest in winter. The park authority ICNF's [guide to the Ria Formosa](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467) notes that more than 20,000 waterbirds gather here in the wintering season, many of them migrants from northern Europe. The same guide lists short marked trails, including the Trilho de São Lourenço, the Ludo routes near Faro and the Barril trail on Ilha de Tavira. They are flat, easy and ideal for a half-day with binoculars.

## Almond blossom

Inland, late winter brings the almond trees into flower, white and pale pink across the hills of the barrocal and the serra. The most visible celebration is in the village of Alta Mora, in the Castro Marim hills, where the [Festival das Amendoeiras em Flor](https://cm-castromarim.pt/info/evento/festival-das-amendoeiras-em-flor-0) has been held in early February. The municipality describes it as a celebration of the traditions of the serra in the depths of winter. Blossom depends on the weather, so dates move from year to year. Check the municipality's agenda nearer the time.

## Carnival in Loulé

If you are here in February, try to catch Loulé's Carnival. The municipality's [events page](https://www.cm-loule.pt/pt/menu/556/grandes-eventos.aspx) calls it the oldest carnival parade in the country, with more than 100 years of history. There are floats, samba groups, and giant-headed and stilt-walking figures along Avenida José da Costa Mealha. Expect sharp political and social satire, plenty of noise and a crowd that is mostly local.

Carnival follows the church calendar. In 2027, Shrove Tuesday falls on 9 February, so the parade should fall around that weekend. Confirm the programme on the municipality's site before booking, because Carnival weekends fill Loulé's accommodation fast.

## Where to base yourself in winter

In low season, the most important choice is a town that stays alive. Getting around is easier now, too. CP says that since 19 July 2026 the [whole Algarve line has been electrified](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line), with direct trains between Lagos and Vila Real de Santo António and no change at Faro. The [current timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/comboios-regionais-vila-real-s-antonio-lagos-19-julho-2026) lists stops at Lagos, Silves, Loulé, Faro and Tavira, among others. Some stations sit outside the town centres, so check the distance before you count on walking from the train.

- **Tavira.** Our first pick for winter. It is a real town on a river, with churches, a castle, a daily rhythm that does not depend on tourism, and the Ria Formosa on its doorstep. From here, Ilha de Tavira and the Barril trail are an easy outing.
- **Lagos.** The liveliest year-round base in the west. It has a working old town and harbour, enough restaurants open in winter to keep you interested, and the cliffs of Ponta da Piedade a short walk away. It is also at the end of the railway line, which makes car-free trips east easy.
- **Faro.** Underrated and practical. The walled old town, the Ria Formosa boardwalks and boat links to the islands are all close at hand, and the airport is nearby. Faro is a good choice for a short break or for anyone who doesn't want to drive.
- **Silves.** Small, inland and atmospheric, under its red sandstone castle. It is well placed for almond-blossom drives and for joining sections of the Via Algarviana. You will want a car.
- **Loulé.** A market town with a strong local life, an obvious choice for Carnival, and a good gateway to the barrocal hills. Again, a car helps.

## Who it suits, and who it doesn't

**It suits** walkers, birdwatchers, photographers, people who want to see everyday Algarve life rather than resort life, remote workers escaping a northern winter, and anyone who would rather have a coastal path to themselves than a sunbed.

**It doesn't suit** anyone whose idea of an Algarve holiday is swimming and sunbathing, families who need guaranteed outdoor days, or travellers who want nightlife and a full choice of beach restaurants. If that is you, come between May and October.

## Before you book

- Look at IPMA's forecast a few days out and pack layers, a waterproof and proper walking shoes.
- Check the CP timetable for your exact dates, and whether your station is in town or a taxi ride away.
- Confirm that any boat trip, restaurant or attraction you care about is open in low season.
- For Carnival, confirm dates on the Loulé municipality's site and book accommodation early.
- For almond blossom, watch the Castro Marim agenda in January.
- Ask your accommodation about heating; it matters more than you might think.
- On cliff paths, keep back from the edge, obey closure signs and finish before dusk.
