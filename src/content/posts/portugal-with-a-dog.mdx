Early on a winter morning, a Portuguese beach belongs to the dog walkers. The tide is out, the sand is hard and cool, and the only sounds are the surf and the occasional bark as somebody's terrier takes on the Atlantic. Portugal is a country where dogs are part of daily life: trotting through cobbled lanes, dozing under café tables, riding along in the back seat on the way to the coast.

Bringing your own dog is very doable, but it takes planning. The paperwork must be in order before you travel, transport rules vary, beaches have their own regulations, and there are a few seasonal hazards that dog owners from northern Europe may not know about. Here is what to sort out.

## The paperwork: getting your dog into Portugal

Portugal follows the EU's rules on pet travel, set out on the EU's [Your Europe page](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/animal-plant/index_en.htm). The essentials:

- **Microchip first.** Your dog must be microchipped, and the chip must be in place before the rabies vaccination, or the vaccination does not count.
- **Rabies vaccination.** Dogs must be at least 12 weeks old for the first vaccination, and you must then wait at least **21 days** before travelling. Boosters must be given before the previous vaccination expires, or the wait applies again.
- **The right document.** Owners who live in the EU use an **EU pet passport**. Coming from outside the EU, your dog needs an **EU animal health certificate** issued by an official state vet, no more than 10 days before arrival. It is then valid for travel between EU countries for six months, or until the rabies vaccination expires, whichever comes first.
- **Tapeworm treatment** is only required for dogs travelling to Finland, Ireland, Malta, Norway or Northern Ireland, so it does not apply to a trip into Portugal.
- **Numbers.** You can travel with up to five pets, unless you meet the extra conditions for competitions and shows.

For travellers from Great Britain, the UK government's guide to [taking a pet to an EU country](https://www.gov.uk/taking-your-pet-abroad/travelling-to-an-eu-country-or-northern-ireland) confirms that you need an animal health certificate for each separate trip, and that you must enter the EU through a travellers' point of entry. Start the process with your vet well before you travel; the 21-day wait after a first rabies jab catches people out.

Bring the documents, the vaccination record and a recent photo of your dog in your hand luggage, and keep copies on your phone.

## Getting around

### By car

Driving is the simplest way to tour Portugal with a dog. You control the stops, the temperature and the timetable, and you can reach the quiet beaches and walking country that public transport does not. Our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers the roads and tolls.

### By train

CP, the national rail operator, does carry dogs, with conditions. Its [pet travel page](https://www.cp.pt/info/pt/w/transporte-animais) sets them out:

- Small pets can travel in a closed carrier that counts as hand luggage, one per passenger.
- A dog **without a carrier** must be muzzled, kept on a short lead and accompanied by an up-to-date vaccination record. It cannot occupy a seat.
- To buy a ticket for your dog you need a document showing its microchip code, and visitors from the EU should carry the pet passport.
- Fares depend on the service: CP lists full fare on Alfa Pendular and Intercidades, half fare on Regional and InterRegional trains, and free travel on the urban lines around Lisbon, Porto and Coimbra.
- CP does not carry animals classed as dangerous or potentially dangerous, and its page lists breeds including the American Staffordshire Terrier, Staffordshire Bull Terrier, Pit Bull Terrier and Rottweiler.

<Figure id="dogpt-douro-train" caption="A CP train below the vineyards near Régua. Dogs can ride the rails, muzzled and on a short lead." />

So a scenic day on the Douro line, as described in our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/), is possible with a well-behaved dog, provided you carry a muzzle and the paperwork. Check the current rules on CP's page before you travel, as they can change.

### City transport

In Lisbon, the metro's [customer rights and duties](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/?p=61064) ask riders to comply with the rules on transporting animals. In practice, expect to need a muzzle and a short lead on most public transport, and check each operator's rules before you set off. Taxis are often the easier option with a larger dog.

## Beaches and promenades

This is the question every dog owner asks first, and the honest answer is: it depends on the beach.

Beach rules in Portugal are set locally and can change with the bathing season. Cascais, for example, sets out its rules in a [municipal beach regulation](https://www.cascais.pt/sites/default/files/anexos/gerais/new/regulamento_revisao_final_ver20.052025.pdf), which allows registered dogs on its seafront promenades on a lead, and muzzled where required under national law. On the sand itself, read the signs at each beach entrance, look for the municipality's notices and, if in doubt, ask the lifeguard.

<Pair a="dogpt-lisbon-beach" b="dogpt-porto-de-mos" captionA="An off-season beach walk near Lisbon." captionB="Evening play on a quiet stretch of sand." />

A few pointers that make life easier:

- **Go out of season.** Spring, autumn and winter are the best times for a beach holiday with a dog. Beaches are emptier, the sand is cooler and you are less likely to find yourself on a busy, supervised beach where dogs are unwelcome.
- **Go early or late.** Even in summer, the first and last hours of daylight are the kindest to paws and to fellow beachgoers.
- **Pick wilder coasts.** The long beaches and dune systems of the Alentejo coast and the west of the Algarve are often quieter than the resort strips; our guide to [Algarve beaches](/stories/algarve-beaches/) will help you choose.
- **Clean up.** Always carry bags, and fresh water for your dog: salt water and hot sand are a poor combination.

## Walking with your dog

Portugal has superb walking, and much of it suits dogs. Coastal paths, pine woods and river valleys make for long, varied days out. The [Rota Vicentina](/stories/walking-the-rota-vicentina/) along the south-west coast and the trails of the [Peneda-Gerês National Park](/stories/peneda-geres-national-park/) in the north are two of the great walking areas; in protected areas, keep your dog on a lead and check local rules before you set out.

<Figure id="dogpt-geres-forest" size="column" caption="Pine woods in the Gerês. Beautiful walking, but watch the ground in late winter and spring." />

### The pine processionary caterpillar

This is the hazard every dog owner in Portugal should know about. The pine processionary moth lays its eggs in pine trees, and its caterpillars, covered in irritating hairs, live in silky nests at the ends of the branches.

According to an information sheet from the [Cascais municipality](https://www.cascais.pt/sites/default/files/anexos/gerais/processionaria-2674.pdf), the caterpillars come down from their nests and move in single file looking for somewhere to bury themselves between January and May, and their stinging hairs are a particular problem from the end of October to the end of spring. The same sheet lists allergic reactions in animals, including changes to the colour and shape of the tongue, and to the eyes.

Dogs are curious and sniff at anything moving on the ground, so treat any contact as an emergency. During those months:

- keep your dog on a lead in pine woods and parks;
- watch for lines of caterpillars on paths and for white, silky nests in the trees overhead;
- if your dog may have touched one, get to a vet at once.

<Figure id="dogpt-caparica-pines" caption="Stone pines near Costa da Caparica. Pine woods are lovely for walks; in late winter and spring, keep the lead on." />

### Heat, ticks and other pests

Summer heat is a real risk. Inland Alentejo and the Douro valley can be very hot, and even the coast can be scorching in the middle of the day. Check the [IPMA weather warnings](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) for heat alerts, walk early and late, never leave a dog in a parked car, and carry water everywhere.

Before you go, ask your vet about protection against ticks, mosquitoes and sandflies in southern Europe, and keep any treatment up to date during the trip. Note the details of a vet near where you are staying when you arrive.

## Where to stay and eat

Many holiday rentals, rural guesthouses and quintas accept dogs, but policies vary widely, so always confirm in writing before you book, and ask about any extra charge or size limit. A house with a garden or a farm stay in the countryside is usually more relaxing than a city flat.

Café and restaurant terraces are often happy to see a well-behaved dog lying under the table. Indoors is a different matter and is up to each owner: ask first.

## Before you book

- Start with your vet early: microchip, rabies vaccination and the 21-day wait, as set out by [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/carry/animal-plant/index_en.htm).
- From Great Britain, arrange the animal health certificate within 10 days of arrival and check the [GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/taking-your-pet-abroad/travelling-to-an-eu-country-or-northern-ireland).
- Confirm in writing that your accommodation takes dogs, including size and any fee.
- If you plan to use trains, pack a muzzle and short lead and check [CP's current rules](https://www.cp.pt/info/pt/w/transporte-animais), especially if your dog is a listed breed.
- If you are travelling between November and May, read up on processionary caterpillars and plan walks away from pine woods, or keep your dog close.
- Save the number of a local vet as soon as you arrive.
