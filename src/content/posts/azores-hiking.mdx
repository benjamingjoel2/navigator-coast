You set off from the trailhead in sunshine. Twenty minutes later you are walking through cloud so thick that the cows in the next field are only shapes, the hydrangeas drip, and the path ahead is a line of wet stones between two walls. Then the wind shifts, the cloud tears open, and a crater lake appears below you, impossibly green. That is hiking in the Azores in one morning.

The islands have some of the most rewarding walking in Europe: crater rims, laurel forest, old mule paths down to fajãs by the sea, and one serious mountain. They also have Atlantic weather, steep terrain and paths that landslides can close overnight. A little planning makes the difference between a great day and a miserable one.

## The official trail network

The place to start is [Azores Trails](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/trails-azores), the official site run by the regional tourism body. It describes a network of walks classified by the Regional Government of the Azores, built largely on the old footpaths islanders once used to move between villages, take cattle to pasture and carry produce to market. You can browse trails by island, difficulty, length and shape (circular or linear), and each route has a description, a map and downloadable files.

<Figure id="azhk-forest-path" caption="A railed path through dense green woodland, typical of the wetter inland trails." />

The routes come in two broad types. Short routes, coded PR, are the bulk of the network. Grand routes (GR) cross whole islands: the [Grand Routes page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/grand-routes) lists, for example, the 52.9 km western Grande Rota de São Jorge, the 78.6 km circuit of Santa Maria and the 36.8 km Faial Costa a Costa. Most visitors pick a handful of short routes and string them together over a trip.

On the ground, trails are waymarked with painted stripes and, according to the [ethics and safety page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/ethics-safety), numbered marker posts every 500 metres. Note the number of the last post you passed: if you need help, it tells rescuers where to find you.

## Check the alerts, every time

This is the most important habit to build. The official [alerts page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/alerts) lists trails that are temporarily closed. When we checked in November 2026, it included routes on Santa Maria, São Miguel, São Jorge and Flores, among them São Jorge's Fajã d'Além and Norte Pequeno walks and the Ponta Delgada – Fajã Grande coastal path on Flores. Closures follow storms, rockfalls and maintenance, and they can appear quickly.

Look at the page the night before and again in the morning. A closed trail is closed for a reason; coastal paths on steep cliffs are exactly where slips and rockfalls happen.

São Jorge is the walker's island, a long, narrow ridge with fajãs, the flat strips of land at the foot of its cliffs, scattered along the coast. Flores is all water and cliff; our guide to [Flores and Corvo](/stories/flores-and-corvo/) covers its waterfall walks and Corvo's crater. On Terceira, the Lagoa do Cerro route climbs to a viewpoint at about 650 metres, and paths on Monte Brasil above Angra lead to viewpoints over the town; see our [Terceira guide](/stories/terceira-guide/).

São Miguel, the largest island, has the famous crater-lake landscapes around Sete Cidades and Lagoa do Fogo. Its trails are popular, and several were on the closed list when we checked, so look at the alerts before you plan around them.

## Climbing Pico

Pico mountain is the big one. The Azores Trails [Pico page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/islands/pico) gives its maximum altitude as 2,350 metres, and it is a serious mountain day, not a walk.

Every ascent starts at the [Casa da Montanha](https://siaram.azores.gov.pt/centros-interpretacao/casa-da-montanha/Casa-da-Montanha.pdf), at about 1,200 metres, where the road ends. The regional environment service describes it as a compulsory stop whose main job is to register and control ascents: you request authorisation there under the rules in force. Check the current arrangements for authorisation before you travel, especially in summer.

The Pico Natural Park's [code of conduct](https://parquesnaturais.azores.gov.pt/media/multimedia/3/normas-de-conduta-na-montanha-do-pico.pdf) sets out the essentials:

- Carry at least 1.5 litres of water and high-energy food.
- Bring warm layers, a waterproof, a hat, gloves and mountain boots.
- Stay on the marked trail, and turn back if the weather changes.
- Keep the GPS locator you are given switched on, dry and easy to reach.
- Camping is forbidden, except for overnight stays in the crater.

The same document says access can be refused for safety reasons following a civil protection warning, to anyone without suitable equipment, and to anyone carrying a babe in arms or bringing a pet. It also warns that winter brings great climatic instability on the mountain. Take that seriously: cloud, wind and cold can arrive fast, and the descent on loose volcanic ground is where many people struggle.

## Reading the weather

The Azores climate is mild. Azores Trails gives Corvo's averages, for instance, as about 14°C in winter and 20°C in summer. Mild does not mean settled. The islands are known for weather that changes by the hour, and conditions on the high ground can be completely different from the coast. Our guide to [the best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons more broadly.

Two official tools help. IPMA, Portugal's weather service, publishes a [10-day forecast by location](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev.localidade.hora/) that includes Azorean towns, and a [weather warnings page](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) that covers the islands. Check both the evening before and the morning of a walk. If a warning is out for wind or rain, pick a low, sheltered route or have a rest day.

In practice: start early, keep a second-choice walk in mind for each day, and be willing to swap islands' plans around. A crater lake hidden in cloud is not worth the drive; come back tomorrow.

## Safety and trail manners

The official [ethics and safety page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/ethics-safety) is worth reading in full. The key points:

- Study the route, its length and difficulty, and check it suits you.
- Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.
- Wear layers and proper walking shoes or boots; use sunscreen even on cloudy days.
- Carry enough drinking water. Water in streams and lakes is not safe to drink.
- Take a charged phone, but expect no signal on many trails except at the start and end. The emergency number is 112.
- Check your footwear for seeds, so you don't spread invasive plants.
- Don't take shortcuts, remove rocks or plants, or disturb cattle. Leave gates as you found them.
- At trailheads, lock your car, hide valuables and don't block gates or emergency access.

## Getting between trailheads

Many trails are linear, so think about the return before you start. A hire car is the practical way to reach most trailheads; for linear walks, a taxi back to the start is often the simplest solution. For island hopping, our overview of [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) and our comparison of [Azores or Madeira](/stories/azores-or-madeira/) cover flights and ferries.

## Before you book

- Check the [Azores Trails alerts page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/alerts) the night before and the morning of every walk.
- If you plan to climb Pico, read the [code of conduct](https://parquesnaturais.azores.gov.pt/media/multimedia/3/normas-de-conduta-na-montanha-do-pico.pdf) and check how to register your ascent with the Casa da Montanha before you go.
- Pack waterproofs, warm layers and proper footwear, whatever the month.
- Build flexible days into your itinerary so you can walk when the weather allows.
- Save 112 in your phone and note marker-post numbers as you go.
