The waiter is delighted to help. Of course there is something without meat, he says, and returns with a plate of grilled sea bass. It happens less than it used to, but it still happens, and it sums up the gap a vegetarian traveller has to cross in Portugal. This is a country whose cooking is built on the pig and the sea, where "no meat" has often meant "fish, then", and where a bowl of vegetable soup can carry a quiet slice of chouriço.

And yet it has never been easier. Lisbon and Porto both have long-established vegetarian restaurants, the markets are full of extraordinary produce, and the traditional table has more meat-free food on it than you might expect, once you know what to ask. Here is how we approach it.

## What you are up against

Portuguese cooking is generous, regional and proudly carnivorous. Our [region-by-region guide to Portuguese food](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) will give you a sense of how much of it revolves around pork, salt cod and shellfish, and those flavours do not always announce themselves. The usual hiding places:

- **Soups.** The daily soup is one of the great pleasures of eating here, and many are vegetable purées. But some are made with meat stock, and caldo verde, the kale and potato soup, is often finished with a slice of chouriço.
- **Beans and greens.** Broad beans, chickpeas and greens are frequently cooked with sausage, bacon or pork fat.
- **Rice.** Tomato rice and other *arroz* dishes may be made with fish or meat stock, and are often served as the side to fried fish.
- **Bread dishes.** Açorda and migas can be built on garlic and olive oil, but often include fish, meat or egg.
- **Sweets.** Many traditional pastries rely on egg yolks, sugar and butter, so they are vegetarian but rarely vegan. Some older recipes use lard.

None of this means you will go hungry. It means asking questions, and asking them early.

## A few phrases that do the work

Staff in cities and tourist areas usually speak good English, but in a village tasca a few words of Portuguese make everything easier, and they signal that you mean it.

| English | Portuguese |
|---|---|
| I am vegetarian | Sou vegetariano (m) / vegetariana (f) |
| I am vegan | Sou vegano (m) / vegana (f) |
| I don't eat meat or fish | Não como carne nem peixe |
| Without meat, please | Sem carne, por favor |
| Is it made with meat stock? | É feito com caldo de carne? |
| Does it have eggs or milk? | Tem ovos ou leite? |
| Is there a vegetarian dish? | Há algum prato vegetariano? |

Be specific. "Vegetariano" is widely understood, but it helps to add *nem peixe* (nor fish) and to ask about stock, which is the ingredient people most often forget.

The idea is hardly foreign, either. Since 2017, Portuguese law has required public canteens, such as those in schools and hospitals, to include at least one vegetarian option on their daily menus, under [Lei n.º 11/2017](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/lei/11-2017-106886578). Restaurants are a different matter, but the word is part of everyday life.

## Naturally meat-free things to order

Some of the best things on a Portuguese table happen to be vegetarian, and a few are vegan.

- **Bread, olives and cheese.** The basket that arrives as you sit down is not free (our guide to [how eating out works in Portugal](/stories/eating-out-in-portugal/) explains the couvert), but the bread is often superb, and the olives are usually dressed simply with garlic, oregano or herbs.
- **Peixinhos da horta.** Literally "little fish from the garden": green beans dipped in batter and fried. A petisco that fooled nobody except the name.
- **Salads.** A *salada mista* of tomato, lettuce and onion, dressed at the table with olive oil and vinegar, is on almost every menu, and in summer the tomatoes are worth the trip.
- **Cheeses.** Sheep's and goat's cheeses from the Serra da Estrela, the Alentejo and the Azores are remarkable. Strict vegetarians should check the rennet. Several of the great sheep's cheeses are set with cardoon, a wild thistle, rather than animal rennet; the Center of Portugal tourism board describes [Serra da Estrela cheese](https://centerofportugal.com/article/serra-da-estrela-cheese) as set with cardoon flower. Our guide to [Portuguese cheese](/stories/portuguese-cheese/) explains what to look for.
- **Roasted and grilled vegetables.** Peppers, aubergine and potatoes roasted with olive oil and garlic often appear as sides; ask for a plate of them.
- **Fruit.** Portuguese fruit in season, from cherries in late spring to figs in late summer, is the simplest pudding there is.

When there is nothing obvious on the menu, try asking for a plate built from the sides: rice, salad, chips or potatoes, and vegetables. Most kitchens will happily put one together.

## Lisbon

Lisbon is the easier city, with a long-established vegetarian scene as well as the newer cafés.

**[Os Tibetanos](https://ostibetanos.com/)**, on Rua do Salitre near the Avenida da Liberdade, says it has been serving since 1978 and calls itself the first vegetarian restaurant in Lisbon. Its menu marks vegan dishes, and it runs a set lunch on weekdays with soup, the day's speciality and dessert.

**[PSI](https://www.restaurante-psi.com/)** is a vegetarian restaurant set in a garden on Campo Mártires da Pátria, above the Baixa. [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/restaurante-psi) describes the garden as part of the experience, with a waterfall and a pond, and lists outdoor seating.

<Pair a="veg-lisbon-carrots" b="veg-lisbon-salad" captionA="Bunches of carrots catching the light in Lisbon." captionB="A plate of greens, tomatoes and radish at a café in Lisbon." />

For produce, the city's markets are your friend, and our guide to [Lisbon's markets and food halls](/stories/lisbon-markets/) covers them in more detail. Three are especially useful if you are cooking for yourself or want a picnic:

- The organic market in the garden at Príncipe Real runs [every Saturday from 7am to 2pm](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/events/fairs-and-markets-of-principe-real), according to Visit Lisboa, with producers from across Portugal.
- The [Agrobio organic market](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/events/agrobio-organic-market) is held on Saturdays in the garden at Campo Pequeno, with certified Portuguese organic farmers selling vegetables, herbs, fruit and pulses.
- On Sundays, the LX Rural farmers' market at LX Factory runs from 10am to 6pm, as listed on [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/events/lx-market).

## Porto

Porto's cooking is, if anything, heartier, and its most famous sandwich is a monument to meat. But the city has some good options.

**[daTerra](https://www.daterra.pt/marca/o-daterra/)** describes itself as the first vegetarian restaurant brand in Portugal and serves its food as a buffet. Its [locations](https://www.daterra.pt/contactos/) include Matosinhos, the Mercado Beira-Rio in Vila Nova de Gaia, near the port lodges, and Guimarães. Check each branch's hours, as some open for lunch only.

The **[Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/)**, the restored market in the city centre, is where to see what the region grows. Its site lists the market as open Monday to Friday from 8am to 7pm and Saturday from 8am to 6pm, closed on Sundays, with its restaurants open later. Fruit, vegetables, nuts, olives and bread are all there under one roof.

Heading up the Douro? Quintas that serve lunch usually cook a set menu, so tell them about your diet when you book rather than on arrival. Our [three-day Douro itinerary](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) suggests where to start.

## Vegan, specifically

Veganism is harder than vegetarianism in Portugal, mainly because of eggs, butter and cheese in pastries and sides. A few tips:

- Ask for dishes *sem ovo, sem leite, sem queijo* (without egg, milk or cheese).
- Bread is usually vegan, but enriched rolls and sweet breads are not.
- Vegetable soups are often vegan, once you have confirmed the stock.
- In supermarkets, plant milks and tofu are widely available in the larger cities.
- Dedicated vegetarian places, such as those above, are the most reliable for a full vegan meal.

## Before you book

- Learn the key phrases above, or save them on your phone, and always ask about meat or fish stock.
- If you are staying somewhere with half board, a quinta or a rural guesthouse, tell them about your diet when you book.
- Check opening days and hours for vegetarian restaurants directly on their sites; several open for lunch only on some days.
- Plan market visits around their schedules: Príncipe Real and Campo Pequeno are Saturday markets, LX Rural is on Sundays, and the [Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/) is closed on Sundays.
- If you eat cheese, ask about rennet, or look for the word *cardo* (cardoon) on labels.
