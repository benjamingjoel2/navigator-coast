You hear Praia do Norte before you see it. On a big day the sound comes up the cliff as a low, heavy boom, and the ground on the headland seems to shiver with it. Then you reach the wall by the old fort, look down, and see a mountain of grey-green water rising out of the Atlantic, with a jet ski and a tiny figure on a board racing across its face.

<Figure id="nbw-beach-from-sitio" caption="From the Sítio, Nazaré's main beach curves south; Praia do Norte lies on the other side of the headland." />

Nazaré has two personalities. To the south of the headland is the town beach: a long, sheltered curve of sand backed by tiled houses, crowded with families in summer. To the north is Praia do Norte, where in winter the ocean does things it does almost nowhere else on Earth. This guide is about the second, and how to see it well and safely.

## Why the waves are so big

The answer lies offshore, under the water. The Nazaré Canyon is a vast submarine canyon running towards the coast here. According to the Centro region's tourism board, [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-big-waves), it is about 230 kilometres long, with a maximum depth of at least 5,000 metres, and it is the reason the waves at Praia do Norte are so big and break so high.

Put simply, the canyon funnels and focuses Atlantic swell into the beach. When a powerful winter swell arrives from the right direction, the result can be waves of extraordinary size breaking close to the shore, close enough that you can watch them from the cliff.

## The records

Nazaré became famous fast. Center of Portugal records that on 1 November 2011 the Hawaiian surfer Garrett McNamara rode a wave of [78 feet (23.77 metres)](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-big-waves) at Praia do Norte, a photo of which went around the world.

The records have kept falling since. Guinness World Records lists the [largest wave surfed by a man](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/78115-largest-wave-surfed-unlimited) as 26.21 metres (86 feet), ridden by Sebastian Steudtner off Praia do Norte on 29 October 2020. The [women's record](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/542139-largest-wave-surfed-unlimited-female), 22.4 metres (73.5 feet), was set here too, by Maya Gabeira on 11 February 2020.

<Figure id="nbw-crowd-watching" caption="Spectators on the headland watch a tow surfer drop down the face of a wave at Praia do Norte." />

These are tow-in waves: far too big and fast to paddle into, so surfers are towed in by jet ski, with a second rider standing by to pull them out. It is a specialist sport for a handful of professionals, and what you are watching is genuinely dangerous.

## When to go

Big waves need big swell, and big swell comes in winter. Center of Portugal gives the big-wave season as running [from November to February](https://www.centerofportugal.com/article/nazare-big-waves). The World Surf League's big-wave contest at Praia do Norte has used a competition window running [from 1 November to 31 March](https://www.worldsurfleague.com/posts/536163/official-competition-window-for-tudor-nazar-big-wave-challenge-opens-today), and it is only called on when a suitable swell arrives, so there is no fixed date to plan around.

That is the key thing to understand: the giant days are not scheduled. They depend on storms far out in the Atlantic. You can visit in the heart of winter and see a calm sea, and you can get lucky in late autumn. If seeing the waves is the whole point of your trip, stay flexible, keep an eye on the forecasts for a few days, and be ready to go when the swell is on its way.

For how winter weather plays out across the rest of the country, see our [guide to the best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/).

## Where to watch: the fort and the lighthouse

The classic viewpoint is the headland itself. At its tip stands the Fort of São Miguel Arcanjo, with a red lighthouse on top. According to the municipality's visitor site, [Findout Nazaré](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/forte-de-s-miguel-arcanjo-farol/), building began in 1577, under King Sebastian, to defend this stretch of coast against pirates; it was renovated and enlarged in 1644, and a navigation light has shone here since 1 December 1903.

<Pair a="nbw-lighthouse-lantern" b="nbw-town-aerial" captionA="The red lantern of the Nazaré lighthouse, on top of the old fort." captionB="The funicular climbs from the town to the Sítio, high above the beach." />

The same page describes the fort as the privileged viewpoint over the big waves of Praia do Norte. Inside, it houses an interpretive centre on the Nazaré Canyon and the Surfer Wall, along with temporary exhibitions. Opening hours and the small entry fee are listed on the [Findout Nazaré page](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/forte-de-s-miguel-arcanjo-farol/); check them for your date.

On big days, the area around the fort fills up. Arrive early, especially at weekends, and be prepared to walk the last stretch. The best views are not always from the fort itself: the cliff paths and terraces along the headland give a broader view of the whole beach and the line-up.

### Staying safe on a big day

- **Stay behind the barriers.** The cliffs here are high and the edges are unstable.
- **Keep off the rocks and the beach.** Waves on a big day can surge far further up the sand than you expect, with no warning.
- **Wrap up.** It is often windy, damp and cold on the headland in winter, and you may stand around for hours.
- **Check the warnings.** IPMA, Portugal's weather service, publishes [weather warnings](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) and a [beach and coastal forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/). If there is a warning in force for your stretch of coast, take it seriously.

## Getting up to the Sítio

The fort sits below the Sítio, the old upper town on the clifftop, and the easiest way up from the beach is the funicular. [Findout Nazaré](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/ascensor-da-nazare/) explains that it opened on 28 July 1889, designed by the engineer Raul Mesnier du Ponsard, who was also responsible for most of Lisbon's funiculars. The line is 380 metres long and was originally built partly to help pilgrims reach the shrine of Our Lady of Nazaré on the Sítio. The municipality has run it since 1932; current hours and fares are on the same page.

From the top station, follow the road out along the headland to the fort. On a big-wave day, the funicular is often the simplest way to avoid the scramble for parking near the lighthouse.

## Nazaré beyond the waves

Even on a calm day, Nazaré is worth the trip. It is still a fishing town at heart, and some of its traditions are on show along the beach.

On the sand near the town's cultural centre, you can see the fish-drying ground, where local women salt and dry fish on wooden racks called paneiros. The [Dried Fish Museum](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/museu-do-peixe-seco-museu-vivo/), a living museum that opened in 2016, covers the drying ground itself, an interpretive centre and an area where you can watch the fish being prepared. Horse mackerel, sardines and octopus are among the most common catches dried this way, and the fish is usually eaten boiled with potatoes and olive oil.

The town museum, the [Museu Dr. Joaquim Manso](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/museu-dr-joaquim-manso/) on the Sítio, reopened in September 2026 after several years of renovation, in time for its 50th anniversary. Its collection of traditional boats, fishing gear and local costume tells the story of the town's relationship with the sea, which makes it a good companion to the drama at Praia do Norte.

## Getting there

Nazaré is an easy trip from Lisbon. The municipality's [travel page](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/como-chegar/) puts Lisbon airport about 100 kilometres away, roughly an hour's drive on the A8 motorway. Rede Expressos coaches link the town with Portugal's main cities, and the nearest railway station is Valado dos Frades on the Oeste line, about six kilometres from town.

With a car, Nazaré fits neatly into a trip up the coast. It combines naturally with the walled town of Óbidos, as in our guide to [Óbidos and Nazaré](/stories/obidos-and-nazare/), and pairs well with a surf-town stop on the way, such as [Ericeira](/stories/ericeira-guide/). Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire, coaches and tolls.

## Before you book

- Remember that the giant waves are swell-dependent: build flexibility into your dates if you are coming specifically to see them.
- Watch the forecasts in the days before you go, and check [IPMA's warnings](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) on the day.
- Check the fort's current [opening hours](https://findoutnazare.pt/en/listing/forte-de-s-miguel-arcanjo-farol/) before you plan your visit around it.
- On a big-wave weekend, arrive early or use the funicular rather than driving up to the lighthouse.
- Bring warm, waterproof layers: the headland is exposed, and winter weather can turn quickly.
