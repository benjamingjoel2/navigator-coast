Stand at the bottom of Rua de Santa Catarina on a bright morning and look up. Many of the façades on the street are wearing tiles: glossy, cool to the touch, some plain white with a raised pattern, some a busy lattice of blue and yellow, a few covered from pavement to cornice in painted scenes. Porto is a granite city, grey and serious, and the azulejos are what make it sing.

Most visitors photograph them. Fewer read them. Yet the tiles are full of information, about saints and kings, about who paid for a building and when, about what the city thought was beautiful a century or three ago. This is a walk through the old centre with that in mind: a morning, give or take, of looking closely.

## How to read a tile

Before the walk, a little vocabulary. Broadly, you will meet two kinds of tilework in Porto.

**Pattern tiles** are the city's wallpaper. Each tile carries part of a design, and the design only resolves when dozens are laid together: a star here, a quatrefoil there, a border running round a window. Most of the tiled houses you pass are dressed this way, and the patterns change with the decades in which the façades were done.

**Figurative panels** are the showpieces. Here the tiles are painted as a single picture, often in blue on white, and the whole wall becomes a canvas. Churches use them for the lives of saints; the railway station uses them for national history. Step back to see the scene, then step close to see the brushwork, and you will notice that the faces and folds of cloth are painted by hand.

A third thing to look out for is the frame. Panels are often edged with painted borders, scrolls and garlands that imitate carved stone. When the light is low, the glaze picks up the sky, and a whole wall can shift from white to silver to blue in the space of an afternoon.

## São Bento: history on the walls

Start where many first-time visitors to Porto arrive, at São Bento station. Porto City Council's own [guide to the station](https://porto.pt/en/news/visit-the-most-charming-sao-bento-train-station-in-porto-city-centre) describes walls and ceilings covered with around 22,000 tiles by Jorge Colaço (1868–1942), whom it calls a painter, caricaturist and potter. The panels depict episodes from Portuguese history, among them King João I and Philippa of Lancaster, Egas Moniz, and Prince Henry at the conquest of Ceuta. The same page notes that the first train reached São Bento in 1896 and the station was inaugurated on 5 October 1916.

It is a working station, so the hall fills and empties with each train. Go early and stand with your back to the platforms: the big narrative scenes are high on the walls, so give your neck a moment and take them one at a time. If you are arriving by rail, our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers the train network in more detail.

## Up to the cathedral cloister

From São Bento it is a short, steep climb to the Sé, Porto's cathedral, sitting on its granite terrace above the old town. Visit Porto's [page on the cathedral](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b4bf979e0000193909e) dates the cloister to the 14th century and credits its tiles to Vital Rifarto. The cloister is part of a paid visit that also takes in the cathedral museum and tower; check current opening hours and tickets on that page before you go.

<Pair a="az-se-cloister" b="az-church-panel" captionA="Gothic arches and blue-and-white tile panels in the cloister of Porto's cathedral." captionB="Painted azulejos covering the wall of one of Porto's churches." />

What makes the cloister worth the climb is the contrast. The architecture is Gothic, all pointed arches and pale stone, and the walls behind it are lined with blue-and-white panels. Walk the four sides slowly. The panels are tall and close, so you read them almost like the pages of a book.

## The Carmo church and the city's tile bank

Drop back down through the old streets to the Carmo church, near the university. According to [Visit Porto](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b43f979e000013bee41), the church was built in the second half of the 18th century and is considered one of the most remarkable buildings of Porto rococo. In 1912 its side façade was covered with tiles, with drawings by Silvestre Silvestri and compositions alluding to the cult of Our Lady.

This is one of the great tiled walls of the city, and it runs along the street, so you can stand right beside it, then cross over to take in the whole composition.

A few minutes away, on Praça de Carlos Alberto, is one of the most interesting and least-visited stops on this walk. The [Banco de Materiais](https://museudoporto.pt/banco-de-materiais/), part of the Museu do Porto, is a municipal project that rescues materials from degraded or demolished buildings so they can one day go back into the city. It occupies the Palacete dos Viscondes de Balsemão, and its collection includes tiles, among them Hispano-Arabic examples and a wide variety of patterns from the 17th to the 20th centuries, alongside plasterwork, street name plates and pieces in wood, iron and stone. The museum notes it has been open to the public since 2010, with free entry.

If the pattern tiles on the street have started to blur together, this is the place to sort them out. Seeing dozens of designs laid side by side, with their dates, sharpens your eye for the rest of the day.

## Santa Catarina: the Capela das Almas

Head east towards Rua de Santa Catarina, the long shopping street that runs uphill from Praça da Batalha. Further up the street stands the most photographed building on this walk.

<Pair a="az-capela-almas-front" b="az-tiled-facade" captionA="The tiled front of the Capela das Almas on Rua de Santa Catarina." captionB="Tall town houses with iron balconies, the nearest dressed in blue-and-white tiles." />

Visit Porto's [entry for the Capela das Almas](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b4af979e000013ddc9b) says the chapel was built at the beginning of the 18th century, and that in 1929 its whole exterior was covered with tiles showing scenes from the lives of St Francis of Assisi and St Catherine. The tile panels were restored in 1982. Entry is free.

The best way to read it is to walk all the way round. The scenes run in sequence along the side wall, and the side street gives you enough distance to take in a whole panel at once. Then come back to the front and look up at the painted frame around the door, where the tiles imitate architectural detail that isn't really there.

## Batalha: a second Colaço

At the bottom of Santa Catarina, on Praça da Batalha, the Church of Santo Ildefonso is worth a detour if you have already seen São Bento. [Visit Porto](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b43f979e000013bee74) records that the church was built between 1709 and 1739, and that its façade tiles were made by Jorge Colaço in 1932, showing scenes from the life of St Ildefonso and allegories of the Eucharist.

Seeing two Colaço works in one morning is a good lesson in style. The station is secular and narrative, full of horses and crowds; the church is devotional and framed for a single large façade. The hand is recognisably the same.

## Coffee, a grocery and a market

You will need a sit-down by now. On Rua de Santa Catarina, [Café Majestic](https://www.cafemajestic.com) is one of the city's set pieces: Visit Porto notes it was [founded in 1921](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b42f979e000013a9c4d) and calls it one of the most representative examples of Art Nouveau in Porto.

Tiles turn up in commerce too. On Rua Formosa, [A Pérola do Bolhão](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b42f979e000013a9c01) is a traditional grocery founded in 1917, with an Art Nouveau front covered in tiles depicting the spice routes; inside, Visit Porto lists Serra cheese, cured sausages, dried and crystallised fruit sold by weight, and regional wines. Close by, the [Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/en/histories/) describes itself as municipal heritage and a monument of public interest, tied to the city's history for more than a hundred years. Check its [opening hours](https://mercadobolhao.pt/en/) before you go: it closes on Sundays. If you want to know what to buy, our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) has a section on the North.

## Making a day of it

The whole walk sits within Porto's compact historic centre, and it works well as the first morning of a city break. If you are still deciding where to base yourself, our comparison of [Lisbon and Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) runs through the neighbourhoods. For a longer trip, the Porto days of our [ten-day Portugal itinerary](/stories/ten-day-portugal-itinerary/) fold the tiled churches into a wider tour of the city.

A few habits make tile-spotting more rewarding:

- **Go in the morning.** Many façades face narrow streets and fall into shade later in the day, and the glaze reads better in soft light.
- **Look up.** Some of the best tilework sits above shopfronts, on upper storeys and around balconies.
- **Respect working spaces.** São Bento is a station and the churches are places of worship. Keep voices down, check whether photography is allowed inside, and avoid visiting during services.
- **Never buy loose old tiles.** A single antique tile on a street stall has to have come off a wall somewhere. If you want a souvenir, buy new, hand-painted work from a shop that makes or sells it openly.

## Before you book

- Check current opening hours for the [cathedral and cloister](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b4bf979e0000193909e) and the [Carmo church](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b43f979e000013bee41) on Visit Porto; they can change for services and holidays.
- The [Capela das Almas](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB/poi/5cd04b4af979e000013ddc9b) and the [Banco de Materiais](https://museudoporto.pt/banco-de-materiais/) are free to enter; confirm the Banco de Materiais' hours on the Museu do Porto site.
- Plan the Bolhão market and A Pérola do Bolhão for a weekday or Saturday morning; both close on Sundays.
- Wear shoes with grip. The walk climbs and descends on granite cobbles, which are slippery after rain.
- Pack a light layer for church interiors and the cloister, which stay cool even on warm days.
