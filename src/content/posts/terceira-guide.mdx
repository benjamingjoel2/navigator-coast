Climb the lane up Monte Brasil early in the morning and Angra do Heroísmo lies below you like a model of itself. Church towers, red roofs, pastel façades picked out in grey stone, all packed around a small bay where ships once waited for treasure fleets. It is one of the most handsome towns in Portugal, and it happens to be in the middle of the Atlantic.

Terceira is the island of Angra, but it is also the island that likes a party. The tourism board calls it the festive island, and from spring to autumn there is usually something going on: a Holy Ghost feast in a village square, a street procession, or a bull running down a lane on the end of a long rope. Come for the town and the festivals; stay for the green interior and the coast.

## Angra do Heroísmo: a World Heritage town

Angra's importance came from its harbour. As [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/the-azores/terceira) tells it, the bay was a stopover in the 15th and 16th centuries for ships sailing between Europe, the Americas and India, and the city, founded in 1534, became the political, economic and religious centre of the Azores. In the 19th century it served as a base for the liberal cause and was briefly named capital of the kingdom, earning the name Heroísmo. UNESCO added the historic centre to the World Heritage List in 1983.

<Figure id="terc-angra-monte-brasil" size="full" caption="Angra and the bay, with the green bulk of Monte Brasil rising beyond the town." />

The pleasure of Angra is wandering. Visit Azores describes façades of trachyte stonework and bright paint, iron balconies and decorated windows, and a grid of streets that generations restored even after earthquakes. Places open to visitors include the cathedral, the Palace of the Captains-General, the City Hall, the Church of São Gonçalo and the Bettencourt Palace.

For background, the [Angra do Heroísmo Museum](https://www.visitazores.com/en/explore/angra-do-heroismo-museum) occupies a former Franciscan convent. Visit Azores lists collections that run from cars and weapons to coins, navigational instruments, paintings and porcelain, and notes the adjoining church of Nossa Senhora da Guia with its 18th-century carved woodwork and tile panels.

### Monte Brasil

The headland that shelters the bay is an extinct volcano. [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/explore/monte-brasil) describes it as three square kilometres in area, ringed by the four kilometres of walls of the São João Baptista fortress, which it calls the oldest fortress continuously occupied by the Portuguese army. Paths climb to viewpoints over the town; the tourism board's island page calls the view from the top one of the finest over Angra and its bay.

<Figure id="terc-monte-brasil-view" caption="From the wooded slopes of Monte Brasil, the view across the water to the Terceira coast." />

### Where to stay in Angra

The most historic option is inside a fort. [Pousada Forte Angra do Heroísmo](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-angra) occupies the Fort of São Sebastião, which the pousada describes as a 16th-century fort that once defended the town against pirates and corsairs. Elsewhere on the island, Visit Azores notes that some old farmhouses and manor houses now take guests.

## The festive island

Terceira's calendar is the main reason many people choose it over other islands. The tourism board's [Terceira page](https://www.visitazores.com/en/the-azores/terceira) sets out the year.

### The Holy Ghost festivals

The Festas do Espírito Santo are the heart of island life. Visit Azores says they take place over the eight weeks between Easter Sunday and Trinity Sunday, centred on small, brightly painted chapels called impérios. You will see impérios in almost every parish, many with elaborate façades; the tourism board says they are part of the island's identity.

<Pair a="terc-imperio" b="terc-angra-church" captionA="The Império da Caridade in Angra, dated 1895 on its façade." captionB="A white church rising above Angra's rooftops." />

### Sanjoaninas

Angra's big summer festival honours São João. The [official Sanjoaninas site](https://www.sanjoaninas.pt/) gave the 2026 dates as 19 to 28 June, describes it as the largest festival in the Azores, and lists concerts on several stages, themed parades, food stalls, exhibitions, sport and bullfighting both in the ring and on a rope. Its high point is the night of 23 June and the marchas populares, the costumed neighbourhood parades. Check the site for next year's programme, and book accommodation early if your dates overlap.

### Bulls on a rope

The tourada à corda is Terceira's own tradition. As [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/the-azores/terceira) explains, a bull runs through the streets on a long rope held by a group of men, and the season normally runs from May to October, with some events fixed in advance and others arranged at short notice. If you watch, keep behind a wall or in a doorway, well out of the bull's reach, and move when the rope handlers tell you to. Not every visitor will want to attend, and that is fine: the festivals around them are just as much a part of island life.

### The rest of the year

Visit Azores also lists the Carnival danças and bailinhos, satirical street performances held over three days; the Praia Festival in August at Praia da Vitória; the Festas da Vinha e do Vinho in Biscoitos at the start of September; and two music festivals in Angra, Angra Rock in September and Angra Jazz in October.

## Beyond Angra

Terceira's interior is a broad green upland of pasture, stone walls and hydrangea hedges, with the largest area of native forest in the archipelago, according to Visit Azores.

### Under the island

The best-known volcanic sight is the [Algar do Carvão](https://www.visitazores.com/en/explore/algar-do-carvao-0), a 90-metre-deep volcanic chimney that Visit Azores says formed about 3,200 years ago. You descend into it rather than looking down into it. It is managed by the speleological association [Os Montanheiros](https://www.montanheiros.com/), and its [visitor page](https://www.montanheiros.com/algarCarvao/) currently says the chimney closed on 1 November 2026 for building works, with reopening expected in March 2027 alongside a new visitor centre. In a normal year it opens daily from April to September with afternoon hours; the same page gives the 2026–2027 schedule and warns that hours may change without notice. The visit involves 338 steps.

Nearby, Visit Azores describes the Gruta do Natal, a lava tube with long, straight passages, and the Furnas do Enxofre, a field of steaming fumaroles. Os Montanheiros also handles visits to the Gruta do Natal, so check their site for its hours before you drive up.

### Coast and pools

Terceira's swimming is mostly in natural rock pools. The tourism board names Porto Martins, Biscoitos, Negrito and Silveira among them, and Praia da Vitória has the island's main sandy beach. Biscoitos, on the north coast, is also the island's wine village; the tourism board describes it as a place with a wine tradition, celebrated each September.

<Figure id="terc-angra-marina" caption="Yachts and fishing boats in Angra's marina, below the old town walls." />

### Walking

The [Azores Trails page for Terceira](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/islands/terceira) lists the island's official routes. Easy options include the circular Lagoa do Cerro walk near Altares, 6.4 km through pasture and woodland to a viewpoint at about 650 metres, and the Rota da Água, a 4.2 km circuit near Angra that follows old mills and the town's historic water supply. For something longer, the Grande Rota do Oeste is listed at 31.2 km. Our guide to [hiking in the Azores](/stories/azores-hiking/) covers trail safety and weather.

## Food

Terceira's signature dish is alcatra. Visit Azores describes it as fish or beef on the bone, cooked slowly in a clay pot with bacon, onion, garlic, bay leaf, pepper and wine, and served with bread or massa sovada, a sweet bread. For something sweet, look for Dona Amélia cakes, which the tourism board describes as made with honey, cinnamon and raisins. Our [regional food guide](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) puts these in context.

## Getting there and around

Terceira is one of the better-connected islands. Azores Airlines' [destinations list](https://www.azoresairlines.pt/en/destinations) includes routes to Terceira from Lisbon, Porto and Toronto as well as inter-island flights, and it added a [direct Terceira–Funchal route](https://www.azoresairlines.pt/en/node/1916) from the 2026 summer season, which makes an Azores and Madeira trip easier. Our comparison of [Azores or Madeira](/stories/azores-or-madeira/) can help you decide how to split your time.

By sea, [Atlânticoline's timetable](https://www.atlanticoline.pt/en/timetable/) shows sailings from Angra do Heroísmo and Praia da Vitória to other central islands. Services vary through the year, so check your exact dates. On the island, a hire car makes it easy to combine Angra with the interior and the north coast in a day. Terceira also makes a good base for a trip that includes [whale watching](/stories/whale-watching-azores/) on Pico or Faial.

## Before you book

- If you want the Sanjoaninas, check the [official festival site](https://www.sanjoaninas.pt/) for next year's dates and book Angra accommodation well ahead.
- Check the Algar do Carvão's [current status and hours](https://www.montanheiros.com/algarCarvao/) before planning around a visit.
- Ask locally about touradas à corda in the area you are staying, and watch from a safe spot.
- Confirm flights and Atlânticoline sailings for your dates, and leave a spare day if you are connecting to another island.
- Pack a light waterproof: the interior uplands can be cloudy even when Angra is in sunshine.
