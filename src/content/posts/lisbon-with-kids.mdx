The moment usually comes in the half-dark of the Oceanário. You round a corner and the main tank fills your whole field of view, a wall of blue with shoals of silver fish turning together, rays gliding overhead, and a small child somewhere near the glass who has gone completely, wonderfully silent.

Lisbon is a better city for children than its hills suggest. It is compact, sunny for much of the year and full of things that delight younger travellers: trams that rattle and squeal, a castle with real battlements, a cable car over the river, gardens with ducks and, at almost every corner, a pastelaria with a custard tart in the window. The trick is to plan around the slopes and give the day a gentle shape.

## How to pace a family day

Lisbon is built on hills, and cobbled hills at that. Pavements of polished limestone can be slippery, and many old streets are stepped. A light, foldable pushchair or a baby carrier is far easier than a big buggy in Alfama or the Bairro Alto.

Plan one big thing in the morning, a long lunch, a rest, and something easy late in the afternoon. Use public transport for the climbs: Lisbon's metro and Carris trams and buses are the way to save little legs. The metro's [ticket page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/) lists a 24-hour ticket covering Carris and the metro, with versions that add the Cacilhas ferry or CP's suburban lines, which is useful if you plan several hops in a day. Check current fares and any children's discounts before you go.

The [Lisboa Card](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisboa-card), from the city's tourism board, includes free public transport and free entry to a long list of museums and monuments. Whether it pays depends on how much you plan to see, so add up your likely sights first.

## Parque das Nações: a whole day by the river

If you only have one day, spend it here. The riverside district built for Expo 98 is flat, open and traffic-light, with wide promenades that are perfect for scooters and pushchairs, and two of the city's best attractions for children side by side.

**The Oceanário.** The [Oceanário de Lisboa](https://www.oceanario.pt/en) sits on the water in Parque das Nações and, according to its website, is open daily. Its permanent exhibition, One Planet, One Ocean, takes you through different ocean habitats, and the website has a section of activities for families. Book tickets online, go early or late in the day, and allow a good couple of hours.

**Pavilhão do Conhecimento.** Next door, the [Pavilhão do Conhecimento](https://www.pavconhecimento.pt/en/) describes itself as Portugal's largest science centre, open since 1999. Its exhibitions are hands-on and change through the year. Check its schedule before you go; its website lists it as closed on Mondays.

**The cable car.** Between the two ends of the riverfront, the [Telecabine Lisboa](https://www.telecabinelisboa.pt/) carries you in a small gondola above the water's edge, with panoramic views of the Tagus estuary and the south bank, according to the operator. Children love it, and it saves a long walk on the way back.

## The castle and the old town

The [Castelo de São Jorge](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/choose-your-ticket/) is the obvious adventure in the old town. There are walls to walk, towers to climb and some of the best views in the city across the rooftops to the river. Families should note that, according to the castle's ticket page, entry is free for children up to 12 with proof of age.

The climb is the hard part. Take a tram, bus or taxi up and walk down, rather than the other way round. Our guide to [Alfama, Mouraria and Graça](/stories/lisbon-alfama-mouraria-graca/) explains the neighbourhoods below the walls. The famous yellow trams are fun but often crowded in high season; ride them early in the morning, and hold small hands tightly near the tracks.

## Belém: boats, pastries and space to run

Belém, on the river west of the centre, is easy with children. The waterfront is flat, the lawns are wide and the sights are close together. Our guide to [Belém in a day](/stories/belem-in-a-day/) covers the monuments; for families, three stops stand out.

- **Museu de Marinha.** Part of the Navy's [Centro de Conhecimento do Mar](https://ccm.marinha.pt/pt/museu), the Museu de Marinha tells the story of Portugal and the sea. The same cultural centre also runs a planetarium and the Aquário Vasco da Gama, which are worth checking for children's sessions.
- **Pastéis de Belém.** On Rua de Belém, [Pastéis de Belém](https://pasteisdebelem.pt/en/history/) says it has been baking its custard tarts since 1837, to a recipe that came from the neighbouring Jerónimos Monastery. Expect a queue for takeaway; the warm tart, dusted with cinnamon and sugar, is a reward children will remember.
- **The riverside.** Let them run along the waterfront between the monuments, watch the boats and ferries, and finish with an ice cream.

## Green spaces

Lisbon's gardens are a lifeline on a hot afternoon. The garden of the [Gulbenkian Foundation](https://gulbenkian.pt/en/useful-information/opening-hours/) is open every day from sunrise to sunset and entry is free, according to the foundation. Shaded paths, a pond, lawns and ducks make it a calm place for a picnic, while parents can take turns in the museum.

<Figure id="lxkid-gulbenkian-duck" size="column" caption="One of the Gulbenkian garden's residents, unbothered by visitors." />

**The zoo.** The [Jardim Zoológico](https://www.zoo.pt/en/visit/how-to-find-us/) is a short walk from Jardim Zoológico metro station on the blue line and close to Sete Rios rail and coach stations, according to the zoo. It makes an easy half-day for animal-mad children.

## Beaches by train

On hot days, do as Lisbon families do and head for the sea. CP's [Cascais line](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/cascais-line) runs along the coast from Cais do Sodré, through Belém and stations such as Carcavelos and Parede, to Cascais. The beaches along the line are an easy outing with buckets and spades, and the ride itself, with the river widening into the Atlantic outside the window, is part of the fun. Choose lifeguarded beaches, follow the flags, and go early to find space on summer weekends.

A day trip to [Sintra](/stories/sintra-day-trip/) is also possible with older children, who will enjoy the palaces and woodland, though the hills and queues make it a long day for little ones.

## Eating out with children

Portugal is relaxed about children in restaurants. Lunch is the main meal for many families, and simple dishes such as grilled chicken, fish, rice and chips are easy to find. Many restaurants will happily share a dish between two small children; ask. Dinner tends to start later than in northern Europe, so a big lunch and an early, light supper often works best.

Pastelarias are the other great family resource: a sandwich, a fresh orange juice and a pastry make an easy, inexpensive stop between sights.

## Where to stay

Choose a base near a metro station and away from the steepest streets. Parque das Nações, Avenidas Novas and parts of the Baixa and Chiado are flatter and well connected; Alfama is atmospheric but hard work with a pushchair. Our guide on [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) compares the neighbourhoods. An apartment with a kitchen and a washing machine makes life easier on a longer stay.

## Before you book

- Book Oceanário tickets online and plan Parque das Nações for a full day.
- Check opening days: the [Pavilhão do Conhecimento](https://www.pavconhecimento.pt/en/) lists Monday closures, and hours change through the year.
- Bring proof of age for children's free or reduced entry, as at the [Castelo de São Jorge](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/choose-your-ticket/).
- Pack a light pushchair or a carrier for the hills, plus sun hats and water in summer.
- Compare the [Lisboa Card](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisboa-card) with day tickets once you know your plans.
- For beach days, check the [Cascais line](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/cascais-line) timetable and choose lifeguarded beaches.
