Porto is a city of granite and gradients. Its houses stack up the north bank of the Douro in narrow, tiled towers, washing hangs from iron balconies, and every street seems to run either steeply down to the river or steeply up away from it. Across the water, the port lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia spell out their names in big letters along the rooftops. The view from one bank to the other is the classic Porto picture, and choosing which side of it to sleep on is the first decision you will make.

Porto is smaller than Lisbon and you can walk across the centre in well under an hour, but the quarters still feel distinct. Some are perfect for a first weekend; others make more sense on a second visit or a longer stay. Here is how they compare, with a few long-established places to stay in each.

## How the city fits together

The historic centre is the oldest part of the city. Visit Porto's own brochure notes that Porto was [classified by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 1996](https://visitporto.travel/storage/01HRF8XZJBM4MMMF31J925Z9F9.pdf), and describes walking down from the cathedral to the Ribeira along streets whose names go back to the Middle Ages.

Porto City Council's own [plan for the city's quarters](https://visitporto.travel/storage/01HZER0RKPNVVFD34JJNNK2ZA4.pdf) is a useful way to think about the rest. It treats the historic centres of Porto and Gaia as one area, then sets out Downtown, Foz, Boavista and Bonfim as quarters with their own identities, and it explicitly encourages visitors to look beyond the busiest central streets.

For getting about, the metro is the backbone. Metro do Porto lists six lines, including [line E between Estádio do Dragão and the airport](https://en.metrodoporto.pt/), and runs on the Andante ticketing system, which includes [Andante Tour tickets for visitors](https://en.metrodoporto.pt/pages/397) valid for 24 or 72 hours. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) explains how the cards work.

## Ribeira and the historic centre

This is the Porto of the postcards: the riverside quay, the stepped lanes behind it, the cathedral above and the Dom Luís I bridge striding across the gorge. Staying here means waking up to the river and walking everywhere. It also means steep climbs back from dinner, and summer crowds and noise along the waterfront.

<Figure id="pts-ribeira-from-bridge" size="full" caption="The Ribeira quayside, with the old city stacked up the north bank of the Douro." />

If you want the historic centre without being right on the quay, look a few streets up. Rua das Flores, a pedestrian street that runs from near São Bento station down towards the river, is a good compromise.

**Where to stay.** On Rua das Flores, [Casa da Companhia](https://www.casadacompanhia.com/) occupies a house that it says was built in the 16th century and rented in the 18th century, in the time of the Marquis of Pombal, by the Companhia Geral de Agricultura das Vinhas do Alto Douro, the forerunner of the port house Real Companhia Velha. A few doors along, the [Pousada Porto Rua das Flores](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-porto) is in an 18th-century building that keeps its vaulted ceilings and original stonework. In the Old Town, [Infante Sagres](https://www.infantesagres.com/) describes itself as the original five-star hotel in Porto and was refurbished in 2024.

## Baixa (Downtown)

Just above the historic centre, the Baixa is the city's commercial heart, around Avenida dos Aliados, the Bolhão market and Rua de Santa Catarina. The council's quarters plan sums it up as iconic avenues with a diverse food scene and a youthful atmosphere of fun and nightlife. It is central, slightly flatter than the riverfront, well served by the metro and a short walk from São Bento station, which makes it the most practical base for a first visit.

<Pair a="pts-azulejo-church" b="pts-rooftop-window" captionA="Blue-and-white azulejo panels on a church wall in central Porto." captionB="Church towers and rooftops of central Porto, framed by a window." />

**Where to stay.** The [Grande Hotel do Porto](https://www.grandehotelporto.com/) is on the pedestrian stretch of Rua de Santa Catarina, about five minutes from São Bento. Its rooms draw on what the hotel calls its classic 1880 style.

## Cedofeita and the Bombarda district

West of the Baixa, the streets flatten out a little and become more residential. Rua de Cedofeita is a long shopping street, and nearby the Bombarda Art District has galleries, independent shops and cafés. The council includes both in its Downtown quarter. It is a good choice if you like to come back to a neighbourhood rather than a tourist hub, and the centre is still a short walk away.

## Bonfim

East of the centre, Bonfim is the quarter the council describes as ["the irreverent and young Porto"](https://visitporto.travel/storage/01HZER0RKPNVVFD34JJNNK2ZA4.pdf), with a creative edge and an artistic offer of its own. It is mostly residential, with older houses, local tascas and newer cafés, and it is quieter at night than downtown. It suits travellers who have seen the main sights before or who want a more local rhythm, and it puts you closer to Campanhã station for trains to Lisbon and the Douro.

## Boavista and Campo Alegre

The council's quarters plan calls this area the Porto of architecture, music and art, with the Casa da Música concert hall and the botanical gardens among its landmarks, and notes its traditional hotel offer. Boavista is a broad modern avenue rather than a historic quarter, so it lacks Ribeira's charm. In exchange, you get larger hotels, flatter streets, easier parking and good metro links. It works well for business travellers, families and anyone with a car.

## Foz do Douro

Where the river meets the Atlantic, Foz is Porto's seaside. The council's plan describes it as ["the Porto that joins the river to the ocean"](https://visitporto.travel/storage/01HZER0RKPNVVFD34JJNNK2ZA4.pdf), and lists Foz Velha and Cantareira, the waterfront and the beaches among its points of interest. It has an esplanade for evening walks, old fishermen's streets, seafood restaurants and long views over the breakers.

<Figure id="pts-foz-waterfront" caption="Waves breaking against the jetty and lighthouse at Foz, where the Douro meets the Atlantic." />

Foz is calm and spacious, with a more upmarket, residential feel, and it is a lovely base for a longer stay or a summer trip. The catch is distance: you will take a bus, taxi or a long riverside walk into the centre.

**Where to stay.** [Hotel Boa-Vista](https://hotelboavista.com/) is a three-star hotel on the Esplanada do Castelo in Foz do Douro, where, in its own words, the Douro meets the Atlantic, and it lists a pool among its facilities.

## Vila Nova de Gaia

Technically a separate city, Gaia faces Porto across the river, and the council's plan treats its historic centre together with Porto's. Its riverbank is lined with the port wine cellars, and the reward for staying here is the view: the whole of the Ribeira lit up across the water at night. The trade-off is that you will cross the bridge often, and Gaia's slopes are as steep as Porto's.

<Figure id="pts-gaia-cellars" caption="Port wine casks in a cellar in Vila Nova de Gaia." />

**Where to stay.** [The Yeatman](https://www.the-yeatman-hotel.com/en/) is a wine hotel and spa set on the hill among the port lodges. Its rooms and suites have terraces or balconies facing the city.

If port is a reason for your trip, combine a night in Gaia with time upriver. Our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) itinerary picks up where the cellars leave off.

## Tourist tax and apartments

Porto charges a municipal tourist tax per person per night, collected by your hotel or apartment host. According to the [city council's leaflet](https://taxaturistica.cm-porto.pt/Content/documents/tmt_folhetos_EN.pdf), it applies for a maximum of seven consecutive nights, and children aged 12 and under are exempt, among other exemptions. Check the leaflet for the current amount.

If you book an apartment, look for its *alojamento local* registration number, which you can check in Turismo de Portugal's [national register of local accommodation](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AL.aspx).

## Before you book

- Check the walk from the nearest metro station to the door on a map with contour lines; Porto's streets are steeper than they look.
- If you are a light sleeper, avoid rooms facing the Ribeira quayside or downtown nightlife streets in summer.
- If you plan to arrive by metro from the airport, check which line stops nearest your accommodation on [Metro do Porto's site](https://en.metrodoporto.pt/).
- Budget for the municipal tourist tax, which is charged on top of the room rate.
- For apartments, ask about stairs and lifts: many historic buildings have neither wide staircases nor elevators.
- Pack proper rain gear outside summer, and grippy shoes for wet granite.
