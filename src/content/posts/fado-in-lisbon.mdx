The lights go down. Somebody at the back of the room hushes a late arrival, and the waiter freezes mid-step with a plate in each hand. Two men with guitars on their knees play a few bars, one of the instruments bright and ringing like a cross between a mandolin and a harp. Then a woman in a black shawl closes her eyes and sings, and for three minutes the whole room, tourists and regulars, forgets to breathe.

That is fado at its best: not a show so much as a confession made in public. It is the sound Lisbon is proudest of, and hearing it properly, in the right room, is one of the great experiences of a trip to Portugal. Hearing it badly is easy too. This guide is about the difference.

## What fado is

UNESCO added fado to its [Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/fado-urban-popular-song-of-portugal-00563) in 2011, describing it as a performance genre combining music and poetry, practised by various communities in Lisbon. It is usually sung by a single voice, man or woman, traditionally accompanied by a wire-strung acoustic guitar and the Portuguese guitar. In recent decades, UNESCO notes, the line-up has often grown to include a second Portuguese guitar and a bass.

Its roots are tangled, which is part of its fascination. UNESCO's account calls fado a multicultural synthesis of Afro-Brazilian sung dances, local traditional song and dance, rural traditions brought to the city by people moving in from the countryside, and early nineteenth-century urban song. Whatever the mix, it took root in Lisbon's older quarters, and its songs return again and again to longing, love, the sea and the neighbourhood itself.

The word most often attached to it is saudade, a longing for something absent that may never return. You do not need to understand Portuguese to feel it.

## The Portuguese guitar

If one sound defines fado, it is the Portuguese guitar. UNESCO describes it as a pear-shaped cittern with twelve wire strings, unique to Portugal. Look at the head of the instrument and you will see its signature: a fan of metal tuning screws, quite unlike an ordinary guitar's pegs. The player picks out melody and counter-melody around the voice, while the second guitarist keeps the rhythm and harmony underneath.

## Where it comes from: Alfama, Mouraria and the Severa legend

Fado belongs above all to Alfama and Mouraria, the old quarters climbing the castle hill. Visit Lisboa's [Fado Route](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/lisbon-stories/1-fado-route/pois) is a good way to walk its history. In Mouraria it points to Rua do Capelão, linked with Severa, the nineteenth-century singer often described as the first fadista, and the Escadinhas de São Cristóvão, a stairway decorated with what it calls the most impressive fado-themed graffiti in Lisbon. In Alfama, by the church of São Miguel, it highlights the steps leading to a little square of simple eateries where fado vadio, informal amateur fado, is sung.

It also points you to one of the city's most moving tributes: a portrait of Amália Rodrigues by the artist Vhils on Rua de São Tomé, made from the city's own cobblestones, which Visit Lisboa says appears to cry when it rains.

The lanes themselves are the best introduction. Walk Alfama in the early evening, as the lamps come on and the doors of the fado houses open, and you can hear guitars tuning through open windows. ## The Museu do Fado

Before you book a night out, spend an hour at the [Museu do Fado](https://www.museudofado.pt/en/visit), at Largo do Chafariz de Dentro, 1, at the foot of Alfama. Visit Lisboa notes that it was founded in 1998. Its permanent exhibition covers fado's history and the people who made it, alongside temporary exhibitions, a library of sheet music, recordings and posters, and a workshop that runs a course in building the Portuguese guitar.

According to its website, the museum opens from Tuesday to Sunday, 10am to 6pm, with last admission at 5.30pm, and closes on 1 January, 1 May and 24, 25 and 31 December. Tickets can be bought online.

## Amália's house

No name looms larger in fado than Amália Rodrigues, and you can visit the house she lived in. The [Casa-Museu Amália Rodrigues](https://amaliarodrigues.pt/pt/home/), run by the Fundação Amália Rodrigues on Rua de São Bento, is the eighteenth-century building where she lived for 44 years, kept as she left it. The foundation describes a salon lined with nineteenth-century tiles, contemporary Portuguese art and a typical 1950s kitchen, shown on guided visits. It opens Tuesday to Sunday, 10am to 6pm, and recommends booking ahead.

## How fado houses work

Most fado in Lisbon is heard in a casa de fado, a fado house, and most fado houses are restaurants. You book a table for dinner, and the music comes in sets between courses: a group of singers and guitarists performs a few songs, the lights come up, the plates come out, and then the next set begins. A night often means dinner, sometimes a set menu or a minimum spend, so check what is included when you book.

A few rules of the room are universal:

- **Silence during the songs.** Conversation, cutlery and phone calls stop when the music starts. Service usually pauses too.
- **No flash, and ideally no filming.** Ask before you photograph, and keep it to the breaks.
- **Arrive on time.** Doors open and sets start at set times, and arriving mid-song is awkward for everyone.
- **Book ahead,** especially on Friday and Saturday nights.

There is also fado vadio, the amateur kind, where neighbours and regulars take turns to sing in small taverns and community associations. UNESCO notes that amateurs perform it in associations in the older neighbourhoods. It is less polished and often more affecting, but it happens on its own schedule; ask locally rather than expecting a fixed show.

## Where to hear it

These are long-established houses that publish their history on their own websites. They are starting points, not a ranking.

**In Alfama**

- **[Parreirinha de Alfama](https://www.parreirinhadealfama.com/)**, on Largo do Chafariz de Dentro, near the Museu do Fado. Its website says it was a coal shop and tavern before beginning its licence process with the city in 1939, and lists Amália Rodrigues and Alfredo Marceneiro among the voices that passed through.
- **[Clube de Fado](https://www.clubedefado.pt/)**, on Rua de São João da Praça in the shadow of the Sé, in a building it describes as more than three hundred years old. It was founded in 1995 by the guitarist Mário Pacheco, who accompanied Amália Rodrigues, and serves a single set menu while the groups alternate through the evening.

**In the Bairro Alto**

- **[A Severa](https://www.asevera.com/)**, on Rua das Gáveas. Its website says it was opened in 1955 by Júlio and Maria José de Barros Evangelista and has been run by the same family for four generations.
- **[O Faia](https://www.ofaia.com/)**, on Rua da Barroca, calls itself a fado house since 1947. Visit Lisboa's [Fado Route](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/lisbon-stories/1-fado-route/pois) describes it as opened that year by a bookseller and a fado singer.

**In Lapa**

- **[Sr. Vinho](https://www.srvinho.com/)**, on Rua do Meio à Lapa, a quieter residential quarter west of the centre. It was founded in 1975 by José Luís Gordo, the fado singer Maria da Fé and António Melo Correia, and lists dinner with fado from Monday to Saturday.

The Bairro Alto and Lapa houses are easy to combine with a sunset at one of the western viewpoints; our [miradouros walk](/stories/lisbon-miradouros/) ends at Santa Catarina, not far from either. For what to order at the table, see our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

## Fado beyond Lisbon

Lisbon is fado's home, but not its only one. Coimbra has its own fado tradition, with a different repertoire and mood; our [Coimbra guide](/stories/coimbra-guide/) points to where to hear it. ## Before you book

- Reserve your fado house in advance, and check whether dinner, a set menu or a minimum spend is included.
- Check the start time of the first set and arrive before it.
- Visit the [Museu do Fado](https://www.museudofado.pt/en/visit) first; remember it is closed on Mondays.
- Book a visit to the [Casa-Museu Amália Rodrigues](https://amaliarodrigues.pt/pt/home/) ahead, as the foundation recommends.
- Keep phones silent and cameras away while the singers are on.
