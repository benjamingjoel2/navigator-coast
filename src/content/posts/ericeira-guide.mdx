Stand on the sea wall above Praia dos Pescadores at the end of the day and Ericeira explains itself. Below you, the fishing beach curls into the cliff. Behind you, the town is all white walls and blue trim, the paint picked out around every window and door. Out past the rocks, a line of small black dots sits patiently on the water: surfers, waiting for the next set.

<Figure id="eric-beach-town" caption="Ericeira's white houses line the clifftop above the town beaches, with the old sea defences below." />

Ericeira is a working fishing town that happens to sit on one of the most consistent stretches of surf in Europe. It is close enough to Lisbon for a day trip, but it rewards a slower stay: a morning in the water or watching from the cliffs, a long lunch of grilled fish, and an evening walk along the clifftop as the light goes orange.

## A fishing town that found the waves

Long before the surfboards, this was a town that lived from the sea. Save The Waves, the organisation behind the World Surfing Reserves programme, notes on its [Ericeira page](https://www.savethewaves.org/ericeira/) that the old population, known as the Jagoz, lived almost entirely from fishing, which set them apart from the farming villages inland. Later, members of the royal family and the aristocracy built summer houses here and became, in the reserve's own words, the town's first sunbathers.

Surfing arrived in the mid-1970s, and Ericeira has been the focal point of Portuguese surfing ever since. In 2011 the coast was dedicated as a World Surfing Reserve, the second anywhere in the world, according to [Save The Waves](https://www.savethewaves.org/ericeira/). The reserve covers roughly five kilometres of coastline, with a high density of quality breaks, among them Ribeira d'Ilhas and Coxos. Lisbon's tourism board, [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/ericeira-world-surfing-reserve), describes it as the first surfing reserve in Europe.

What that status means in practice is protection: the designation recognises the waves themselves as something worth preserving, alongside the fishing heritage that shaped the town.

## The breaks, and the beaches for the rest of us

You do not need a board to enjoy Ericeira's coast, and most of it is not suitable for beginners anyway. [Visit Lisboa's beach guide](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/beaches-of-ericeira) singles out Pedra Branca, Coxos and Ribeira d'Ilhas as world famous for their wave conditions. These are reef and point breaks for experienced surfers; Coxos in particular has a serious reputation. Watching from the cliff is a perfectly good way to spend an hour.

<Figure id="eric-ribeira-dilhas" caption="Surfers waiting in the line-up at Ribeira d'Ilhas, the reserve's best-known break, seen from above." />

For swimming and calmer water, head into town. The same Visit Lisboa page describes Praia dos Pescadores as one of the safest beaches in the region, thanks to the bay created by the pier. Praia do Sul, just south of the centre, is an Ericeira landmark in its own right.

Further out, the coast opens up. Foz do Lizandro, where the river meets the sea south of town, and Praia de São Julião beyond it are wide, open beaches. To the north, Visit Lisboa also recommends the small Praia do Matadouro and Praia de São Lourenço.

A few practical points for anyone getting in the water:

- **Check the sea first.** Swell size and wind change everything on this coast. IPMA, Portugal's weather service, publishes a [beach and coastal forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) for the sea along this coast.
- **Learn with a school.** If you want to try surfing, book lessons rather than renting a board and heading for the nearest peak.
- **Respect the line-up.** Local surfers share these waves every day. Take turns, and stay out of the way at the breaks that are beyond your level.

If you are planning a wider beach trip, our [guide to the best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the sea temperature and crowd patterns through the year.

## Walking the old town

Ericeira's centre is compact and best explored on foot. The streets are narrow and cobbled, the houses low and whitewashed, and the trim is that particular Ericeira blue. There is no checklist of monuments here; the pleasure is the wandering, cutting down an alley and suddenly finding the sea at the end of it.

<Pair a="eric-cobbled-street" b="eric-blue-trim" captionA="A cobbled shopping street in the centre, lined with whitewashed houses." captionB="The ornate façade of the Casa de Cultura Jaime Lobo e Silva, in white and blue." />

The clifftop is the town's promenade. Walk it from the fishing beach towards Praia do Sul in the evening and you will understand why so many people who came for a weekend end up staying longer. Ericeira Surf & Skate, a local surf brand, opened its [first shop here in October 1996](https://ericeirasurfskate.pt/en/about-us), right in the heart of the old fishing village, and it now has shops across the country; it is a reminder of how far the surf economy has spread from this small town.

## Eating by the sea

Ericeira is a seafood town first. Visit Lisboa notes that the town's restaurants serve [the freshest fish possible](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/beaches-of-ericeira), and that is the thing to order: whatever came in that morning, grilled simply, with boiled potatoes, salad and a cold glass of white wine.

For a broader picture of what to eat on the coast and how it changes from region to region, see our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

## Where to stay

The town fills up in summer and at weekends, when Lisbon heads for the coast. Stay in the centre if you want to walk everywhere; look north towards Ribeira d'Ilhas if you are mainly here to surf and have a car.

For something with a story, [Vila Galé Ericeira](https://www.vilagale.com/en/hotels-portugal/hotels-lisbon-coast/vila-gale-ericeira/) sits just above the beach. According to the hotel, it is a rehabilitation of the old Ericeira Tourism Hotel, with sea-view rooms and balconies looking over the Atlantic. Elsewhere in town, smaller guesthouses and surf houses are the norm, many of them in converted family homes.

## A side trip to Mafra

A short drive inland, the town of Mafra holds one of the most extraordinary buildings in Portugal. The [National Palace of Mafra](https://www.museusemonumentos.pt/en/museus-e-monumentos/palacio-nacional-de-mafra) is a vast Baroque complex of royal palace, basilica, convent, garden and hunting estate. It was commissioned by King João V in 1711 to fulfil a vow for the birth of an heir, construction began in 1717, and it has been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 2019.

Its library holds around 30,000 volumes in a single grand room, and the building has two carillons and six historical organs. The palace is open Wednesday to Monday and closed on Tuesdays, with last entry before closing time; check the hours and ticket price on the [official page](https://www.museusemonumentos.pt/en/museus-e-monumentos/palacio-nacional-de-mafra). The same page notes that the basilica closed in January 2025 for restoration works expected to last at least a year, so check whether it has reopened before you go.

Combined with Sintra, Mafra makes Ericeira a good base for a few days outside the capital. If you are still deciding how to split your time between cities, our [Lisbon or Porto guide](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) may help.

## Getting there

Ericeira is about half an hour from Lisbon by road, as both [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/beaches-of-ericeira) and the hotels in town point out, though traffic on summer weekends can stretch that.

There is no train. The public option is the bus: Carris Metropolitana runs several lines between Lisbon's Campo Grande interchange and the Ericeira bus terminal, including [line 2801](https://carrismetropolitana.pt/lines/2801), which goes via the A21 and A8 motorways, and [line 2803](https://carrismetropolitana.pt/lines/2803), which goes via Mafra. Campo Grande is on the Lisbon metro, so it is easy to reach from the city centre. Check the timetable for your day, as frequencies drop in the evening and at weekends.

A car makes it easier to hop between beaches and to combine Ericeira with Mafra and the coast further north. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire, tolls and the bus network.

## Before you book

- Decide whether you are here to surf, learn or watch, and choose your base accordingly: the centre for walking, the north of town for the breaks.
- Check the [IPMA coastal forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) before you plan a surf day.
- Book accommodation early for summer and any weekend between June and September.
- If you are going by bus, check the current [Carris Metropolitana timetable](https://carrismetropolitana.pt/lines/2801) for both the outward and return journeys.
- Visiting Mafra? Remember the palace is closed on Tuesdays, and check the [current hours and the basilica's status](https://www.museusemonumentos.pt/en/museus-e-monumentos/palacio-nacional-de-mafra).
