The road climbs out of the green Minho farmland and, within a few bends, the country changes. Fields give way to scrub and pine, then to bare granite domes, smoothed and streaked by weather. Water is everywhere: in roadside channels, in plunge pools the colour of bottle glass, in the sound that follows you along every path. Up here the villages are built of the same stone as the mountains, and some of them still store their maize in granaries raised on stone legs.

This is Peneda-Gerês, in Portugal's far north-west, pressed against the border with Galicia. It is the country's wildest mainland landscape that is still easy to reach, and it rewards anyone who comes for more than an afternoon.

## Why it is a national park

Portugal has many protected areas, but only one carries the title of national park. The Parque Nacional da Peneda-Gerês was created by [Decree 187/71 of 8 May 1971](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/detalhe/decreto/187-1971-644352), and the official tourist guide of the cross-border biosphere reserve describes it as [the only protected area in Portugal to have reached national park status](https://www.reservabiosferageresxures.eu/sites/default/files/2022-12/ingles.pdf).

The same guide gives the park about 70,000 hectares spread across 22 parishes in five municipalities: Melgaço, Arcos de Valdevez, Ponte da Barca, Terras de Bouro and Montalegre. It runs from the Serra da Peneda to the Serra do Gerês, and from the Castro Laboreiro plateau in the north to the Mourela plateau in the east, taking in the Soajo and Amarela ranges on the way, with high points up to 1,545 metres. The rock is mostly granite, with deposits left by glaciers, moraines and erratic boulders among them.

The park also sits inside something larger. Together with the Baixa Limia–Serra do Xurés natural park across the border, it forms the Gerês-Xurés Transboundary Biosphere Reserve, which Spain's environment ministry records was [declared by UNESCO on 27 May 2009](https://www.miteco.gob.es/en/parques-nacionales-oapn/reservas-biosfera-mab/programa-mab-espana/nuestras-reservas-de-la-biosfera/transfronterizageres-xures.html).

## Wildlife and old woods

The reserve's page on [natural values](https://www.reservabiosferageresxures.eu/en/the-reserve/natural-values) lists the wolf, roe deer, the garrano (the small native horse of these hills) and the golden eagle among its fauna, along with bats, martens and vipers. It also names an endemic iris, the Iris boissieri, and points out that the park contains the Matas de Palheiros-Albergaria biogenetic reserve.

The tourist guide adds that the Mata de Albergaria holds a centuries-old oak wood, and that the park counts 246 vertebrate species, among them the Iberian wolf and the wild goat. For birdwatchers, it suggests the Senhora da Peneda and Soajo areas, the Castro Laboreiro plateau, Lindoso, and the area around Portela do Homem and the Mata da Albergaria.

Will you see a wolf? Almost certainly not, and that is as it should be. What you will see are garranos grazing on open slopes, birds of prey turning over the ridges, and long-horned cattle wandering the high pastures with the air of owning them.

## The villages: stone, granaries and summer pastures

Peneda-Gerês is not empty wilderness. People have farmed these mountains for millennia, and the villages are as much the point as the peaks.

**Soajo**, in Arcos de Valdevez, is one of the best known. The [tourist guide](https://www.reservabiosferageresxures.eu/sites/default/files/2022-12/ingles.pdf) describes a set of 24 granaries, or espigueiros, built on a granite outcrop and still used by the community, a pillory possibly from the 17th century, and megalithic remains nearby.

**Lindoso**, in Ponte da Barca, pairs its granaries with a castle. The guide says the castle was rebuilt in 1278 and calls the village's granaries, 64 of them, the largest set in the Iberian Peninsula. Seen from above, they stand in rows below the walls like a small grey town of their own.

<Figure id="pg-lindoso" caption="Rows of granite granaries below Lindoso, with the village and terraced fields behind." />

**Castro Laboreiro**, high in the north in Melgaço, is the most remote. Its castle is classified as a National Monument, and the plateau above 1,100 metres holds what the guide calls the largest megalithic necropolis in mainland Portugal. This is also the place to understand the old transhumance of the mountains: families moved between *inverneiras*, winter settlements sheltered from the north wind, and *brandas*, used in the warmer months for grazing.

Then there is **Vilarinho da Furna**, a communal village at the foot of the Serra Amarela that was submerged in 1971 when a dam was built on the Homem river. When the reservoir runs low, the guide notes, its old walls and houses emerge from the water. Few places in Portugal tell the story of the 20th century's changes to rural life so plainly.

## Walking in Peneda-Gerês

This is walking country first and foremost. Distances on a map look small, but the terrain is steep and rocky, so allow more time than you think.

A few routes listed in the [biosphere reserve's guide](https://www.reservabiosferageresxures.eu/sites/default/files/2022-12/ingles.pdf) give a sense of the range:

| Route | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Trilho da Geira | A 10 km circular route starting and ending at the Capela de São Sebastião da Geira, rated somewhat difficult, about four hours |
| GR crossing the Peneda and Soajo mountains | A 77 km circular route of about four days from the Porta do Mezio, rated medium |
| GR50 Peneda-Gerês National Park | A long-distance route of about 200 km |

The Geira itself is worth knowing about: the guide describes it as a Roman road crossing what are now two countries, with bridges and milestones still marking its line.

If you have walked the [Rota Vicentina](/stories/walking-the-rota-vicentina/) in the south, expect something very different here: fewer sea views, more climbing, more shade under oak and pine, and a great deal more water.

A short list of habits worth keeping:

- Start early. Mountain weather changes quickly, and the light on the granite is best in the morning.
- Carry water and a map, and do not rely on phone signal in the valleys.
- Stay on marked paths. Many of the most photographed pools sit on steep, slippery rock.
- Leave gates as you find them and give livestock room.

## Fire, rules and timing

Wildfire is a real risk in Portugal's hills. UK government [travel advice for Portugal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) notes an increased risk from April to October, when the weather is hot and dry, and warns that starting a fire, even by accident, is illegal and can lead to a fine or prison. Before a walk, check the rural fire danger for your area on [IPMA's fire risk page](https://www.ipma.pt/en/riscoincendio/rcm.pt/), which also shows which activities are restricted. Never light a stove or barbecue outside designated places.

Rules on access to the most sensitive areas can change. The park is managed by the [ICNF](https://www.icnf.pt/), Portugal's nature conservation institute, so check its site and local notices before you set out.

As for timing, late spring brings full rivers and green slopes, and autumn colours the oak woods. High summer is the busiest time at the famous pools and waterfalls. Our month-by-month guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) has more on the north's weather through the year.

## Where to base yourself

Braga and the Minho valleys are the natural approach, and a car makes the park far easier to explore. Our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire and driving.

**Vila do Gerês**, in Terras de Bouro, is the park's busiest base. The tourist guide notes it has a spa of Roman origin and a wide choice of places to stay. On the main avenue, [Águas do Gerês](https://www.aguasdogeres.pt/) is a hotel with its own thermal baths and spa.

For something quieter on the southern edge, the [Pousada Mosteiro Amares](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-amares) occupies a 12th-century Cistercian monastery between Braga and the Serra do Gerês. Pousadas de Portugal notes that its restoration was designed by the architect Eduardo Souto de Moura.

In the north-west of the park, the [Porta do Mezio](https://portadomezio.pt/) in Arcos de Valdevez is one of the park's gateways, with a biological park, walking trails and a stargazing area. Its website lists seasonal opening hours and closing days.

If you are coming from Porto for a day, keep your expectations modest: mountain roads are slow, and the park is best with at least one night. Combine it with a few days in the city, perhaps starting with our walk among [Porto's azulejos](/stories/porto-azulejos/).

## Before you book

- Plan on driving, or on a guided trip, and allow plenty of time for winding mountain roads.
- Check [IPMA's fire danger page](https://www.ipma.pt/en/riscoincendio/rcm.pt/) on the day you walk, and follow any restrictions it shows.
- Look up current access conditions on the [ICNF site](https://www.icnf.pt/) before visiting the Mata da Albergaria and other sensitive areas.
- Book accommodation early for summer weekends, when Vila do Gerês and the river pools are at their busiest.
- Pack layers and rain gear in every season; the mountains catch Atlantic weather.
