You sit down, and before you have opened the menu a basket of bread appears, with a saucer of olives, a few pats of butter and perhaps a little dish of fresh cheese. Nobody has asked whether you want them. The waiter has already moved on. This is the first small puzzle of eating out in Portugal, and the answer, as with most of them, is simple once you know it.

Eating out here is relaxed, generous and rarely complicated, but it runs on its own habits: when people eat, what the different kinds of place are for, how menus are read and how the bill is paid. This guide covers the things we wish someone had told us on our first trip.

## The rhythm of the day

Breakfast is light and quick: a coffee and a pastry, often standing at a café counter. Lunch is the main meal for many people, eaten from around half past twelve, and on weekdays it is when you will find the best value, with set menus and dishes of the day aimed at people on a work break. Dinner starts later than in northern Europe; restaurants fill from about eight, later in summer and at weekends.

In between there is the *lanche*, a mid-afternoon snack of coffee and something sweet or a small sandwich. It is a good habit to adopt on long sightseeing days.

Many smaller restaurants close between lunch and dinner, and many close one day a week, so check before you set out across town for a particular place.

## Know your café from your cervejaria

The names on the door tell you what to expect.

| Name | What it is |
|---|---|
| Café | Coffee, drinks, snacks and often simple meals; a neighbourhood fixture |
| Pastelaria / confeitaria | Pastry shop and café, with cakes and savoury snacks; many serve lunch |
| Tasca / taberna | A small, informal place with home cooking, short menus and petiscos |
| Restaurante | A full restaurant, from simple to formal |
| Marisqueira | A seafood restaurant, with shellfish often sold by weight |
| Cervejaria | A beer hall, typically with steak, seafood and snacks |
| Churrasqueira | A grill house, often specialising in charcoal-grilled chicken |

Some of the most atmospheric places to eat are the old cafés and beer halls. In Porto, the **[Majestic Café](https://www.cafemajestic.com/pt/Utilidades/Homepage.aspx)** on Rua de Santa Catarina keeps its Belle Époque room and lists its hours as Monday to Saturday from 9am to 11pm. Not far away on Rua Formosa, **[Confeitaria do Bolhão](https://www.confeitariadobolhao.com/en/)** says that when it was founded, Porto's well-to-do took breakfast there before shopping at the Bolhão market; today it bakes its own bread and pastries, and has a restaurant with à la carte and daily menus.

<Figure id="dine-majestic" caption="The carved façade and red doors of the Majestic Café on Rua de Santa Catarina, Porto." />

In Lisbon, **[Cervejaria Trindade](https://www.cervejariatrindade.pt/)**, on Rua Nova da Trindade in the Chiado, occupies a former convent; the house describes itself as the oldest beer hall in Portugal, notes that the building survived the earthquake and two fires, and centres its menu on steak, seafood and petiscos. For shellfish, **[Cervejaria Ramiro](https://www.cervejariaramiro.com/hist%C3%B3ria)** on Avenida Almirante Reis was founded in 1956 and, by its own account, gradually turned from a small eating house into a seafood restaurant. Our guide to [Portuguese seafood](/stories/portuguese-seafood/) explains what to order in places like these.

## The couvert

Back to the bread basket. The bread, butter, olives, cheese or pâté that arrive at the start of the meal are known as the *couvert*, and they are not free. The good news is that you only pay for what you choose.

Portugal's legal framework for restaurants, [Decreto-Lei n.º 10/2015](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/legislacao-consolidada/decreto-lei/2015-73045620), states that no dish, food or drink, including the couvert, may be charged if the customer did not order it or did not use it. In practice:

- If you want it, eat it, and it will appear on the bill, usually priced per item or per person on the menu.
- If you do not, say so straight away, *não, obrigado* (or *obrigada*), and ask for it to be taken away.
- If you eat part of it, expect to pay for that part. Opened butter or cheese counts as used.

Nobody will be offended either way; sending it back is perfectly normal.

## Reading the menu

A few words make menus much easier.

- **Prato do dia.** The dish of the day, usually written on a board or a slip clipped to the menu. It is often the best-value and freshest thing on offer.
- **Menu do dia / menu executivo.** A weekday set lunch, often soup, a main course, a drink and coffee or dessert.
- **Dose and meia dose.** A *dose* is a full portion, and Portuguese portions are generous. Ask whether a *meia dose* (half portion) is available; two half portions can be a good way to share.
- **Petiscos.** Small plates for sharing, from fried pastries to clams, octopus salad or grilled chouriço.
- **Preço ao quilo / kg.** Fish and shellfish are often priced by the kilo. Ask the waiter to weigh your choice, or to tell you the price of the portion, before it goes on the grill.
- **Sopa.** The soup of the day is usually inexpensive and filling; Portuguese meals very often begin with it.

If you have dietary needs, say so early. Pork and seafood turn up in many dishes, including some you would not expect; our guide to [eating vegetarian and vegan in Portugal](/stories/vegetarian-in-portugal/) covers the questions to ask.

## Drinks and coffee

House wine, *vinho da casa*, is often sold by the jug or the half-jug and is usually perfectly good. Water is ordered as *água*, specifying *natural* (room temperature) or *fresca* (chilled), and *com gás* if you want it sparkling. In the north, look out for vinho verde, light and fresh, which is the natural partner to grilled fish.

Coffee comes at the end of the meal, and almost nobody drinks it milky after lunch. The basic order is an espresso, called a *bica* in Lisbon and a *cimbalino* in Porto, or simply *um café* anywhere. A *galão* is a tall, milky coffee served in a glass, more a breakfast drink. Our guide to [Portugal's pastries and sweets](/stories/portuguese-pastries/) suggests what to eat with it.

## Paying the bill

In most sit-down places the bill does not arrive until you ask for it, and nobody will hurry you. Catch the waiter's eye and ask for *a conta, por favor*. At a café counter, you usually pay as you leave.

You will often be asked whether you want your tax number on the receipt (*com contribuinte?*). Visitors can simply say no.

Cards are widely accepted in cities, and food halls such as [Time Out Market Lisboa](https://www.timeout.com/time-out-market-lisboa), which our guide to [Lisbon's markets](/stories/lisbon-markets/) covers in detail, take both cards and cash. In small tascas, village cafés and at market stalls, carry some cash in case.

Tipping is a matter of choice rather than obligation. Many people round up the bill or leave a few coins at a café, and leave a little more for good service at a restaurant. Check whether a service charge has already been added, which is uncommon in ordinary restaurants.

## Reservations, children and complaints

Book ahead for well-known restaurants, for Friday and Saturday dinners and for Sunday lunch, when families eat out together. Simple tascas often do not take bookings: arrive early, or put your name down and wait.

Children are welcome almost everywhere, including at dinner, and kitchens are usually happy to make a smaller portion or something plain.

If something goes seriously wrong and cannot be resolved on the spot, Portugal has an official complaints book, the *Livro de Reclamações*, which you can ask for at the restaurant or submit online through the [electronic complaints book](https://www.livroreclamacoes.pt/Inicio/). Most problems, though, are best solved with a polite word to the waiter.

## Before you book

- Plan big meals for lunchtime on weekdays, when set menus and dishes of the day give the best value.
- Decide about the couvert as soon as it arrives: keep it and pay for it, or send it back.
- Ask the price of fish and shellfish sold by the kilo before it is cooked.
- Note any dietary needs in Portuguese before you travel.
- Check opening days and book ahead for popular restaurants, especially at weekends.
- Carry some cash for small cafés, tascas and markets.
