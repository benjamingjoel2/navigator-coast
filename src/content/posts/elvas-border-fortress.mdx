Drive towards Elvas from the west and the town announces itself slowly. First the aqueduct, striding across the fields on stacked arches. Then the walls, low and angled and grassed over, folding around the hill in a pattern that only really makes sense from the air: a star. Up on the heights to the north sits a second fortress, and on another hill to the south a third. Spain is a short drive away, and for centuries that was the whole point.

Elvas is not a pretty-village stop in the way Monsaraz or Marvão are. It is a working market town with a military soul, and its pleasures are slightly austere: ramparts in the late sun, a cathedral square of whitewash and stone, a plate of salt cod turned gold. Give it a day, or a night if you can, and it rewards you far beyond its fame.

## Why Elvas matters

UNESCO added the town to its World Heritage list in June 2012 as the Garrison Border Town of Elvas and its Fortifications. CP's [page on Elvas](https://www.cp.pt/info/w/elvas-circuit) sums up why: the town sits about 8 km from Badajoz, it was heavily fortified between the 17th and 19th centuries, and it guarded the main border crossing on the road between Lisbon and Madrid. The listing covers the historic centre, the forts of Santa Luzia and Graça, the Amoreira aqueduct and three smaller 19th-century forts.

The regional tourism board's [World Heritage page](https://www.visitalentejo.pt/en/alentejo/culture/unesco-world-heritage/world-heritage-list/) adds the human scale: narrow, cobbled streets, a compact cathedral, and the drama of the castle, forts and the 7 km aqueduct.

## The walls: a walk around the star

Start with the circuit of the walls, because everything else in Elvas hangs off it. The municipality's [notes on the 17th-century walls](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/patrimonio/militar/muralhas-seiscentistas/) explain that they belong to the early Dutch school of military architecture: seven bastions, four half-bastions and a redan, joined by curtain walls into twelve fronts. They were built during the Restoration War of 1641 to 1668, when Portugal was fighting to secure its independence from Spain and the town's medieval walls were too vertical to survive artillery. The Jesuit engineer Cosmander was sent to design something better.

You still enter the old town through the same three double gates, the Portas de Olivença, de São Vicente and da Esquina. Walk out through one and along the dry ditch for a while, and you start to see how the geometry works: every stretch of wall is covered by fire from another.

## Forte da Graça and Forte de Santa Luzia

Of the outlying forts, the **Forte da Graça** is the showpiece. According to [the municipality](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/forte-da-graca/), work began in 1763 under Wilhelm, Count of Schaumburg-Lippe, who had been asked by King José to reorganise the Portuguese army, and ended in 1792. It is built in three layers, outer works, main body and central redoubt, and later served as a prison: from 1894 a disciplinary depot held political prisoners from the First Republic until 1974. After an eleven-month restoration it reopened in November 2015, with the governor's house, chapel and firing galleries brought back to their original colours. It is closed on Mondays; the same page lists the seasonal hours, guided visits and the special visits to the cistern and countermines, which run only a few times a year and need booking.

The **Forte de Santa Luzia** is older and closer, on a rise a few hundred metres south of the walls. The municipality's [page on the fort](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/patrimonio/militar/forte-de-santa-luzia/) dates it to 1641 to 1648 and credits it with a pivotal role in the Battle of the Lines of Elvas in 1659. Since works in 1999 and 2000 it has housed a [military museum](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/cultura/museus/museu-militar-forte-de-santa-luzia/) on the city's long military history, open Tuesday to Sunday.

## The aqueduct and the cistern

Elvas had a water problem for centuries. Wells inside the walls were not enough, and in wartime the springs outside could not be reached. The [Amoreira aqueduct](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/patrimonio/civil/aqueduto-da-amoreira/) was the answer, paid for by a tax on consumer goods. The municipality says it starts in underground galleries 1,367 metres long, then runs on arcades for more than five and a half kilometres, in places over 30 metres high. Francisco de Arruda directed the work, and water finally reached the Fonte da Misericórdia in 1622.

The best views are from the western edge of town in the evening, when the arches throw long shadows over the fields. Inside the walls, look for the great [bomb-proof cistern](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/patrimonio/militar/cisterna/) built in 1650 to store the aqueduct's water against a siege, with a fountain and marble spouts outside.

## The old town

Inside the walls, Elvas is all slopes and whitewash. The castle area is the oldest part of town, and from the main square the lanes climb towards it past churches and small museums. Take your time over the details: carved doorways, iron balconies, the yellow and white trim that runs through so much of the Alentejo.

## What to eat

The municipality's [food page](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/gastronomia/) is proud and specific. The town's signature dish is **bacalhau dourado**, shredded salt cod bound with egg and fine fried potato until it turns gold. It was born in the kitchen of the old Pousada de Santa Luzia, which opened in April 1942 as the first pousada in Portugal. Dessert is **sericaia** (also spelled cericaia or cericá), a baked custard of milk, eggs, sugar and flour dusted with cinnamon, usually served with the town's preserved **Ameixas d'Elvas** plums. For the wider picture of the region's bread, pork and wine, see our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

Two places listed by the municipality are a sensible starting point:

- **1942**, the restaurant at SL Hotel Santa Luzia on Avenida de Badajoz, occupies the former pousada building and lists [bacalhau dourado among its specialities](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/onde-comer/1942-sl-hotel-santa-luzia/).
- **Adega Regional**, on Rua João de Casqueiro inside the walls, is listed for [traditional Alentejo cooking](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/onde-comer/adega-regional/), with seafood rice and home-made desserts. Check the closing days on the listing before you go.

## Where to stay

- **Vila Galé Collection Elvas** sits in the historic centre in the former São Paulo convent. The hotel's [own description](https://www.vilagale.com/en/hotels-portugal/hotels-alentejo/vila-gale-collection-elvas/) notes that after the religious orders were abolished the building served as a military court, barracks and detention house, and that the hotel is themed on Portuguese fortifications around the world.
- **SL Hotel Santa Luzia**, just outside the walls on Avenida de Badajoz, is in the building of the old [Pousada de Santa Luzia](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/onde-ficar/sl-hotel-santa-luzia/), per the municipal listing.
- **Hotel São João de Deus** is on Largo São João de Deus, by the former military hospital and convent; the [municipal listing](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/onde-ficar/hotel-sao-joao-de-deus/) has its details.

## Getting there

Elvas is on the railway. CP's [Raiano train](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/raiano) runs daily in both directions between Entroncamento and Badajoz, through Portalegre and Elvas, with connections at Entroncamento for Lisbon and Porto. There are only a few services a day, so plan around the timetable on that page.

Most visitors drive, often combining Elvas with Évora, Estremoz and Vila Viçosa. Our [five-day Alentejo road trip](/stories/alentejo-road-trip/) builds Elvas into a loop, and our [Évora guide](/stories/evora-guide/) covers the obvious base to the west. For tolls and the motorway network, see our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

## Before you book

- Check opening days: both the [Forte da Graça](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/forte-da-graca/) and the [Santa Luzia military museum](https://cm-elvas.pt/descobrir/cultura/museus/museu-militar-forte-de-santa-luzia/) close on Mondays.
- If you want to see the Graça's cistern and countermines, ask well ahead; those visits run only a few times a year.
- Arriving by train, confirm the current [Raiano timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/raiano) and leave a margin for the return.
- Bring flat shoes for the cobbles and slopes, and water and a hat for the walls in summer.
- If you drive and plan to taste wine nearby, remember Portugal's limit is 0.5 g/l for most drivers, per the EU's [Your Europe road rules page](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/road-rules-and-safety/portugal/index_en.htm).
