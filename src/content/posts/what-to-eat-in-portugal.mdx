The bread arrives before the menu does. A basket, a saucer of olives, perhaps a little cheese, and you have not yet said a word. That is the first lesson of eating in Portugal: the table is already talking. The second is that the country changes its accent every couple of hours on the road, and the food changes with it. Cured pork gives way to clams, clams to sheep's cheese, cheese to sugar-cane spirit and stew cooked in volcanic earth.

We have organised this guide the way most of us travel: region by region, with the dishes, the protected names worth looking for, and the wines that belong beside them.

## The North: Porto and the Minho

Porto eats like a city that works hard. Its two signature plates are unapologetically heavy, and locals will defend both.

- **Francesinha.** A tower of bread, cured and fresh meats and steak, sealed under melted cheese and drowned in a spiced, beer-and-tomato sauce, usually with chips. It is a lunch that decides your afternoon for you.
- **Tripas à moda do Porto.** Tripe slow-cooked with white beans and assorted meats. The dish is so bound up with the city that its people are still nicknamed *tripeiros*, the tripe-eaters.
- **Caldo verde.** The north's gift to the whole country: a potato broth thick with ribbons of finely shredded kale, a coin of chouriço floating on top and a thread of olive oil. You will find it everywhere, from tascas to festival stalls.

To drink, look north to the Minho and **Vinho Verde**. The name means "green wine" in the sense of young, not colour, and the classic bottle is a fresh, light, often faintly spritzy white that suits a plate of grilled fish on a warm evening.

## The Douro: Port country

Upriver from Porto, the Douro valley is terraced into schist hillsides so steep they seem carved rather than planted. This is where Port is made. The [IVDP](https://www.ivdp.pt/en), the body that regulates the wine, describes the Douro as the oldest demarcated and regulated wine region in the world, dating from 1756, and Port as a protected designation of origin produced there.

Port is not one wine but a family. The IVDP's [special categories](https://www.ivdp.pt/en/wines/port-wines/special-categories/) are worth learning before you sit down to a tasting:

| Style | What it means |
|---|---|
| Ruby Reserve | A blend of good young Port, fruity and intense |
| Tawny 10, 20, 30, 40 years old | Aged in wood, gaining nutty, dried-fruit and spice notes |
| Colheita | A single-vintage tawny, aged in cask for at least seven years |
| LBV (Late Bottled Vintage) | A ruby from one year, aged in wood before bottling |
| Vintage | From a single harvest; the only Port that ages in bottle |

A chilled white Port with tonic is the local aperitif of choice on a hot terrace; an old tawny at the end of a meal needs nothing at all.

## Centro: pork, sweets and mountain cheese

The Centro is easy to underrate and a pleasure to eat through.

**Leitão da Bairrada** is the region's showpiece: suckling pig roasted whole in a wood-fired oven until the skin shatters, then served with potatoes, orange slices and a glass of the local sparkling wine. The towns along the old road between Coimbra and Aveiro have built their reputations on it.

On the coast, Aveiro is famous for **ovos moles**, a sweet of egg yolk and sugar wrapped in thin wafer moulded into shells, fish and barrels. Its full name, Ovos Moles de Aveiro, is registered in the EU as a [Protected Geographical Indication](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013759), so the name on the box carries a guarantee of where and how it was made.

Climb inland to the Serra da Estrela, Portugal's highest mainland range, and the treat is cheese. **Queijo Serra da Estrela** is a sheep's-milk cheese, registered as a [Protected Designation of Origin](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013223). At its ripest the interior turns soft enough to spoon from beneath the rind; at the table it is often served with the top sliced off like a lid.

## Lisbon: custard, cherries and sardine smoke

Lisbon's most famous mouthful needs no introduction. The **pastel de nata**, a crisp, layered pastry shell holding a just-set custard scorched at the top, is, in Visit Lisboa's words, the city's most famous cake. Its [official guide](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisbon-flavours/specialties/pastel-de-nata) recommends eating it warm, dusted with cinnamon, ideally with a bica (espresso), and notes the long queues in Belém for the original *pastéis de Belém*, which come from the convent-sweet tradition.

Beyond the custard tarts:

- **Bifana.** A thin pork steak marinated with garlic and wine, simmered and stuffed into a soft roll, eaten standing at a counter with a dab of mustard.
- **Ginjinha.** A sweet liqueur of sour cherries, poured into a small glass (sometimes with a cherry in it, sometimes without) at hole-in-the-wall bars around Rossio.
- **Grilled sardines in June.** This is when Lisbon smells most like itself. During the celebrations for Saint Anthony, the city's patron saint, the neighbourhood street parties fill with charcoal smoke, and [Visit Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/events/st-anthonys-night-12-13th-june) calls grilled sardines an essential feature of the main night, 12 to 13 June. Eat them on a slice of bread that catches the juices.

## Alentejo: bread, pork and big red wines

The Alentejo kitchen was built on scarcity, and it turned stale bread and a pig into something close to genius.

**Porco preto**, the black pig of the Alentejo, forages under cork and holm oaks and gives rich, marbled meat. The fresh meat of the Alentejano breed is protected as [Carne de Porco Alentejano PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013604). Look for cuts such as *secretos* and *plumas* grilled simply over coals.

Bread does the rest:

- **Açorda** is bread soaked in a garlicky broth fragrant with coriander and olive oil, usually crowned with a poached egg. Simple, and quietly perfect.
- **Migas** are fried breadcrumbs pressed into a soft, savoury mass, the classic partner for fried pork.

Alentejo wine country rolls across the plains around Évora, Reguengos and Borba, and its reds tend to be generous and sun-ripened. Order a jug of the house wine at a local tasca and you will rarely be disappointed.

## Algarve: the sea, and the dry orchard

Away from the beach strips, Algarve cooking is two kitchens at once: the sea and the dry hills behind it.

The signature dish is the **cataplana**, named after the hinged copper pan it is cooked in. Turismo do Algarve's [cuisine guide](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf) explains that its two clamshell halves seal so the food steams evenly with little oil, that most versions start with tomato, onion, coriander and pepper, and that over 40 recipes have been recorded in the region. Clams, on their own or with pork and chouriço, are the classic filling.

The same guide highlights the shellfish of the coast, including clams, cockles, razor clams and oysters, and the Ria Formosa lagoon near Faro and Olhão as one of the most prized sources. Then there is what the guide calls the "Algarve trilogy" of dried fruits: **almonds, figs and carob**. Almond paste is shaped into tiny fruits and animals in pastry-shop windows; dried figs become fig "cheese"; carob flour turns up in breads and cakes with a gentle, cocoa-like flavour.

## Madeira: skewers, flatbread and fortified wine

Madeira's island cooking is festive and smoky. [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/food-and-wine-enthusiasts/traditional-madeira-food/typical-dishes/) calls **espetada** an iconic dish: chunks of beef grilled on a bay-laurel skewer, in some places hung from a hook over the table, and commonly served with cubes of fried cornmeal. Alongside comes **bolo do caco**, a round flatbread served warm and spread with garlic butter.

To drink, try **poncha**, the island's punch of sugar-cane spirit, honey and lemon, stirred hard with a wooden stick.

Then there is **Madeira wine**, fortified and famously long-lived. The island's wine institute, IVBAM, [explains](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/vinificacao-e-envelhecimento) that fermentation is stopped by adding grape spirit, and the timing sets the sweetness, from dry to sweet. The wines then age in one of two ways: *estufagem*, where the wine is warmed to 45–50°C in tanks for at least three months, or *canteiro*, where it ages in casks, usually in warm upper floors, for at least two years. Ask which method made your glass; it changes everything.

## Azores: food from the volcano

On São Miguel, lunch is sometimes dug out of the ground. **Cozido das Furnas** is a stew of meats, sausages and vegetables layered in a pot that is lowered into earth heated by volcanic steam in the Furnas valley. [Taste Azores](https://taste.visitazores.com/en/recipe/cozido-furnas) notes that it cooks for about six hours and that the only liquid comes from the ingredients themselves. Expect beef, pork, chicken, blood sausage, chouriço, cabbage, potatoes, sweet potatoes and yam.

From the island of São Jorge comes a firm, sharp cow's-milk cheese registered as [Queijo S. Jorge PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013102). It is excellent with a glass of local wine and a slice of sweet bread.

São Miguel also grows pineapples under glass, small and intensely perfumed, and it is home to tea. On the island's north coast, the plantation at Gorreana has its terraced rows of tea bushes running down towards the Atlantic, and a cup of the local green or black tea there, looking at the ocean, is a fine way to end a day.

## Eating customs worth knowing

A few habits will make every meal smoother.

- **The couvert.** The bread, butter, olives, cheese or pâté placed on the table as you sit down are not free. They are generally charged if you eat them. If you would rather not, simply say so and ask for them to be taken away before you start.
- **Order the dish of the day.** Ask for the *prato do dia*; it is usually what the kitchen is proudest of that morning.
- **Coffee is a vocabulary.** A *bica* in Lisbon, a *cimbalino* in Porto, an espresso everywhere. Ask for a *galão* if you want it milky, in a glass.
- **Half portions.** Ask whether a *meia dose* is available; Portuguese servings are generous.

## Before you book

- Check the dates of the festivals you want to eat your way through, such as Lisbon's June celebrations, on the official tourism sites before fixing your travel.
- If you are planning Port lodge or winery visits, check opening days and whether advance booking is required directly with the estate before you travel.
- Look for the PDO and PGI seals on cheese, sweets and charcuterie you plan to bring home, and check your home country's rules on importing dairy and meat products.
- Note any dietary needs in Portuguese before you travel; pork and seafood turn up in more dishes than you might expect.
- Leave room in your itinerary for markets. A morning at a municipal market is the quickest way into any region's kitchen.
