Look up as you pass under the gateway into Faro's old town and there is a stork looking back at you. It stands on a ragged nest of sticks on the bell tower above the arch, unbothered by the traffic of the square below, clattering its beak now and then like a pair of castanets. Inside the walls the noise drops away. Cobbled lanes run between whitewashed houses, orange trees shade the cathedral square, and every so often a gap opens onto the flat, shining water of the lagoon.

<Figure id="faro-arco-bell-tower" caption="The bell tower above the Arco da Vila, the main gateway into Faro's old town, with a stork's nest beside it." />

Faro is the capital of the Algarve and, for most visitors, the name on a boarding pass and little more. People land, collect a hire car and head west to the beaches. That is a shame, because the city has one of the best-preserved old quarters in the south, a cathedral with a long and turbulent history, and the Ria Formosa lagoon on its doorstep. It is also, unlike much of the coast, a working city with its own life all year round.

## The walled town

The old town, known as the Vila Adentro (literally the town within), sits on a low rise above the water, ringed by walls. The grandest way in is through the Arco da Vila, a stately gateway on the harbour side, topped by its bell tower. Once you are through, the scale shrinks to quiet lanes, small squares and low houses with tiled doorsteps.

<Pair a="faro-old-town-arch" b="faro-old-town-street" captionA="A cobbled lane passing through the old walls." captionB="Iron lamps and a church dome at the end of a quiet street." />

It is a small area and you can walk across it in ten minutes. The pleasure is in doing it slowly: tracing the line of the walls, peering into courtyards, stopping in the cathedral square as the light shifts on the façades. In the late afternoon the stone turns honey-coloured and the swifts and swallows come out over the roofs.

## The cathedral

The Sé, Faro's cathedral, stands at the heart of the walled town, and its story is bound up with the region's. The Diocese of the Algarve's [historical note](https://diocese-algarve.pt/nota-historica/) explains that after the Christian conquest the bishopric was established at Silves, then the region's principal city. As Silves declined and the coast grew in importance, the bishop asked in 1538 for the cathedral to be transferred to Faro. The Pope agreed the following year, but the move only took effect on 30 March 1577.

The building reflects that long history, with work of several periods layered together inside. Climb the tower if it is open when you visit: the view takes in the red roofs of the old town, the salt marsh and, on a clear day, the line of the barrier islands out towards the sea. Opening hours and ticketing change, so check on the spot.

A short walk outside the walls, the Igreja do Carmo is known for its Capela dos Ossos, a small chapel whose walls are lined with human bones and skulls. It is a sobering place, and perhaps not one for small children.

## Storks, rooftops and the city beyond the walls

Faro's storks are part of its personality. Nests sit on bell towers, chimneys and rooftops across the centre, and in spring the birds are busy raising their young. Please do not try to get too close; binoculars or a long lens will give you a far better view than a scramble up a wall.

<Figure id="faro-palacete" size="column" caption="An ornate mansion with a turret and iron balconies, a reminder of Faro's grander townhouses." />

Outside the walls, the city spreads out in pedestrian streets lined with shops, cafés and pastry counters, and the occasional extravagant townhouse with a turret, iron balconies and painted stucco. The [Mercado Municipal de Faro](https://www.mercadomunicipaldefaro.pt/), on Largo Doutor Francisco Sá Carneiro, is the city's market hall and a good place to see what is in season. The Algarve's tourism board, in its [guide to regional cooking](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf), singles out the Ria Formosa near Faro and Olhão as one of the coast's most prized sources of clams, cockles, razor clams and oysters, so shellfish is the thing to look for. Our [guide to Portuguese seafood](/stories/portuguese-seafood/) will help you read the menu.

If you are travelling with children, or it rains, the [Centro Ciência Viva do Algarve](http://www.ccvalg.pt/web/) on Rua Comandante Francisco Manuel is a hands-on science centre whose own site describes aquariums dedicated to the Ria Formosa and to tropical ecosystems.

## The lagoon on the doorstep

The reason to linger in Faro is the water. The city looks out over the Ria Formosa, a lagoon of channels, mudflats, salt pans and sandy islands protected as a natural park. The park guide published by the nature authority ICNF with Turismo do Algarve describes it as stretching [60 km along the coast](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467), sheltered from the open sea by a chain of barrier islands and sandy peninsulas.

<Figure id="faro-marina" caption="Faro's small marina, at the edge of the old town, where the city meets the lagoon." />

The marina at the foot of the old town is where many boat trips into the lagoon depart. Ilha Deserta, also called Ilha da Barreta, is reached by boat from Faro, and the ICNF guide notes that it holds Cabo de Santa Maria, the southernmost point of mainland Portugal, along with a short [boardwalk trail](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467) across the dunes. Our guide to [the Ria Formosa islands](/stories/ria-formosa-islands/) covers Deserta, Culatra and Armona in detail.

You do not need a boat to see the lagoon at its best. The same ICNF guide lists the Ludo trails, west of the city, which cross salt pans and marsh where flamingos can be seen at certain times of year, along with herons, egrets and waders. Winter is the richest season for birds, as our guide to [the Algarve in winter](/stories/algarve-in-winter/) explains.

Faro's own beach, Praia de Faro, lies out on the Ancão peninsula, a long sandy spit on the ocean side of the lagoon, joined to the mainland by a bridge. On the sea side, the usual Atlantic rules apply: the UK government's [safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) notes that supervised beaches use a flag system, that a red flag means you must not enter the water, and that some beaches have a strong undertow.

## Estoi: a palace and a Roman villa

A short drive inland, the village of Estoi makes an easy half-day trip. The [Pousada Palácio Estoi](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-estoi) occupies a restored 18th-century palace with French-style gardens; its own description notes that the reception is in the old chapel and that the restoration kept the palace's original features.

The pousada's page also points visitors to the Roman villa of Milreu, about 8 km from Faro and just west of Estoi, where the remains include mosaics and marble and ceramic finishes. Seeing the two together, a Roman country house and an 18th-century aristocrat's palace a few minutes apart, is a neat lesson in how long people have chosen to live well in this corner of the Algarve.

## How long to stay

One full day covers the old town, the cathedral and a walk along the waterfront. Two days lets you add a boat trip to the islands or a morning on the Ludo trails, and an afternoon in Estoi. Faro also works well as a base for the eastern Algarve without a car: it sits on CP's [Algarve line](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line), which runs from Vila Real de Santo António to Lagos, with regional trains running hourly between Faro and Vila Real de Santo António and every two hours between Faro and Lagos, according to CP. Olhão and Tavira are both on the line; our [Tavira guide](/stories/tavira-guide/) picks up the story further east.

## Before you book

- Allow at least one night in Faro rather than treating it as a transit stop; the old town is at its best early and late in the day.
- Check cathedral and church opening hours on arrival, as they vary through the year.
- Book boat trips into the lagoon with the tide and weather in mind, and confirm return times before you set off.
- If you plan to use the train, check the current Algarve line timetable on [CP's site](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line).
- Bring binoculars for the storks and lagoon birds, and keep a respectful distance from nests.
- If you want to stay in Estoi rather than the city, book early for the summer months.
