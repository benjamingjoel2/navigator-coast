The beach is wide and golden, the water a bright, inviting blue, and the waves look gentle from the top of the steps. Down on the sand, a lifeguard has planted a flag. Most visitors glance at it and carry on. It is worth more than a glance.

Portugal is a relaxed, welcoming place to travel, and most trips pass without a hitch. The risks that do exist are mostly predictable: pickpockets where crowds gather, an Atlantic that is more powerful than it looks, cliffs that crumble, and, in the hot months, wildfire. A little knowledge handles nearly all of them. This guide draws heavily on GOV.UK's [Portugal travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security), which is detailed and regularly updated, and on Portugal's own weather and civil protection services.

## Pickpockets and distraction tricks

Petty theft is the crime visitors are most likely to meet. GOV.UK warns that thieves use distraction techniques and often work in teams, and advises particular care on public transport and at crowded bus and tram stops. In Lisbon, that means the packed historic trams and funiculars, the viewpoints, and the busiest streets of the Baixa and Alfama. In Porto, the riverside at Ribeira and the streets around São Bento station draw the same crowds.

The tricks themselves are old ones. Someone asks for directions with a map held over your bag, or "helps" you clean something spilled on your jacket, or a small group crowds you in a doorway while a hand works your pocket. None of this is unique to Portugal, and the defences are the usual ones:

- Keep your phone and wallet in a front pocket or a zipped bag worn in front of you, especially on trams.
- GOV.UK advises not carrying all your valuables in one place. Split cards and cash, and leave a spare card at your accommodation.
- Keep a copy of your passport's photo page separately from the passport itself.
- Do not hang bags on the back of a café chair or leave phones on outdoor tables.
- If someone is unusually keen to help, step back and keep a hand on your belongings.

For card safety, use cash machines inside bank branches where you can, cover the keypad, and keep your card in sight when paying. Our guide to [money and payments in Portugal](/stories/money-and-payments-in-portugal/) covers cards, cash and fees in more detail.

## Nightlife, drinks and drugs

GOV.UK notes that drinks served in Portuguese bars are often stronger than visitors from the UK may expect, and advises buying your own drinks and keeping them in sight to avoid spiking. It also says that attacks, including sexual assaults, are rare but do happen, with the risk highest late at night around popular nightlife areas. Stay with friends, plan how you will get home before you go out, and use licensed taxis or ride-hailing apps rather than accepting lifts.

If someone offers you drugs in the street, keep walking. GOV.UK is blunt that illegal drugs, including cannabis, carry severe penalties in Portugal.

Some resorts have their own rules on behaviour. GOV.UK reports that the municipality of Albufeira has introduced a Code of Conduct banning inappropriate behaviour in public places, with on-the-spot fines for breaking it. If you are heading to the Algarve's busiest party towns, take it seriously.

## The Atlantic: flags, currents and rips

Portugal's beaches are one of its great pleasures, and the sea is the biggest real risk most visitors face. GOV.UK states plainly that every year people drown in the sea and in swimming pools in Portugal. The west coast in particular is open Atlantic, with powerful waves and strong currents even on sunny days.

Learn the flags before you swim. According to GOV.UK, a red flag means you must not enter the water, and a yellow flag means it is only safe to paddle at the water's edge. On supervised beaches, swim within the area the lifeguards are watching, and if no lifeguard is on duty, treat the sea with extra respect.

<Pair a="saf-barra-lifebuoy" b="saf-sao-lourenco-rescue-board" captionA="A lifebuoy on its post at Praia da Barra, near Aveiro." captionB="A lifeguard's rescue board on the sand at Praia de São Lourenço, near Ericeira." />

If you are caught in a rip current, GOV.UK's advice is not to swim against it. Swim parallel to the shore until the pull eases, then head back in. Other hazards it lists are hidden rocks and sudden shallows, and the strong currents where rivers meet the sea, which are best avoided for swimming. GOV.UK also warns against walking close to the water's edge on unsupervised stretches of beach, where a single large wave can knock you over.

Children need constant supervision near water. GOV.UK's advice is to keep small children within arm's reach at pools, even when there is a lifeguard. Our guide to the [Algarve's beaches](/stories/algarve-beaches/) can help you pick sheltered coves, and if you want to see the ocean at its most dramatic, watch it from a safe vantage point, as we describe in our guide to [Nazaré's big waves](/stories/nazare-big-waves/).

## Cliffs and coastal paths

The golden sandstone cliffs of the Algarve are spectacular and fragile. GOV.UK warns that falling rocks are a hazard, particularly in the Algarve, and that the authorities can fine people who ignore warning signs about cliff erosion. Do not sit or lay your towel directly beneath a cliff face, keep well back from edges on clifftop paths, and never cross barriers for a better photo.

## Wildfires

Wildfire is the hazard that defines a Portuguese summer inland. GOV.UK says there is an increased risk of wildfires from April to October, that wildfires are highly dangerous and unpredictable, and that starting a fire, even by accident, is illegal and can bring a fine or a prison sentence. If you see a fire, call 112.

The national weather institute, IPMA, publishes a daily [rural fire danger map](https://www.ipma.pt/en/riscoincendio/rcm.pt/) by municipality. Select a district and county, and it shows which activities, such as barbecues, burning, the use of machinery and fireworks, are allowed, restricted or prohibited at the current level of danger. Check it before a picnic, a camping trip or a walk in the hills.

Practical habits help. Do not throw cigarette ends from car windows, avoid lighting anything outdoors in the hot months, and follow the instructions of the GNR and fire services if roads are closed. If you are hiking or driving in forested areas on a high-risk day, tell someone your route and keep an eye on the sky for smoke.

## Weather warnings and storms

Portugal's weather is mostly kind, but it can turn. IPMA's [weather warnings page](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/) shows current warnings by district for hazards including high temperatures, rough coastal seas, wind, heavy rain and thunderstorms. In summer, look out for heat warnings; in autumn and winter, Atlantic storms bring wind, heavy rain and big seas.

When serious weather or another emergency hits, the national civil protection authority, [ANEPC](https://prociv.gov.pt/), publishes warnings and preventive advice for the public, along with information on active incidents. Follow local instructions, stay away from seafronts during storms, and do not drive through floodwater.

## Hiking on the islands

Madeira and the Azores have some of the finest walking in Europe, and some of the most demanding. GOV.UK warns that paths can be narrow, uneven and exposed, and slippery after rain. It suggests leaving details of your route with your accommodation, carrying a phone, and considering a group or guide. Our guide to [levada walks for beginners](/stories/levada-walks-for-beginners/) is a good place to start.

## Who to call

- **Emergencies:** 112, for police, ambulance and fire, as listed in [GOV.UK's travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security).
- **Police:** GOV.UK notes that the PSP covers cities and the GNR covers smaller towns and rural areas. Report theft promptly; you will need a police report for an insurance claim.
- **Lost passport:** contact your country's embassy or consulate. For documents and entry rules more generally, see our guide to [Portugal's entry requirements](/stories/portugal-entry-requirements/).

## Before you book

- Read the latest [GOV.UK travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) or your own government's equivalent shortly before you travel.
- Take out travel insurance, and keep a copy of your passport and policy separately from the originals.
- On the beach, check the flag before you swim and choose supervised beaches where possible.
- From April to October, check IPMA's [fire danger map](https://www.ipma.pt/en/riscoincendio/rcm.pt/) before outdoor plans, and never light fires.
- Watch IPMA's [weather warnings](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/), especially for coastal walks and island hikes.
- Save 112 in your phone.
