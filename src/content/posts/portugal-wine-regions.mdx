Order a glass of house wine anywhere in Portugal and you are drinking geography. A white from the green, rain-soaked north tastes nothing like a red from the baking Alentejo plain, and neither tastes like the wine grown in walled pens of black lava in the middle of the Atlantic. For a small country, Portugal packs in an astonishing range of wine, and much of it is made from grapes you will not find anywhere else.

The trouble for a first-time visitor is the label. Portuguese bottles name the place before the grape, so a little geography goes a long way. This is the map we wish we had been handed on our first trip.

## The big picture

Wines of Portugal, the industry's official promotional body, divides the country into [14 wine regions](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/), running from Vinho Verde in the far north-west down to the Algarve, plus Madeira and the Azores out in the Atlantic. Its page on grapes counts [more than 250 native varieties](https://winesofportugal.com/en/portuguese-wines/grape-varieties/), which is why so many names on a wine list will be new to you.

A useful rule of thumb: the closer to the Atlantic, the fresher and lighter the wine; the further inland and south, the warmer and fuller. Granite and rain in the north, schist in the Douro, sand and clay near the coast, and hot plains in the south all leave their mark in the glass.

## Vinho Verde: the green north

Up in the Minho, Wines of Portugal describes a region with an [extreme Atlantic influence](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/vinho-verde/), green and humid, with cool temperatures and abundant rainfall. It is the largest denomination in the country, split among thousands of small plots on mostly granite soils.

The whites are the stars: aromatic, clear and refreshing, made from varieties such as **Alvarinho** and **Loureiro**. The sub-region of Monção e Melgaço, the only one sheltered from direct Atlantic influence, makes fuller-bodied Alvarinho with more alcohol. There are reds too, from grapes like Vinhão, and the region has made sparkling wine since 1999.

Our guide to [the Minho and vinho verde country](/stories/minho-and-vinho-verde/) has the towns and wineries to build a trip around.

## The Douro and Trás-os-Montes

The Douro is Portugal's most famous wine landscape, and the IVDP, the public body that regulates it, describes it as [the oldest demarcated and regulated wine region in the world](https://www.ivdp.pt/en), dating from 1756. Wines of Portugal notes the [poor schist soils](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/porto-e-douro/) and the thousands of terraces built on steep slopes, and names five red varieties singled out for Port: Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, Tinta Barroca and Tinto Cão.

The valley makes two great families of wine: fortified Port and unfortified Douro reds and whites. If you only have time for one wine region, make it this one, and see our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) for how to do it by train.

<Pair a="wr-mirandela-terraces" b="wr-madeira-grapes" captionA="Terraced vines below an old farmhouse near Mirandela, in Portugal's north-east." captionB="Red grapes ripening on the vine at Porto da Cruz, on Madeira's north coast." />

North and east of the Douro, remote **Trás-os-Montes** is cut off from the coast by mountains. Wines of Portugal describes a [harsh continental climate](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/tras-os-montes/) with long, scorching summers and long, cold winters, and three sub-regions: Chaves, Valpaços and Planalto Mirandês. It is little visited and all the more interesting for it.

## The centre: Dão and Bairrada

Two neighbouring regions in the centre make a fascinating contrast.

**Dão** sits in a bowl of mountains, its vineyards scattered among pine forests at altitudes from around 200 metres up to about 1,000 metres in the Serra da Estrela. Wines of Portugal says the mountains [shelter the vines](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/dao-e-lafoes/) from both continental and maritime influence, on poor, mostly granite soils. Touriga Nacional leads the reds, and **Encruzado** is the white to look for.

**Bairrada**, closer to the coast, is flat and Atlantic, with [clay-limestone soils and sandy strips](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/bairrada/). Its reds are dominated by the **Baga** grape, and the cool, humid climate suits sparkling wine, which the region took up early.

## Lisboa and Setúbal: near the capital

Rolling hills run north from the capital along the coast, and this is the **Lisboa** region. Wines of Portugal lists [nine sub-areas](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/lisboa/), from Bucelas, known for fresh, long-lived whites from the Arinto grape, to Colares, where Ramisco vines grow in loose sand by the sea. Coastal vineyards here make light wines with low alcohol; inland, sheltered by hills, the climate turns more Mediterranean.

<Figure id="wr-lisboa-arruda-vines" caption="Rows of vines at Arruda dos Vinhos, one of the Lisboa region's sub-areas, with the town on the ridge beyond." />

Across the Tagus, the **Península de Setúbal** is the [birthplace of Moscatel de Setúbal](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/peninsula-de-setubal/), a fortified Muscat that counted Louis XIV among its admirers. The red grape here is **Castelão**, which Wines of Portugal says gives its best in the warm, sandy soils of Palmela. Pair a tasting with a day on the coast using our guide to [Arrábida and Setúbal](/stories/arrabida-and-setubal/).

## The Alentejo: the big plains

The [Alentejo](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/alentejo/) stretches across almost a third of mainland Portugal, a region of rolling plains with a Mediterranean climate that Wines of Portugal describes as hot and dry with a strong continental influence. The reds are generous and sun-ripened, built on **Aragonez**, **Trincadeira** and **Alicante Bouschet**, while **Antão Vaz** and **Roupeiro** lead the whites. The region has eight sub-regions, from cooler, granite Portalegre in the north to warmer Vidigueira and Moura in the south.

<Figure id="wr-alentejo-plains" size="full" caption="Scattered trees on the dry, golden plains of the Alentejo in summer." />

[Herdade do Esporão](https://esporao.com/en/origins/alentejo), at Reguengos de Monsaraz, says it has welcomed visitors since 1997 and offers tours of its vineyards, olive groves and cellars with tastings, plus a restaurant. Reguengos sits right beside the hilltop village in our guide to [Monsaraz and Alqueva](/stories/monsaraz-and-alqueva/), so the two make an easy pairing.

## Madeira: the wine that lasts

Madeira's wine is fortified and, in Wines of Portugal's words, has [an almost unlimited storage capacity](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/madeira/), able to survive for more than two centuries. The vines grow on small raised terraces on steep volcanic slopes, and the soil and humid climate give wines of very high acidity.

The four noble white grapes, **Sercial**, **Verdelho**, **Boal** and **Malvasia**, are made into dry, medium dry, semi-sweet and sweet wines. The red **Tinta Negra** makes up more than 80 per cent of the harvest and can be made in all four styles.

<Figure id="wr-madeira-terraces" caption="Houses and small terraced plots climbing a steep green valley on Madeira." />

In Funchal, [Blandy's Wine Lodge](https://blandyswinelodge.com/en/home/), on Avenida Arriaga, occupies buildings that Visit Madeira says include [the oldest Madeira wine cellars](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/culture-passionates/heritage/museums/madeira-wine-company-museum/), dating from the 17th and 18th centuries, with a museum, tasting rooms and a wine shop. Our [Funchal guide](/stories/funchal-guide/) has the rest of the city.

## The Azores: vines in lava

The most extraordinary vineyards in Portugal may be on Pico. Visit Azores confirms that the [Landscape of the Pico Island Vineyard Culture](https://www.visitazores.com/en/explore/landscape-of-the-pico-island-vineyard-culture) has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2004, with vines planted in black lava ground and walls running parallel and perpendicular to the coast.

<Figure id="wr-pico-currais" size="column" caption="Vines in their walled pens of black lava stone on Pico, with Faial across the channel." />

Wines of Portugal explains the logic: vines were traditionally planted in corrals [sheltered by volcanic stone walls](https://winesofportugal.com/en/discover/wine-regions/acores/) that hold the day's heat and warm the vines at night. The region has three appellations, Pico, Biscoitos on Terceira and Graciosa, and **Verdelho** is its most famous grape, alongside Arinto and Terrantez.

## Where to taste the whole country

If you cannot get to the regions, bring them to you. Wines of Portugal runs two [tasting rooms](https://www.winesofportugal.com/en/discover/tasting-rooms/): one on Terreiro do Paço in Lisbon, where it says you can taste from more than 1,000 wines from every region, and one at World of Wine in Vila Nova de Gaia. It notes that wine tasting is not permitted for anyone under 18.

## Before you book

- Check opening days and book winery visits ahead, especially at harvest time in late summer and early autumn.
- Plan your driving before you taste. The EU's [Your Europe guide](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/road-rules-and-safety/portugal/index_en.htm) gives Portugal's blood alcohol limit as 0.5 mg/ml for most drivers and 0.2 mg/ml for novice and professional drivers. Use a driver, a tour or the train.
- Check current tasting-room hours and prices on the [Wines of Portugal site](https://www.winesofportugal.com/en/discover/tasting-rooms/) before you go.
- If you are shipping wine home, check your home country's import allowances first.
