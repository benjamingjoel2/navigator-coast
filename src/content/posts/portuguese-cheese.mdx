It usually arrives before you have ordered. A small plate set down with the bread and olives: a wedge of something pale and firm, or a little round cheese with its top sliced off, its interior soft enough to scoop with a knife. In Portugal, cheese is less a course than a way of starting the conversation.

Most visitors know the country for its wine and pastries, and miss how good its cheese is. That is a mistake worth correcting. Portugal makes cheese from sheep, goats and cows, on mountains, plains and volcanic islands, and many of the best carry a protected name that ties them to a single place.

## How to read a Portuguese cheese

Two things are worth knowing before you shop.

First, the protected labels. Many of Portugal's traditional cheeses are registered in the EU's eAmbrosia database as a Protected Designation of Origin, or PDO (DOP in Portuguese). That means the cheese must come from a defined area and follow a set method. A whole group of them were registered together in 1996, including Serra da Estrela, Azeitão, Évora, Serpa, Nisa and São Jorge.

Second, the thistle. Several of the great sheep's cheeses are not set with animal rennet but with an infusion of cardoon, a wild thistle, which the EU summaries name by its Latin name, *Cynara cardunculus*. Look for the word *cardo* or a mention of thistle on labels and menus; it is one of the threads that connects the country's great sheep's cheeses.

## Serra da Estrela: the mountain queen

Portugal's most famous cheese comes from its highest mountains. Center of Portugal, the regional tourism board, describes Serra da Estrela cheese as [made from the milk of Bordaleira sheep](https://centerofportugal.com/article/serra-da-estrela-cheese) and set by hand with cardoon flower. It says the cheese can be buttery or dry, that the curd is made from November to May, and that it has been documented since the 16th century. The name is protected as [Queijo Serra da Estrela PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013223).

<Figure id="chz-serra-da-estrela-winter" caption="Snow on the Serra da Estrela in winter, the season when the mountain's cheese is made." />

The buttery version is the one to spoon: cut away the top crust like a lid and scoop the soft interior onto bread. Look out too for the mountain's fresh whey cheese, protected separately as [Requeijão Serra da Estrela PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013542).

To understand the shepherding behind it all, the [Cheese Museum of Pêraboa](https://centerofportugal.com/entity/cheese-museum-of-peraboa-covilha), in a village about 14 km from Covilhã, tells the story of the sheep and the shepherds of the Serra and the routes their flocks travelled across Portugal and Spain. Our guide to the [Serra da Estrela](/stories/serra-da-estrela/) covers the mountain towns, walks and winter driving.

## Beira Baixa: three cheeses in one name

South of the Serra, the [Queijo da Beira Baixa PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013224) covers three cheeses under one umbrella: Queijo de Castelo Branco, Queijo Amarelo da Beira Baixa and Queijo Picante da Beira Baixa. The EU summary describes the Amarelo as a ripe cheese with a medium-hard or medium-soft texture, made from ewe's milk alone or mixed with goat's milk. As the name suggests, the Picante is the one with a kick.

## Azeitão: the soft cheese of the south bank

Cross the Tagus from Lisbon and you reach the Arrábida hills and the little cheeses of Azeitão. The EU's summary for [Queijo de Azeitão PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013227) describes a cured ewe's milk cheese of buttery, semi-soft consistency, white or light yellow, set with cardoon and made only in the municipalities of Palmela, Sesimbra and Setúbal.

These are small cheeses, and the softest can be eaten the same way: lid off, spoon in. Combine a cheese stop with the coast and the Moscatel wine country using our guide to [Arrábida and Setúbal](/stories/arrabida-and-setubal/).

## The Alentejo: Évora, Serpa and Nisa

The Alentejo is sheep country, and three of its cheeses carry protected names. All three are made from raw ewe's milk set with cardoon, but they taste quite different.

<Pair a="chz-sheep-estremoz" b="chz-sheep-alentejo" captionA="Two sheep resting on a green hillside near Estremoz, in the Alentejo." captionB="A sheep with its bell on dry summer pasture in the Alentejo." />

- **Évora.** The EU summary for [Queijo de Évora PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013087) describes a cured cheese of semi-hard or hard consistency, light yellow, with few or no holes, from a defined area of central Alentejo municipalities that includes Évora, Estremoz, Arraiolos, Borba and Reguengos de Monsaraz. Its summary links the milk's character to the region's hot, dry summers and Mediterranean pastures.
- **Serpa.** Further south, [Queijo Serpa PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013095) is a cured cheese of buttery, semi-soft consistency, from Serpa, Beja, Moura, Mértola and neighbouring municipalities of the Lower Alentejo.
- **Nisa.** Up near the Spanish border in the north, [Queijo de Nisa PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013220) is a semi-hard cheese with small holes, made in Nisa, Castelo de Vide, Marvão, Portalegre and nearby. The EU summary credits its character to natural pastures under oak groves.

<Figure id="chz-alentejo-lone-tree" size="full" caption="A lone tree on the golden Alentejo plain, the open country that feeds the region's sheep." />

Évora makes the natural base for a cheese-minded trip, with markets and restaurants close at hand; see our [Évora guide](/stories/evora-guide/). If you head north towards Nisa, our guide to [Marvão and Castelo de Vide](/stories/marvao-and-castelo-de-vide/) covers the hill towns in the same protected area.

## The Azores: São Jorge and Pico

Out in the Atlantic, the cheese changes animal. The green pastures of the Azores are cow country, and the islands' most famous cheese is a world away from the soft sheep's cheeses of the mainland.

<Figure id="chz-pico-cows" caption="Cattle grazing on Pico, with the island's volcano rising behind." />

**São Jorge** cheese is, according to its EU summary, a [cured cheese of firm consistency](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013102), hard or semi-hard and yellowish, with small irregular holes, made from the raw whole milk of cows on the island of São Jorge. Visit Azores describes [Uniqueijo](https://www.visitazores.com/en/explore/uniqueijo), a union of the island's dairy cooperatives founded in 1986, as the body that stores, cures and packages São Jorge cheese, and notes it has launched a version cured for seven months.

<Figure id="chz-sao-jorge-wheel" size="column" caption="A whole wheel of São Jorge cheese, its name pressed into the rind." />

Neighbouring **Pico** has its own protected cheese, [Queijo do Pico PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013436), worth seeking out if you are visiting the island for its walled vineyards.

## Three more names for the list

The protected cheeses above are the ones you will meet most often, but the register goes further, and spotting the rarer names on a shop counter is half the fun. Three worth noting down:

- **Queijo de Cabra Transmontano**, the goat's cheese of Trás-os-Montes in the far north-east, protected as [Queijo de Cabra Transmontano PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013236).
- **Queijo Terrincho**, registered as [Queijo Terrincho PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013228).
- **Queijo Rabaçal**, registered as [Queijo Rabaçal PDO](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013231).

None of them is easy to find outside its home region, which is exactly the point. If you see one, it never hurts to ask for a taste before you buy.

## Where to buy and how to serve

In Lisbon, [Manteigaria Silva](https://www.manteigariasilva.pt/), in the Baixa on Rua D. Antão de Almada, has been trading since 1890 and sells cheese alongside cured meats and salt cod; it is a good place to put together a selection from different regions. Elsewhere, municipal markets and village shops in cheese country are the best places to buy direct, and our [region-by-region food guide](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) has more on what to eat with it.

At the table, keep it simple: good bread, perhaps some quince paste, and a glass of something local. The IVDP, which regulates Port, recommends a [10-year-old tawny with strong cheeses](https://www.ivdp.pt/en/wines/port-wines/choose-and-enjoy/), dried fruit and walnuts, and a Ruby Reserve with mild, creamy cheese.

## Before you book

- If cheese is the point of your trip, aim for the cooler months: Center of Portugal notes that Serra da Estrela cheese is made from November to May.
- Look for the DOP or PDO seal and the producer's label when buying, especially for Serra da Estrela, Azeitão and São Jorge.
- Soft, ripe cheeses travel badly. Buy them near the end of your trip and keep them cool.
- Check your home country's rules on bringing dairy products back before you pack cheese in your luggage.
