Stand on any of Lisbon's miradouros in the late afternoon and you will understand the pull of this country in about thirty seconds. The light comes off the Tagus and turns the tiled façades gold, a tram grinds uphill somewhere below you, and the whole city seems to slope gently towards the water. Portugal does that to first-time visitors: it looks small on a map, and then it quietly refuses to be seen in a week.

This guide is the one we wish we could hand everyone before their first trip. It covers how the country fits together, how long to give it, what the entry rules look like right now, and how to book with confidence. Our other guides go deeper on each piece.

## The shape of the country

Mainland Portugal is a long, narrow rectangle with the Atlantic along its whole western and southern edge and Spain to the east. Two archipelagos, Madeira and the Azores, sit far out in the ocean and feel like separate worlds. For planning purposes, we think of it as seven regions.

### Lisbon

The capital is the natural starting point: hilly, bright and easy to love. Alfama's lanes, the riverside monuments at Belém and the miradouros reward slow wandering. Lisbon also makes an excellent base for day trips, with the palaces and forested hills of Sintra and the seaside town of Cascais both reachable on [Lisbon's urban train lines](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/lisbon).

<Figure id="ftg-lisbon-belem-tower" caption="The Torre de Belém at the water's edge, one of the riverside monuments of Belém." />

### Porto and the Douro

Porto is granite and river mist where Lisbon is limestone and sunshine. Its old centre tumbles down to the Ribeira waterfront, and the port lodges sit across the river in Vila Nova de Gaia. Inland, the Douro Valley is one of the great wine landscapes of Europe: steep terraced vineyards falling to the river. The [Douro Demarcated Region](https://www.ivdp.pt/en/viticulture/region/characteristics-of-the-region/) covers a large area of north-east Portugal and is divided into three sub-regions, from the greener west to the hot, remote Douro Superior far upriver.

### Alentejo

South and east of Lisbon, the Alentejo is big skies, cork oaks, whitewashed villages and long, unhurried lunches. Its capital, Évora, has history going back to Roman times, and the [whole city is a UNESCO World Heritage site](https://www.visitalentejo.pt/en/alentejo/culture/unesco-world-heritage/world-heritage-list/), as is the hilltop border town of Elvas. The Alentejo coast is wilder and quieter than the Algarve's.

### Algarve

Portugal's southern coast is the one most people picture: golden cliffs, sea caves, fishing towns and grilled fish eaten with your toes almost in the sand. Go a little inland and you find hill villages and cork country; go west towards Sagres and the coast turns rugged and windswept.

<Pair a="ftg-evora-roman-temple" b="ftg-algarve-ponta-da-piedade" captionA="The granite columns of Évora's Roman temple, in the Alentejo's UNESCO-listed capital." captionB="Sea stacks and cliffs at Ponta da Piedade, near Lagos on the western Algarve." />

### Centro

The region between Lisbon and Porto is the one first-timers most often skip, and we think that is a mistake. Coimbra's old university town, the canals and lagoons around Aveiro, the monasteries of Alcobaça and Batalha, the schist villages and the granite heights of the Serra da Estrela all live here.

<Figure id="ftg-coimbra-river-sunset" size="full" caption="Coimbra's old town climbing from the Mondego to the university tower at the top of the hill." />

### Madeira

A volcanic island of cliffs and laurel forest, Madeira is made for walking. Its famous levadas, irrigation channels with paths alongside, thread through the mountains. Note that access to the official walking routes [now carries a fee for non-residents](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/advice-to-walkers/), and the island's tourism board publishes safety advice for walkers that is worth reading before you set out.

### The Azores

[Nine islands](https://www.visitazores.com/en/plan-your-visit) in the mid-Atlantic: crater lakes, hot springs, hydrangea-lined lanes and whale-watching waters. The archipelago is roughly [two hours' flight from Lisbon or Porto](https://www.visitazores.com/en/plan-your-visit), but it feels much further from the mainland's rhythm.

## How long to give it

There is no wrong length of trip, only a wrong amount of ground for the time you have. Our rough rule of thumb:

| Time | What works well |
| --- | --- |
| 4 to 5 days | One city and its surroundings: Lisbon with Sintra, or Porto with a day in the Douro |
| 7 to 10 days | Lisbon and Porto linked by train, with a stop in the Centro, or one city plus the Alentejo or Algarve |
| Two weeks | A mainland loop of three or four regions, or a mainland city plus one of the islands |
| Three weeks or more | Room for the islands as well as the mainland, at a slower pace |

The islands deserve their own block of time. We would rather you saw one Azorean island properly than three in a rush. Our itinerary posts suggest specific routes, and our guide to getting around covers trains, buses and when to hire a car.

## Entry rules as of November 2026

Portugal is part of the Schengen area. Rules depend on your nationality, so always check with your own government and the official EU sites before you travel.

**The 90/180 rule.** Visa-free visitors, including British citizens, can stay in the Schengen area for [up to 90 days in any 180-day period](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/entry-requirements). Time spent in other Schengen countries counts towards the same total.

**Passport validity.** For British travellers, [GOV.UK's entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/entry-requirements) says your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before the day you arrive and must be valid for at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave the Schengen area. Check this well before you book, not at the airport.

**EES, the Entry/Exit System.** The EU's new digital border system [registers non-EU nationals](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/ees/what-is-the-ees) entering and leaving the participating countries for short stays. In practice, as [GOV.UK explains](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/entry-requirements), it replaces passport stamping and records your fingerprints and a photo, so the first time you cross a border under the system may take longer. Allow extra time at the airport.

**ETIAS, the travel authorisation.** You may have read that visa-exempt travellers will need to apply online before travelling. As of this writing, the official EU site states that [ETIAS is not yet in operation and no applications are being collected](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/etias/about-etias/what-is-etias), and that the EU will announce a start date several months before launch. Be wary of unofficial websites offering to process an ETIAS for you; the official route is through the EU's own site.

## Language

Portuguese is the national language, and a few words go a long way: *bom dia* (good morning), *obrigado* or *obrigada* (thank you, depending on whether you are male or female), *por favor* (please). Portuguese is not Spanish, and you will win more smiles by trying a little Portuguese than by defaulting to Spanish.

## A few classics you will meet

Some places turn up on almost every first trip, and for good reason: several have been part of daily life here for well over a century. These are the names worth knowing before you arrive.

### In Lisbon

- **[Pastéis de Belém](https://pasteisdebelem.pt/en/history/)**, on Rua de Belém, has been making its custard tarts since 1837. According to its own history, the recipe came from the neighbouring Jerónimos Monastery and is still known only to the master pastry cooks who work in its "secret room".
- **[A Brasileira](https://ovalordotempo.pt/marcas/a-brasileira-do-chiado/)**, on Rua Garrett in the Chiado, opened in November 1905. It is known for its literary past: Fernando Pessoa was a regular, and a bronze statue of the poet sits on its terrace.
- **[Time Out Market Lisboa](https://www.timeout.com/time-out-market-lisboa)** opened in 2014 inside the Mercado da Ribeira market hall at Cais do Sodré, bringing restaurants, bars and shops together under one roof.

### In Porto

- **[Livraria Lello](https://livrarialello.pt/)**, at Rua das Carmelitas 144, is a neo-Gothic bookshop that has been open since 1906 with a carved wooden interior built around a curving red staircase. Entry is by ticket, booked through its website, and the ticket price can be deducted from a book you buy there.
- **[Majestic Café](https://www.cafemajestic.com/pt/Majestic-Cafe/Historia.aspx)**, on Rua de Santa Catarina, first opened to the public in December 1922, and has kept its original name ever since; the café describes its main room as Belle Époque.

### A historic place to stay

Portugal's **[Pousadas de Portugal](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel)**, now part of the Pestana group, are hotels set in historic buildings: castles, convents and palaces, with 35 spread from north to south. Two examples in the Alentejo: the [Pousada Castelo Estremoz](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-estremoz), in a castle built in the 13th century, and the [Pousada Convento Arraiolos](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-arraiolos), in a former convent. They work well as a stop between cities on a road trip.

## Staying safe

Portugal is generally a relaxed place to travel, but the usual city sense applies. The UK government's [safety and security advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) highlights a few points worth knowing:

- **Pickpockets.** Take care on public transport and at crowded tram and bus stops, where thieves may work in teams and use distraction.
- **The sea.** Supervised beaches use a flag system: red means no entry to the water, yellow means paddling only. Rip currents can be strong. If you are caught in one, do not swim against it; swim parallel to the shore until it eases.
- **Wildfires.** The risk rises from April to October in hot, dry weather. Follow local authorities' advice, and remember that starting a fire, even by accident, is illegal.
- **Emergencies.** The number to call is 112.

## How to book safely

Portugal has a public register for the tourism trade, and it is one of the most useful tools a first-time visitor rarely knows about. Turismo de Portugal's [Registo Nacional de Turismo (RNT)](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/) brings together registrations for accommodation, travel agencies and activity operators.

Two parts of it matter most when you are booking tours or packages:

- **RNAVT**, the national register of travel and tourism agencies. You can [search it here](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AVT.aspx) to check that an agency holds a valid licence to operate in Portugal.
- **RNAAT**, the national register of tourism activity operators, which covers companies running activities and experiences as well as maritime-tourism operators. You can [search it here](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AAT.aspx).

The pages are in Portuguese, but searching by name or registration number is straightforward. A reputable Portuguese operator will usually show its RNAVT or RNAAT number on its website or booking documents; if it doesn't, ask for it and check it.

## Before you book

- Check your passport against the issue-date and validity rules for your nationality.
- Re-check the [EES](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/ees/what-is-the-ees) and [ETIAS](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/etias/about-etias/what-is-etias) pages close to departure, as the rules are changing.
- Read the latest [GOV.UK travel advice for Portugal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security), or your own government's equivalent.
- Look up any agency or activity operator on the [RNAVT](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AVT.aspx) or [RNAAT](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AAT.aspx) registers before you pay.
- Decide how many regions you can do justice to, and then consider dropping one.
