Pour a glass of old Madeira and hold it up to the window. The colour runs from pale gold to deep amber and mahogany, and the smell is of dried fruit, toasted nuts, caramel and something sharper underneath, an acidity that keeps it bright. Taste it and that acidity cuts through the sweetness, so that even the richest styles finish clean. Nothing else tastes quite like it.

Madeira is one of the world's great fortified wines, and also one of the strangest. It is deliberately warmed, sometimes for years, in a way that would ruin almost any other wine. And it comes from a small, steep island where vineyards are squeezed onto terraces between banana plantations and village houses. If you are on Madeira, learning to drink it is one of the best things you can do.

## A wine shaped by the sea

The Madeira wine institute, IVBAM, tells the [story of the wine](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/historia) from the island's discovery in 1419, when settlers cleared land for wheat, vines and sugar cane. It says exports of wine were already under way 25 years after colonisation began, and that between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries the great routes ran to the Indies and the Americas. IVBAM notes that the independence of the United States in 1776 was toasted with Madeira, and names George Washington and Thomas Jefferson among the wine's admirers, along with Winston Churchill on his visits to the island.

According to the same history, the ageing method known as estufagem was introduced in the eighteenth century and came to define Madeira wine. Heat is the island's signature.

## How Madeira is made

IVBAM's page on [vinification and ageing](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/vinificacao-e-envelhecimento) sets out the process. The grapes are sorted and checked, the must is fermented, and then the wine is fortified with neutral grape spirit, which stops the fermentation. When that happens decides how much natural sugar is left, and so which of the four types the wine becomes: dry, medium dry, medium sweet or sweet.

Then comes the heat, by one of two routes.

**Estufagem.** The wine is heated in stainless steel tanks fitted with a hot-water coil, at 45 to 50°C, for at least three months, followed by a rest of at least 90 days at ambient temperature. IVBAM says these wines cannot be bottled or sold before 31 October of the second year after the harvest, and that they are mostly blends.

**Canteiro.** The slower, traditional method. Casks rest on wooden beams, usually on the upper floors of the lodges where it is warmest, and the wine ages oxidatively in wood for a minimum of two years. IVBAM says canteiro wines can be sold only after at least three years, counted from 1 January of the year after the harvest.

## The grapes

IVBAM lists six [grape varieties](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas) as the most widely used for Madeira wine. One workhorse and five classic names that you will see on labels:

| Grape | Where it grows | Style |
|---|---|---|
| [Tinta Negra](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/tinta-negra) | South coast around Funchal and Câmara de Lobos, and São Vicente in the north | All four, from dry to sweet |
| [Sercial](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/sercial) | Mainly the north and Câmara de Lobos | Dry |
| [Verdelho](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/verdelho) | Around 400 metres | Medium dry |
| [Boal](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/boal) | Between 100 and 300 metres | Medium sweet |
| [Malvasia](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/malvasia-candida-e-malvasia-de-sao-jorge) | Cândida on the south coast; São Jorge on the north coast | Sweet |
| [Terrantez](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/castas/terrantez) | South coast, at Calheta, Câmara de Lobos and Funchal | Medium dry and medium sweet |

Tinta Negra, which IVBAM says was introduced in the eighteenth century, accounts for between 80 and 85 per cent of all Madeira wine. Sercial, also known as Esgana Cão for its high acidity and astringency, gives the driest, most piercing wines. Verdelho is slightly fuller and less acidic. Boal is prized for its balance of acidity and sweetness. Malvasia-Cândida, the first variety planted on the island, makes the richest and most unctuous wines. Terrantez is rare enough that islanders have a saying about it: do not eat the grapes or give them away, for God made them for wine.

## Reading the label

IVBAM's page on [types and categories](https://vinhomadeira.com/o-vinho-madeira/tipos-categorias) lists the traditional designations. Blends may carry an age indication: 5 years (also called Reserva), 10 years (Reserva Velha), 15 years (Reserva Extra), and 20, 30, 40 or over 50 years. Others carry no indication of age or grape at all. Separately, there are wines with a stated date: Colheita, and Frasqueira, also called Garrafeira.

A simple rule of thumb for a first tasting: try a dry Sercial or a medium-dry Verdelho before food, and a Boal or Malvasia with dessert or cheese, and compare a younger blend with a 10-year-old. The difference that time in wood makes is the whole point.

## Where to taste in Funchal

Funchal is home to several historic Madeira houses with lodges or tasting rooms you can visit, and our [guide to Funchal](/stories/funchal-guide/) covers the rest of the city. Three long-established names:

**Blandy's Wine Lodge.** On Avenida Arriaga in the centre of Funchal, the [Madeira Wine Company](https://www.madeirawinecompany.com/en/wine-tourism/blandys-wine-lodge.html) describes the lodge as a historic building that has been the heart of the Blandy family's wine business for seven generations. It ages wine by the canteiro method, with each room keeping different temperature and humidity, and offers tours and a range of tastings.

**H.M. Borges.** Founded in 1877 by Henrique Menezes Borges, [H.M. Borges](https://www.hmborges.com/) is still run by the founding family. Its [visits page](https://www.hmborges.com/visitar/) says the company has been in a century-old building in the centre of Funchal since 1924, offering guided visits and tastings among old photographs and paintings by the German artist Max Römer. It opens on weekdays, and places are limited, so book ahead.

**Barbeito.** [Vinhos Barbeito](https://www.vinhosbarbeito.com/en/) is a family company founded in 1946 by Mário Barbeito de Vasconcelos. Its [tour and tasting sheet](https://www.vinhosbarbeito.com/images/Tour-Tasting-2026-en.pdf) describes a guided visit of the winery with tastings of aged and dated wines, run on weekdays except public holidays, with pre-booking required.

<Figure id="mw-camara-de-lobos" caption="Câmara de Lobos, with terraced slopes climbing towards Cabo Girão: Sercial and Tinta Negra country." />

Outside the city, the vineyards themselves are part of the experience. IVBAM names Câmara de Lobos as a growing area for Tinta Negra, Sercial and Terrantez, and our [five-day Madeira road trip](/stories/madeira-road-trip/) passes right through them on the way to Cabo Girão.

## The wine festival

If you can time a trip for late summer, do. Visit Madeira's [Wine Festival page](https://visitmadeira.com/en/whats-on/events/wine-festival/) lists the next festival from 22 August to 12 September 2027, held during the harvest both in the streets of Funchal and in vineyards and wine cellars in rural areas. The programme includes tastings at the Madeira Wine Lounge in Praça do Povo and concerts in the vineyards, and the village of Estreito recreates the work of the harvest at its own festival in the first half of September.

## Madeira at the table

Madeira is wonderfully versatile with food. The drier styles work as an aperitif with salty snacks, and the sweeter ones with cake, chocolate or a cheese board. Our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) covers the island's food, and if you want to compare Madeira with that other great Portuguese fortified wine, our [guide to Port wine styles](/stories/port-wine-styles/) and our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) explain how Port is made.

## Before you book

- Book lodge visits and tastings in advance; H.M. Borges and Barbeito both ask for it, and places are limited.
- Check opening days: several lodges run visits on weekdays only.
- If you are driving between vineyards, remember that Portugal's limit for standard drivers is 0.5 g/l, as set out on [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/road-rules-and-safety/portugal/index_en.htm), and that a few tasting pours add up. Use the spittoon, or let someone else drive.
- For the wine festival, check this year's programme on [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/whats-on/events/wine-festival/) and book accommodation early.
- Buying bottles to take home? Check your airline's rules for liquids in hand luggage, or pack them in hold baggage.
