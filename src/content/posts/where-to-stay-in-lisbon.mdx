From the terrace at Portas do Sol, Lisbon looks like a city that was poured down its hills and set before it reached the river. Terracotta roofs tumble towards the Tagus, church towers poke up between them, and somewhere below a tram grinds round a corner it should not fit. It is a beautiful view, and it holds the single most useful thing to know before you book a bed here: Lisbon is steep, and where you sleep decides how much of it you climb.

The city is compact, but its neighbourhoods feel very different from one another. Some are flat, central and well connected; some are steep, atmospheric and loud on a summer night; some are calm, residential and a short ride from everything. This guide walks through them one by one, with a few long-established places to stay in each.

## How to choose

Three questions sort out most decisions.

**How do you feel about hills?** The Baixa sits in the valley between two ridges, and it is the flattest part of the centre. Almost everywhere else involves stairs, cobbles and gradients. If you are travelling with heavy luggage, small children or anyone who finds slopes hard, look at the map's contour lines as closely as the hotel photos.

**How much noise can you sleep through?** Bairro Alto and Cais do Sodré are where Lisbon goes out. Alfama and Graça get festive in June, when the city's saints' celebrations fill the lanes. The quieter options are Príncipe Real, Lapa, Estrela and Belém.

**How will you get around?** The metro covers the centre and the newer avenues, and the airport has its own metro station, Aeroporto, according to [Metro Lisboa's tickets page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/). The same page notes that contactless bank cards can be used to pay for and validate metro journeys. A base within a few minutes' walk of a metro stop makes arrival and day trips easier. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers the city's cards and passes in more detail.

If you are still deciding between the two big cities, start with our comparison of [Lisbon or Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) and come back here once you have chosen.

## Baixa and Chiado: central and practical

The Baixa is the grid of streets rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake, running from Rossio down to the great riverside square of Praça do Comércio. It is the easiest place in Lisbon to get around on foot, and it puts you within a short walk of almost everything a first visit involves. Uphill to the west, Chiado is the elegant shopping and café quarter; Visit Lisboa calls it ["Lisbon's perennial upmarket neighbourhood"](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/chiado-41699ec3-eda7-414e-a9c1-e985f2f0efb7).

<Figure id="lxs-praca-do-comercio" caption="Praça do Comércio and its triumphal arch, the riverside edge of the Baixa." />

The trade-off is that the Baixa is busy by day and has more shops and chains than local life. It suits a first trip, a short city break or anyone who wants to minimise walking uphill.

**Where to stay.** The [Pousada Lisboa](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-lisboa) occupies a building on Praça do Comércio erected after the 1755 earthquake. Its own page says it was once a royal residence and later housed ministries, including the former Ministry of Internal Administration, before the Pestana group converted it into a hotel in 2015. At the top of the Baixa, the [Hotel Avenida Palace](https://www.hotelavenidapalace.pt/) on Rua 1º de Dezembro is a neoclassical building designed in the late 19th century, which the hotel describes as a historical landmark of the city.

## Alfama, Graça and Mouraria: old Lisbon

Visit Lisboa describes Alfama as ["the oldest and most traditional neighbourhood in Lisbon"](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/alfama). It is a tangle of lanes, stairways and tiny squares below the Castelo de São Jorge, with fado houses tucked into its streets and viewpoints such as Santa Luzia looking out over the river. Next door, Mouraria climbs the other flank of the castle hill, and above both sits Graça, with what Visit Lisboa calls ["one of the best views of Lisbon"](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/graca) from the Miradouro da Graça. The same page suggests riding the no. 28 tram up to it.

<Figure id="lxs-alfama-rooftops" caption="Alfama's rooftops fall towards the Tagus, with the dome of the National Pantheon on the left." />

This is the Lisbon of postcards and it is a lovely place to wake up. It is also steep, many streets are too narrow for taxis to reach the door, and in June the neighbourhood parties run late. Choose it if you travel light and like to walk.

**Where to stay.** [memmo Alfama](https://www.memmohotels.com/alfama/) is a small hotel on Travessa das Merceeiras, in the heart of the quarter. Its wine bar and terrace sit high above the rooftops, looking out over the Tagus.

## Bairro Alto and Príncipe Real: nightlife and gardens

Bairro Alto is one of Lisbon's old quarters, laid out on the hill above Chiado. By day it is quiet, with record shops and small studios. By night, as [Visit Lisboa puts it](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/bairro-alto), it is where many nights out begin, before the crowd drifts downhill to Bica and ends in Cais do Sodré. Staying in the middle of it is fun if you are part of the party and miserable if you are not.

<Pair a="lxs-azulejo-facade" b="lxs-funicular-street" captionA="Tiled façades, wrought-iron balconies and tall windows: the Lisbon streetscape." captionB="One of the city's funicular cars at the top of its steep street." />

A few minutes further along the ridge, Príncipe Real is the grown-up neighbour. Visit Lisboa places it between the Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara and São Bento, around the [Praça do Príncipe Real](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/principe-real), and mentions its grand mansions, small antique shops and restaurants. It is leafy and calm at night, with the bars of Bairro Alto close enough to walk to and far enough not to hear. For many returning visitors, it is the best all-round base in the city.

Getting up to this ridge is part of daily life. The city's funiculars and the Santa Justa lift are run by Carris, and at the time of writing [Carris lists the Santa Justa Lift as temporarily closed](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/lift/), so do not plan your daily route around it.

## Cais do Sodré and the riverfront

Cais do Sodré is Lisbon's riverside transport hub. Visit Lisboa notes that it links the [metro, trains and boats](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/cais-do-sodre), with trains to Cascais about an hour away and a ferry across the Tagus to Almada. The old market hall, the Mercado da Ribeira, is one of the city's main food courts, and the bar-lined Pink Street is just behind it.

That makes Cais do Sodré very convenient for beach days and day trips along the coast, and very lively after dark. Light sleepers should pick a room on a side street or a higher floor, or look a little west towards Santos.

## Lapa, Santos and Estrela: quiet and residential

West of Cais do Sodré, the city calms down. Santos has design shops and a younger crowd; Lapa, up the hill, is the embassy quarter, with large houses and walled gardens; Estrela has its basilica and one of the city's prettiest public gardens. Trams and buses connect them to the centre, but this is a place to stay if you like to come home to quiet streets.

**Where to stay.** [York House](https://www.yorkhouselisboa.com/) is on Rua das Janelas Verdes, near the National Museum of Ancient Art and the Lapa district. The hotel describes the building as a 17th-century Carmelite convent.

## Belém and Alcântara: monuments and space

Belém is further out along the river, about the distance of a tram or train ride from the centre. Visit Lisboa highlights its monuments linked to the Age of Discoveries, including the Jerónimos Monastery and the Torre de Belém, which it describes as [internationally recognised UNESCO World Heritage sites](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/belem-c9f84a77-b1cc-4d28-9160-2fa78caf5d37). The same page mentions green spaces, marinas and a riverside cycle lane.

<Figure id="lxs-belem-tower" caption="The Torre de Belém, on the river at the western edge of the city." />

Belém has more space and greenery than the old centre and it is good for families and for repeat visitors. In the evenings, though, it is much quieter, and you will be travelling in for dinner.

**Where to stay.** [Pestana Palace](https://www.pestana.com/en/hotel/pestana-palace), in the Alto de Santo Amaro neighbourhood near Belém, occupies the Palácio de Valle Flôr, built from 1890 as a family residence for the Marquess of Valle-Flôr. The hotel's page says the palace and its French-style gardens are classified as National Monuments.

## Avenida da Liberdade and the Avenidas Novas

North of Rossio, the Avenida da Liberdade runs uphill as a tree-lined boulevard, and beyond it spread the wide streets of the Avenidas Novas. These areas are less atmospheric than the old quarters, but they are flatter, well served by the metro and full of mid-range business hotels. They are a sensible base if you have an early flight, a lot of luggage, or plans that take you out of the centre each day.

## Booking an apartment

Many visitors rent an apartment rather than a hotel room, and Lisbon has a great many. In Portugal, holiday rentals are registered as *alojamento local*, and Turismo de Portugal keeps a public [national register of local accommodation](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/Pesquisa_AL.aspx) within its wider [Registo Nacional de Turismo](https://rnt.turismodeportugal.pt/RNT/). Listings normally show their registration number; if one does not, ask the host for it.

## Before you book

- Look at the walk from the nearest metro stop or tram stop to the door, and check the gradient, not just the distance.
- If you are a light sleeper, avoid central Bairro Alto and the main streets of Cais do Sodré, and ask for a room away from the street.
- If you are visiting in June, expect late, loud nights in Alfama, Graça and Mouraria during the festivities.
- For an apartment, check that the listing has a local accommodation registration number and whether there is a lift.
- Check the latest opening status of lifts and funiculars on [Carris's site](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/lift/) before relying on them.
