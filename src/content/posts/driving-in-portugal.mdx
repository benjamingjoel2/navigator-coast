The moment usually comes about twenty minutes out of Lisbon. The city falls away behind the red towers of the bridge, the motorway opens up across the cork-oak country of the Alentejo, and you realise that the most beautiful parts of Portugal are the ones the trains never reach: the hill villages, the empty western beaches, the quintas at the end of a dirt track. A car turns a city break into a country.

It also brings a few things that catch visitors out, chief among them a toll system that has no booths on some motorways at all. Here is what we would want to know before picking up the keys.

## Do you need a car at all?

For Lisbon and Porto, no. Parking is scarce, the old quarters are a web of one-way lanes and tram tracks, and public transport covers most of what you will want to see. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers trains, metros and coaches in detail, and the Douro line is the classic way to reach the wine country, as we explain in [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/).

A car earns its keep once you leave the cities. The Alentejo plains, the Costa Vicentina, the Serra da Estrela and the mountains of the north are all far easier to explore under your own steam. A pattern that works well for many visitors is to spend the city days without a car, then collect one on the way out.

## Licences and documents

If you hold a UK photocard licence, GOV.UK's [Portugal travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) says it is valid for driving in Portugal. Holders of an older paper licence may need to update to a photocard or get the right International Driving Permit. GOV.UK also notes that it is mandatory to carry ID when driving, so keep your passport or national identity card with you, not back at the hotel.

If your licence was issued elsewhere, check your own government's travel advice before you travel, and ask the hire company what it requires at the desk. Rental firms often set their own conditions, such as minimum ages or how long you must have held a licence, and those vary from company to company.

## The rules of the road

The EU's [Your Europe page on Portugal's road rules](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/road-rules-and-safety/portugal/index_en.htm) is the clearest official summary in English. The headline points:

| Rule | What Your Europe lists for cars |
|---|---|
| Speed in built-up areas | 50 km/h |
| Speed outside built-up areas | 90 km/h |
| Motorways and expressways | 120 km/h and 100 km/h |
| Blood alcohol limit | 0.5 g/l standard drivers, 0.2 g/l novice and professional drivers |
| Mobile phones | Hands-free only |
| Seat belts and child restraints | Mandatory |

Signs override the defaults, so watch for lower limits through villages and roadworks. The page also lists a reflective vest and a warning triangle as safety equipment for cars. Before you drive off a rental lot, check both are in the car and that you know where they are.

The alcohol limit deserves a second look. If you are touring wine country, a couple of generous tasting pours can take you close to it. In the Douro, the Alentejo or the Vinho Verde region, agree on a non-drinking driver before the first quinta, or book a driver for the day.

## Motorway tolls: the part that trips people up

Many of Portugal's motorways are tolled, and there are two systems. [PT Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/en), the information site run by Infraestruturas de Portugal, explains both:

- **Traditional tolls** have barriers. You stop, and pay by cash or bank card, or drive through an electronic lane if you have a working device.
- **Electronic-only tolls** have no barriers. Gantries over the road detect your car, a panel near each gantry shows the charge, and these roads are marked with their own signs. To use them you need an activated means of payment.

If you are driving a foreign-registered car, PT Tolls lists several ways to pay on electronic-only roads. Easytoll links a bank card to your number plate. Tollcard is a prepaid card activated by text message. Via Verde offers subscription plans for visitors, and some toll devices issued in other countries also work in Portugal. Each comes with its own fees and validity rules, which PT Tolls sets out, so read the details before choosing.

One detail matters around Lisbon. PT Tolls notes that Easytoll and Tollcard are not valid on the Lusoponte crossings, which are the 25 de Abril and Vasco da Gama bridges over the Tagus. Via Verde says on its [visitor plans page](https://visitors.viaverde.pt/en/home/s/identificador) that only its plans let visitors use all national motorways without stopping at toll gates.

<Figure id="drv-vasco-da-gama-bridge" caption="The Vasco da Gama bridge striding out across the Tagus estuary from Lisbon." />

To estimate what a route will cost, use the official [toll calculator](https://portagens.infraestruturasdeportugal.pt/) from Infraestruturas de Portugal. And if you think you have driven through an electronic toll without paying, PT Tolls explains how to check your journeys and pay through the relevant operator's portal. GOV.UK's travel advice points to the same system. Sort it out promptly rather than leaving it for the post.

## Hire cars: questions to ask at the desk

Most visitors drive a rental, and the toll question is the one to settle before leaving the car park. Ask:

- **Does the car have a toll device fitted?** If so, how will tolls be charged to you, and when? Is there a daily fee for the device?
- **If not, how do I pay electronic tolls?** You will need one of the PT Tolls options set up before joining an electronic-only motorway.
- **Can I take the car into Spain?** Many itineraries cross the border, for example from the Algarve to Seville. Some rental agreements restrict this or charge for it.
- **What happens with fines?** Ask how traffic or toll penalties are passed on, and whether an administration fee is added.

Photograph the car, inside and out, before you leave, and again when you return it. GOV.UK's [travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) also reminds visitors to look after their belongings when arranging car hire. Do not leave bags visible on the seats when you park at a viewpoint or a beach.

## Mountain roads, towns and parking

Portugal's motorways are a pleasure, but the memorable driving is on the smaller roads, and those ask for a different rhythm. In the north and in the Serra de Monchique, routes climb in tight hairpins with low stone walls or bollards between you and the drop. In the Alentejo, minor roads are quiet and straight, then suddenly reduce to a single lane through a whitewashed village. Allow more time than a mapping app suggests, and do not count on overtaking.

Historic town centres are often the hardest part. Many have narrow, one-way streets designed long before cars, steep cobbled lanes, and limited-access zones. The simplest strategy is to park at the edge, in a signed car park, and walk in. Accommodation in old towns will usually tell you where guests can park, so ask before you arrive rather than circling the walls looking for a space.

## If something goes wrong

In an emergency, call 112. GOV.UK's [travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) gives 112 as the number for emergencies, and notes that the PSP police cover cities while the GNR covers smaller towns and rural areas, which is who you are likely to meet on country roads.

For a breakdown or a minor accident in a hire car, call the assistance number on the rental agreement first. Keep that paperwork in the glovebox along with your licence and ID. If you have to stop on a motorway hard shoulder, put the reflective vest on before you get out.

## Before you book

- Read [PT Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/en) and decide how you will pay electronic tolls before you collect the car, especially if you will use the Lisbon bridges.
- Ask the rental company whether the car has a toll device, how tolls and fines are charged, and whether you can drive into Spain.
- Check your licence is accepted. UK drivers should read [GOV.UK's advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security); others should check their own government's guidance.
- Run your route through the official [toll calculator](https://portagens.infraestruturasdeportugal.pt/) to budget for tolls.
- Agree on a non-drinking driver for wine tastings, and keep [Your Europe's limits](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/road-rules-and-safety/portugal/index_en.htm) in mind.
- Plan to park outside old town centres and walk in.
