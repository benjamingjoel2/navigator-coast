The first thing you hear is the water. A levada does not rush; it slides, a steady clear stream a hand's width deep, running in a stone channel no wider than a shoebox. Beside it runs a path, sometimes broad and soft with leaf litter, sometimes a ledge with the channel wall on one side and a long drop on the other. You follow the water, and the water follows the contour of the mountain.

That is the whole idea, and it is why levada walking is such a good way into Madeira. You get the island's ravines, waterfalls and forest without the brutal climbs of its peaks. But "gentle" is relative here, and the rules for walking changed in 2026. This is what a first-timer needs to know.

## What a levada is

Levadas are irrigation channels. Visit Madeira's [own guide to levada hikes](https://visitmadeira.com/en/blog/ellie-green/the-best-off-the-beaten-path-levada-hikes-in-madeira/) explains that some date back as far as the 15th century, and that they were built to carry water from the rainy north of the island to the drier south, first for sugar cane and later for bananas. Many still carry water today. The paths beside them were maintenance routes, not hiking trails, which is worth remembering when one narrows to a ledge.

Because water has to flow, the channels keep to a gentle gradient. The tourism board's [advice to walkers](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/advice-to-walkers/) describes levada walks as following "ancient water courses" that wind across the island, "always gently descending", in contrast to the craggy, rocky footpaths of the high peaks. That is the appeal for beginners: long stretches are close to flat.

Many of the best levadas run through the Laurissilva, the laurel forest that once covered much of southern Europe. [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/laurissilva-forest/) says it covers around 15,000 hectares, about a fifth of the island, mostly on the higher ground of the north, and that it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1999.

## How booking and fees work now

This is the part that catches people out. Madeira's regional nature institute, IFCN, manages a list of **classified walking routes**, coded PR for *pequena rota*. Since 2026, according to [IFCN's FAQ](https://ifcn.madeira.gov.pt/en/atividades-de-natureza/percursos-pedestres-recomendados/faq-s-taxas-percursos-pedestres-classificados/frequently-asked-questions-fees-classified-walking-routes.html):

- Each day on a classified IFCN trail is split into **entry time slots**, and you book the slot in which you want to start.
- Booking is **online only**, through the [SIMplifica portal](https://simplifica.madeira.gov.pt/simplifica/). Bookings are not made at IFCN's offices.
- Registration is compulsory for everyone, including those who don't pay. Children aged 12 and under are exempt from the fee but still need booking.
- Non-resident visitors pay a fee per trail, with day and multi-day rates for doing several. The FAQ lists the current amounts.
- Slots close once they are full, and a closed trail cannot be booked.
- Refunds or changes are only possible if the authorities close or restrict the trail. Rain, a missed bus or a change of heart means losing the fee.

The FAQ also says IFCN allows a tolerance of around 15 minutes either side of your slot. Book a few days ahead for the popular routes, and build the rest of your day around the slot you get.

Not every path on the island is classified, and IFCN says it only gives information on the routes it manages. For a first levada, stick to the official PR routes: they are signed, maintained and monitored.

## Four walks to start with

All of these are classified routes, described on Visit Madeira's hiking pages. Distances are one way: most levada walks are out-and-back, so double them. Status changes, sometimes for weeks at a time, so check each one before you plan around it.

### PR 11 Vereda dos Balcões

The gentlest introduction there is. [PR 11](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/activities/hiking/pr-11-vereda-dos-balcoes/) is rated easy and runs 1.5 km from the main road at Ribeiro Frio to the Balcões viewpoint, alongside the Levada da Serra do Faial. Visit Madeira allows about an hour and a half for the return trip. From the viewpoint you look over wooded valleys of Laurissilva and, on a clear day, up to Pico do Areeiro, Pico das Torres and Pico Ruivo. Back at Ribeiro Frio there is a forest park with a trout nursery.

### PR 6.2 Levada do Alecrim

A little longer and higher. [PR 6.2](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/activities/hiking/pr-62-levada-do-alecrim/) is rated easy, starts at Rabaçal and runs 3.5 km at around 1,300 metres, with wide views over the Rabaçal valley and tunnels of heather. It ends at the spring that feeds the levada and a small lake, the Lagoa Dona Beja. Allow two and a half hours. The Rabaçal area has had several closures, so check this one carefully.

### PR 18 Levada do Rei

A step up in length, though not in steepness. [PR 18](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/activities/hiking/pr-18-levada-do-rei/) on the north coast near São Jorge is rated moderate, at 5.3 km each way and about three and a half hours in total, with very little change in altitude. It runs through forest and tunnels of vegetation to Ribeiro Bonito, which Visit Madeira describes as an isolated sanctuary of Laurissilva. Near the end is a working water mill driven by the levada.

<Pair a="lvb-mossy-channel" b="lvb-levada-do-rei-waterfall" captionA="A moss-covered channel wall under an arch of trees." captionB="A thin waterfall in the forest along the Levada do Rei." />

### PR 9 Levada do Caldeirão Verde

Save this for when you know you like it. [PR 9](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/activities/hiking/pr-9-levada-do-caldeirao-verde/) starts at the Queimadas Forest Park above Santana and runs 8.7 km to the Caldeirão Verde waterfall and lake. Visit Madeira rates it moderate and allows six and a half hours for the return, with four rock-cut tunnels on the way. It is a full day, and a properly memorable one.

## What to pack

The [advice to walkers](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/advice-to-walkers/) is blunt: wear walking boots on every walk, and carry waterproofs, a hat, spare clothing, food and drink and a map. It adds that a torch is useful because some levada walks pass through tunnels. We would make that a head torch, so your hands are free on the wet, uneven floor.

Weather is the other thing to plan for. [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/useful-information/weather-in-madeira/) explains that the north and the mountains are more exposed to wind and rain than the south, and that you can be in fog up high while the coast is in sunshine. Funchal in shorts can mean Queimadas in a fleece. Check [IPMA's Madeira forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/otempo/prev-sam/?p=RAM) the evening before. Our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons more broadly.

## Staying safe

Many levada paths are narrow and some sections are unfenced, so the most important skill is knowing when to turn back. Visit Madeira's [notice to walkers](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/notice-to-walkers-and-list-of-walks/) sets out the basics:

- Check the route is open before you set out.
- Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back.
- Know how long the walk takes, so you finish before dark.
- Carry extra food and water, and a phone.
- In heavy rain or strong wind, don't go on. Turn back the way you came.

When we checked in November 2026, that notice listed several routes as closed, including Levada Velha do Rabaçal and the accessible "path for all" section of the Caldeirão Verde. Closures change, so look again on the day.

If you don't have a head for heights, choose the shorter, wider routes such as the Balcões walk, or go with a licensed local guide. Visit Madeira's own walking writer notes that a guide or transfer also makes one-way routes much easier to manage.

## Getting to the trailheads

Most trailheads are in the mountains or on the north coast, a long way from Funchal by road. Visit Madeira's [guide to levada hikes](https://visitmadeira.com/en/blog/ellie-green/the-best-off-the-beaten-path-levada-hikes-in-madeira/) suggests a hire car, a guide or a transfer, since parking at the busier trailheads can be tight. If you are planning a walking-led trip, consider a few nights outside the capital; our [guide to Funchal](/stories/funchal-guide/) and our comparison of [Azores or Madeira](/stories/azores-or-madeira/) both look at where to base yourself.

Once the levadas have you hooked, the obvious next step is the high mountain path between [Pico do Arieiro and Pico Ruivo](/stories/pico-arieiro-to-pico-ruivo/), a very different kind of walk.

## Before you book

- Check each route's status on the [notice to walkers](https://visitmadeira.com/en/what-to-do/nature-seekers/notice-to-walkers-and-list-of-walks/) before you plan, and again on the day.
- Book your slot on [SIMplifica](https://simplifica.madeira.gov.pt/simplifica/) a few days ahead, and register children too.
- Read [IFCN's FAQ](https://ifcn.madeira.gov.pt/en/atividades-de-natureza/percursos-pedestres-recomendados/faq-s-taxas-percursos-pedestres-classificados/frequently-asked-questions-fees-classified-walking-routes.html) on fees and refunds: changing your mind is not covered.
- Pack boots, a waterproof and a head torch, even for a short walk.
- Plan how you will get to and from the trailhead, especially for linear routes.
