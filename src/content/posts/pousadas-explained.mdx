There is a particular kind of quiet you only find inside very thick walls. You step out of an Alentejo afternoon, white-hot and buzzing with cicadas, through a stone doorway into a cloister, and the temperature drops and the sound falls away. Your room was once a monk's cell. Your breakfast will be served in what used to be the refectory. That, in a sentence, is the pleasure of a pousada.

Portugal's pousadas are a network of hotels set in historic buildings: castles, convents, monasteries and palaces, with a few modern buildings in remarkable places. They are one of the most memorable ways to stay in the country, and one of the most misunderstood. Here is what they are, where they came from, and how to choose one.

## What a pousada is (and is not)

The word pousada simply means an inn, a place to rest. Today, Pousadas de Portugal is a hotel brand, and its own site describes it as [an invitation to delve into the country's history and tradition](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/pousadas-portugal), with hotels located in historic buildings including castles, monasteries and palaces. The same page puts the network at 35 pousadas across the country.

One thing to know before you search: a **pousada de juventude** is something else entirely. That is the Portuguese name for a youth hostel, and the national network has [its own website](https://www.pousadasjuventude.pt/en/). If you want a castle, make sure you are looking at Pousadas de Portugal.

## Where the idea came from

The pousadas began in the 1940s. According to the history published by the [Pestana group](https://stories.pestana.com/en/eight-decades-of-pousadas-de-portugal/), which runs the network today, the project was driven by António Ferro, director of the National Propaganda Service, who wanted Portugal to invest in tourism as Spain had begun to do. The first pousadas were modest regional inns offering what was called rustic comfort: hospitality in tune with local culture and food. Elvas, in the Alentejo, was the very first to be built.

The historic pousadas came next. The same account explains that from the 1950s the regional inns were joined by historic pousadas housed in classified monuments, many of them in poor repair, so that converting them into hotels also saved them. The first was the castle at Óbidos, opened in 1951. Palaces, monasteries and more castles followed.

<Pair a="pous-obidos-towers" b="pous-obidos-walls" captionA="The towers of Óbidos castle, home to the first historic pousada since 1951." captionB="Óbidos castle rising above the town's white houses and terracotta roofs." />

For decades the state built the pousadas and handed their day-to-day running to operators. After the 1974 revolution, the state tourism company ENATUR took over management. In 2003, according to [Pestana](https://stories.pestana.com/en/eight-decades-of-pousadas-de-portugal/), the government passed the management of the network to the private sector, and the concession went to the Pestana group through a public tender.

## The kinds of building you can stay in

The joy of the pousadas is their variety. Some highlights, all described on the brand's own pages:

### Castles

- **Óbidos.** The [Pousada Castelo Óbidos](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-obidos) is inside the castle of the walled medieval town. The pousada notes that King Dinis gave the castle to Queen Isabel as a wedding present, and that guests can choose between rooms in the castle itself and the Casa do Castelo just beside it.
- **Estremoz.** The [Pousada Castelo Estremoz](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-estremoz) occupies a castle built by Dom Dinis for Queen Isabel in the 13th century, with a pool surrounded by the castle walls.

### Convents and monasteries

- **Évora.** At the [Pousada Convento Évora](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-evora), in the heart of the historic city, the rooms are the old monks' cells, with only their function and decor changed, so almost no two are alike. The pool sits in the cloister courtyard.
- **Amares, near Braga.** The [Pousada Mosteiro Amares](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-amares) is a restored 12th-century Cistercian monastery, rebuilt by the architect Eduardo Souto de Moura, winner of the 2011 Pritzker Prize.
- **Crato.** The [Pousada Mosteiro Crato](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-crato) is a Gothic monastery in the Alentejo, where some of the historic rooms are inside the castellated towers.
- **Tavira.** In the Algarve, the [Pousada Convento Tavira](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-tavira) grew out of the reconstruction of the Convent of Nossa Senhora da Graça; restoration work uncovered the remains of an Almohad-era neighbourhood beside it.

### Palaces and grand buildings

- **Estoi, near Faro.** The [Pousada Palácio Estoi](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-estoi) is a restored 18th-century palace with French-style gardens, and its restaurant is in the old palace kitchen.
- **Lisbon.** The [Pousada Lisboa](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-lisboa) sits on Praça do Comércio, a few metres from the Tagus, in a building that the pousada says was once a royal residence and the seat of ministries.
- **Viseu.** The [Pousada Viseu](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-viseu) occupies the former São Teotónio hospital, dating from 1842, rebuilt by the architect Gonçalo Byrne in 2009.

### Places chosen for the view

Not every pousada is a monument. Some are about the setting. The [Pousada Marvão](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-marvao) is in the historic centre of the walled hill village of Marvão, and the pousada points out that from the castle and the pousada, at over 860 metres above sea level, you look out over the Alentejo plains and into Spain.

## Choosing the right pousada

The brand's site labels each property with a style, such as Monument Hotel, Historic Hotel or Charming Hotel. That is a useful first filter, but think about how you travel:

- **City or country?** City pousadas such as Lisbon, Évora and Viseu are easy to reach without a car and put you on the doorstep of the sights. Rural ones such as Crato, Amares and Marvão are about peace and landscape, and you will almost certainly need a car. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers car hire and driving.
- **One night or a base?** A single night in a castle is a wonderful stop on a road trip. If you want a base, choose a pousada with a pool, a restaurant and easy day trips.
- **Room type matters.** In historic buildings, rooms vary a great deal in size, shape and view. At Évora, for example, the pousada says almost all of its converted cells are different. Read the room descriptions carefully, and pay attention to which wing or building a room is in.
- **Seasonal facilities.** Pools and outdoor bars are often summer-only; the [Guimarães pousada](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-guimaraes), for example, describes its outdoor pool and terrace as something to enjoy in the summer months. Check what is open on your dates.

## Eating at a pousada

From the start, food was part of the idea. The Pestana history describes the first pousadas as places of hospitality in tune with [local culture and gastronomy](https://stories.pestana.com/en/eight-decades-of-pousadas-de-portugal/), and many pousada restaurants still lean into regional cooking: Alentejo dishes at Évora and Crato, local cuisine at Guimarães. For a sense of what to order, region by region, see our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/).

## Pousadas and the rest of your trip

A pousada night works best as a highlight rather than a habit. Mix them with city guesthouses and smaller hotels, and save the castle or convent for the places where the building itself is the point. In Lisbon, for example, you might choose between the grandeur of the riverside square and a neighbourhood stay; our guide to [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) compares the city's neighbourhoods.

## Before you book

- Make sure you are on the Pousadas de Portugal site, [pousadas.pt](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/pousadas-portugal), and not a youth hostel listing.
- Read the room descriptions closely: in converted monuments, rooms differ in size, layout and view.
- Check whether the pool, restaurant or bar you are looking forward to is open on your dates.
- For rural pousadas, plan for a car and check the driving time from your previous stop.
- Book early if you are travelling on summer weekends or public holidays.
