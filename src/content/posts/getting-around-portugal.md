There is a particular pleasure in pulling out of Lisbon's Santa Apolónia station with a coffee in hand, the Tagus widening on your left, knowing you will be in Porto before you have finished your book. Portugal is a compact country with a decent rail spine, two good city metro systems and a dense web of coaches. It is also a country where a car opens up places no timetable will take you.

The trick is knowing which tool suits which part of the trip. Here is how we think about it.

## Trains: the backbone

Portugal's national rail operator is CP (Comboios de Portugal), and for most first trips its long-distance trains are the easiest way to link the big cities.

### Alfa Pendular

The [Alfa Pendular](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/alfa-pendular-train) is CP's fastest service, running the length of the country between Braga and Faro, with Lisbon to Porto the journey most visitors take. Seats recline, there are power sockets at every seat, free Wi-Fi and a bar service on board. If you are doing the classic Lisbon and Porto combination, this is the train you want.

### Intercidades

The [Intercidades](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/intercidades-train) trains are slightly slower but reach further: from the Minho in the north to the Algarve, with branches into the Alentejo, the Beiras and the Centro. These are the trains for Évora, Coimbra and the Algarve when an Alfa Pendular isn't running at a time that suits you.

### Regional and urban trains

Regional and InterRegional trains fill in the smaller stations. Around the two big cities, urban trains do the heavy lifting: from Lisbon, the [Sintra and Cascais lines](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/lisbon) are how most visitors reach the palaces of Sintra and the beaches of the Cascais coast.

### The Douro by train

One of Europe's loveliest railway journeys runs east from Porto along the Douro. CP's [MiraDouro](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/miradouro-train) service uses restored vintage carriages and runs daily in both directions between Porto São Bento, Régua, Pinhão and Pocinho, with the river and the terraced vineyards beside you most of the way. Check the current timetable and fares on CP's site before you go.

### Booking

Long-distance trains can fill up on busy dates, so book ahead where you can. Through the [CP App](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/terms-cp-app) or CP's online ticket office you can buy Alfa Pendular, Intercidades, Regional and InterRegional tickets up to 60 days in advance and up to 15 minutes before departure. The same page sets out the rules for exchanging tickets. Check the current fare on CP's site rather than relying on old figures; promotional fares come and go.

## Lisbon and Porto: metro, cards and contactless

### Lisbon

Lisbon's metro is clean, simple and covers most of the places you will want to reach that aren't on a tram line. You have three main ways to pay, all set out on the [Metropolitano de Lisboa ticket page](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/buy/):

- **A contactless bank card**, tapped at the gates. It covers one person per journey, so each traveller needs their own card. Take it out of your wallet before you tap, to avoid paying with the wrong one.
- **A navegante occasional card**, a reusable card you top up with tickets or with "zapping" pay-as-you-go credit, which also works across other member operators.
- **24-hour tickets**, including combined options covering buses, trams, the metro and some CP suburban lines.

Machines and ticket offices in stations sell and top up cards. Lisbon is very hilly; the metro and the trams will save your legs more than you expect.

### Porto

Porto's metro uses the Andante ticketing system, explained on [Metro do Porto's ticket page](https://en.metrodoporto.pt/pages/397). The basics:

- You buy a reusable Andante card, then load it with trips for a given number of **zones**. Check at the departure station how many zones your journey needs.
- You validate each time you start a trip, and the ticket is then good for a set time within those zones.
- **Andante 24** gives you unlimited travel for 24 hours in the zones you choose, and **Andante Tour** tickets give unlimited travel across the network for one or three days.

One card holds one type of ticket at a time, and each person needs their own.

## Intercity coaches

Coaches go where trains don't, and in parts of the interior they are the only public transport. The biggest national network is [Rede Expressos](https://rede-expressos.pt/en), which links Lisbon and Porto with towns across the mainland and sells tickets online. Several other operators, including international coach companies, run on the main corridors too.

Coaches are especially useful for the Alentejo, the interior of the Centro and smaller Algarve towns away from the railway. Book online and keep your ticket on your phone, and check whether your ticket is in a named passenger's name and what ID you need to carry.

## Driving

### Tolls, and the electronic-only ones

Many of Portugal's motorways are tolled, and they use two different systems, explained on [PT Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/en), the toll information site run by Infraestruturas de Portugal:

- **Traditional tolls** have booths. You take a ticket on entry and pay on exit, by cash or card, or use the electronic lanes if you have an active payment device.
- **Electronic-only tolls** have no booths at all. Gantries over the road register your car as you pass, and a panel near each gantry shows the charge. These stretches are signposted, and PT Tolls lists which motorways use the system.

If you drive a **foreign-registered car** on an electronic-only motorway, PT Tolls says you must have an active means of payment. The options it lists include linking a bank card to your number plate (Easytoll), prepaid Tollcards and Via Verde subscriptions; administrative fees apply to some of them. PT Tolls also explains what to do if you think you have passed through a toll without paying.

If you hire a car in Portugal, ask the rental company how tolls are handled before you drive away: whether the car has a toll device fitted, and how and when you will be charged.

### When a car makes sense

We would hire a car for:

- **The Alentejo.** Distances are long, villages are small and the joy is in the in-between: cork groves, hilltop castles, a lunch stop you didn't plan.
- **The Algarve interior and the far west.** The coast towns are linked by train and bus, but the hill villages and the wild beaches towards the Costa Vicentina are far easier with your own wheels.
- **The Douro Valley.** The MiraDouro train is wonderful for the river itself, but visiting quintas and viewpoints up in the hills is much easier by car, ideally with a designated non-drinking driver.

We would not hire a car for **central Lisbon or Porto**. Streets are steep, narrow and often one-way, parking is hard to find, and the metro, trams, trains and your own feet will do a better job. If your trip combines cities and countryside, pick the car up on the day you leave the city, not the day you land.

## Flying to the islands

Madeira and the Azores are reached by air from the mainland.

- **Madeira.** The main gateway is the international airport at Santa Cruz, near Funchal. According to [Visit Madeira](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/how-to-get-here/), the flight from Lisbon takes about 90 minutes.
- **The Azores.** [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/plan-your-visit) puts the archipelago about two hours' flight from Lisbon and Porto.

## Getting around Madeira

Madeira's tourism board is clear that [hiring a car is the most independent option](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/how-to-move-around/), but not the only one. Buses, taxis, ride-hailing apps and organised excursions all work, particularly if you are staying around Funchal. A few useful points from the same page:

- There are **no toll roads** on Madeira; expressways and main roads are free.
- Main roads are straightforward, but some mountain roads are very winding and need care.
- Street parking is colour-coded, and paid bays are usually charged on weekdays and Saturday mornings.
- From the airport to Funchal you can take the Aerobus, a taxi, a transfer or a hire car, in around 20 to 25 minutes.
- Cable cars link Funchal with some of the island's best-known viewpoints.

For **Porto Santo**, the neighbouring island with the long golden beach, there is a short air shuttle from Madeira or the *Lobo Marinho* ferry, which takes [around two and a half hours](https://visitmadeira.com/en/travel-info/how-to-get-here/).

## Getting around the Azores

On the larger islands, a hire car is the easiest way to reach crater lakes, hot springs and trailheads; [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/plan-your-visit) describes plenty of kilometres of quiet road to explore. Between islands, you can travel by **regional flights** and, on some routes, by **boat**. Check inter-island options early and plan these links before you fix the rest of your itinerary, and leave some slack: Atlantic weather can delay both planes and ferries.

## Before you book

- Book Alfa Pendular and Intercidades seats ahead for busy dates, and check the current fare on [CP's ticket pages](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/terms-cp-app).
- In Lisbon, decide between a contactless card and a navegante card; in Porto, work out your Andante zones.
- If you are driving, read [PT Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/en) and set up toll payment before you join an electronic-only motorway, or confirm the rental company's toll arrangements.
- Hire cars for the countryside, not the city centres.
- For the islands, book flights and inter-island links first, then build the rest of the trip around them.
