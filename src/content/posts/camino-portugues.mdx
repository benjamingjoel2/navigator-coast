The first yellow arrow is easy to miss. It might be painted on a kerb in the old streets of Porto, or on the corner of a granite wall, pointing north. Follow it, and the next one, and the one after that, and within two weeks or so you will be standing in front of the cathedral in Santiago de Compostela, with a credential full of stamps and a very different relationship with your feet.

The Camino Portugués has become one of the most popular routes to Santiago, and it is easy to see why. It starts in one of Europe's most likeable cities, passes through the green, granite country of the Minho, crosses a river into Spain and finishes in Galicia's pilgrim capital. Here is how to plan it.

## Two ways north: central or coastal

From Porto there are two main options, and both are now officially recognised in Portugal.

The **central route** heads inland through Barcelos and Ponte de Lima to the border at Valença. Turismo de Portugal reports that the [Caminho Central in the Porto and North region](https://business.turismodeportugal.pt/pt/Conhecer/programas-iniciativas/caminhos-da-fe/Paginas/certificado-caminho-portugues-santiago-central-porto-norte.aspx) was certified in December 2023, covering 177.8 kilometres in 12 walking stages between Oliveira de Azeméis and Valença, with Porto, Barcelos and Ponte de Lima recognised as historic routes along it.

The **coastal route** follows the Atlantic shore north from Porto through fishing towns and beach resorts. According to Turismo de Portugal, the [Caminho da Costa](https://business.turismodeportugal.pt/pt/Conhecer/programas-iniciativas/caminhos-da-fe/Paginas/certificado-caminho-portugues-santiago-caminho-costa.aspx) was certified in February 2022 and runs for 137.9 kilometres in nine stages between Porto and the border with Galicia at Valença.

<Pair a="cp-vila-do-conde-chapel" b="cp-vila-do-conde-shore" captionA="A small chapel in Vila do Conde, an early stop on the coastal route." captionB="Waves on the rocks at Vila do Conde, where the coastal Camino meets the Atlantic." />

Which to choose? The central route is the classic pilgrimage way: more villages, more churches, more woodland and farmland, and more of the traditional pilgrim infrastructure. The coast offers sea air, boardwalks and long beaches, and tends to be breezier and cooler on hot days. Many walkers mix the two, starting on the coast and cutting inland, which is perfectly possible as long as you follow the rules for the Compostela described below.

## Starting in Porto

Most walkers begin at the cathedral, the Sé, on its hill above the Ribeira. The [Pousada Porto Rua das Flores](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-porto), in the historic centre, describes the cathedral as a Romanesque-Gothic building dating from the 12th and 13th centuries with Baroque alterations, and notes its rose window and old baptismal fonts.

Give yourself at least a day in the city before you set off. Our [Porto in 48 hours](/stories/porto-in-48-hours/) itinerary covers the essentials, and [where to stay in Porto](/stories/where-to-stay-in-porto/) compares the neighbourhoods. The first day out of the city involves a fair amount of urban walking on either route, which is why some walkers take public transport through the suburbs and start on foot further north; if you are aiming for a Compostela, check that you will still walk enough of the route (see below).

## The central route: Barcelos, Ponte de Lima and the Minho

Once clear of the city, the central route settles into the rhythm that makes the Camino addictive. Paths and quiet lanes wind between vineyards, maize fields and stone walls, past wayside crosses and granite villages, with a café never too far away.

Barcelos, a market town on the River Cávado, is a traditional overnight stop. Ponte de Lima, further north, is one of the loveliest towns on the whole route, its long medieval bridge crossing the River Lima beside a broad riverside avenue. Beyond Ponte de Lima the route climbs over the wooded hills of the Serra da Labruja before dropping towards the River Minho.

This is vinho verde country, and our [Minho and vinho verde guide](/stories/minho-and-vinho-verde/) is a good companion for the region. If you have time before or after, nearby [Guimarães and Braga](/stories/guimaraes-and-braga/) are both worth a detour.

## Valença and Tui: crossing into Galicia

Both Portuguese routes converge on Valença, a fortified town whose walls look across the Minho to Tui in Spain. The [Pousada de Valença](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-de-valenca) sits within the fortress, next to the walls on the north side of the town, above the river.

<Figure id="cp-valenca-walls" caption="A watchtower on the star-shaped walls of the fortress at Valença, the last Portuguese town on the route." />

Crossing the bridge into Tui, with its fortress-like cathedral on the hill, is one of the Camino's great moments.

## The Galician section: Tui to Santiago

On the Spanish side the route is managed and waymarked by Galicia. The Xunta's page on [the Portuguese Way](https://www.caminodesantiago.gal/en/make-plans/the-ways/portuguese-way) lists it at 118.8 kilometres, rated medium-low in difficulty, with stages from Tui to O Porriño, Redondela, Pontevedra, Caldas de Reis, Padrón and finally Santiago de Compostela.

<Pair a="cp-galicia-waymark" b="cp-santiago-towers" captionA="A granite waymark in Galicia, with the scallop shell and yellow arrow." captionB="The towers of Santiago cathedral, the end of the road." />

The history runs deep. The same page explains that the route grew in importance from the 12th century, after Portugal's independence, and follows ancient roads including the Roman Via XIX, which linked Braga with Astorga through Ponte de Lima, Tui, Pontevedra, Santiago and Lugo. Elizabeth of Portugal made the pilgrimage in the 14th century, and King Manuel I travelled from Lisbon in 1502.

If you prefer to stay by the sea, there is a Galician coastal variant too. Galicia's page on [the Portuguese Way of the coast](https://www.caminodesantiago.gal/en/make-plans/the-ways/portuguese-coastal-way) describes it entering Galicia at A Guarda, after crossing the mouth of the Minho, and passing Oia, Baiona and Vigo before joining the main Portuguese Way at Redondela. Baiona, the page notes, was the first town to learn of the success of Columbus's first voyage, when the Pinta arrived there on 1 March 1493.

## The credential and the Compostela

You need a pilgrim credential, the credencial, before you start. The Pilgrim's Reception Office in Santiago explains that [the credencial](https://oficinadelperegrino.com/en/pilgrimage/the-credencial/) identifies you as a pilgrim, gives access to pilgrim hostels and is used to certify your journey. It is issued by the office itself and by authorised institutions such as parish churches, Camino associations and pilgrim hostels, and there is also a digital version via an app. Stamps are usually collected where you sleep, and also at churches, town halls, hotels and other places along the way.

The Compostela, the certificate of completion, has specific rules. According to the [Pilgrim's Office](https://oficinadelperegrino.com/en/pilgrimage/the-compostela/), walkers must complete at least 100 continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago, and cyclists 200 kilometres. Your credential should be stamped at the start and end of each stage. The office is at Rúa Carretas 33 in Santiago and opens daily from 10:00 to 19:00, except 25 December and 1 January. You collect a ticket with a QR code to queue, and in busy periods same-day collection is not guaranteed.

Tui to Santiago is just over that 100-kilometre minimum, which is why it is such a popular shorter start. Walking from Porto gives you the whole story.

## Where to sleep

Accommodation ranges from public and parish hostels to guesthouses, rural houses and hotels. In Galicia, the Xunta runs a [network of public hostels](https://www.caminodesantiago.gal/en/on-the-way/network-of-public-hostels) along the routes. In Portugal there is a mix of municipal, parish and private options. Booking at least the first and last few nights is sensible, especially in popular overnight towns such as Ponte de Lima and Valença, and in Porto and Santiago themselves.

## When to walk

Our own preference is late spring or early autumn, when days are long and walking temperatures are usually comfortable. Summer can be hot on the inland stages, and the north-west is green for a reason: expect rain at any time of year, and more of it in winter. Check [IPMA's forecasts](https://www.ipma.pt/en/) for the Portuguese stages as you go. Our [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) guide explains the train links between Porto and the north if you want to return or skip a stretch.

## Before you book

- Choose your route: central for villages and tradition, coastal for sea views, or a mix.
- Get your credential before your first day and collect stamps every day; read the [Compostela rules](https://oficinadelperegrino.com/en/pilgrimage/the-compostela/) if you plan to skip any stretch.
- Allow a rest day in Porto at the start and a day in Santiago at the end.
- Book beds ahead for busy months, especially in the towns where many walkers stop.
- Break in your boots and keep your pack light; you will carry it every day.
- Pack waterproofs whatever the season.
