The first sign is the smoke. On a warm evening in a Portuguese fishing town, it drifts out of side streets from charcoal grills set up on the pavement, carrying the smell of hot oil, salt and blistering fish skin. Follow it and you will usually find a cook in a stained apron turning sardines or a whole sea bream over the coals, a stack of plates, and a table of locals who knew exactly where to come.

Portugal is a nation of fish-eaters, and it cooks from the sea with very little fuss. The best seafood here is often the simplest: grilled over charcoal, dressed with olive oil and lemon, served with boiled potatoes and a tomato and onion salad. Knowing what to order, and when, is most of the battle.

## The rules of the counter

A few habits will make every seafood meal easier.

- **Shellfish is often priced by weight.** On many menus, prawns, crab, lobster, clams and barnacles are listed per kilo rather than per plate. Ask how much a portion weighs before you order, so there are no surprises.
- **Fish may be priced by weight too.** Whole grilled fish, such as sea bream or sea bass, is often brought to the table for you to choose, then weighed and charged accordingly.
- **The little dishes are not free.** Bread, butter, olives and small plates that arrive before you order are charged if you eat them. Send back what you do not want.
- **Look for the grill.** A restaurant with a charcoal grill by the door, especially near a harbour, is generally a good sign.

## Sardines: the taste of summer

No fish is more Portuguese than the sardine, and no sardine is better than one grilled whole over charcoal in summer, when the fish are fat and the oil runs into the bread underneath. Lisbon's June celebrations are built around them: Visit Lisboa calls grilled sardines [an essential feature](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/events/st-anthonys-night-12-13th-june) of the street parties on St Anthony's night, 12 to 13 June, and the wider [Festas de Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/pt-pt/eventos/festas-de-lisboa-1) fill the month with music and smoke.

Sardine fishing is tightly managed. It is run jointly by Portugal and Spain with annual catch limits, and in 2026 the government's [order reopening the fishery](https://dgrm.pt/documents/20143/121101/Despacho+sardinha+2026.pdf/bcde08e1-0883-1c67-52f5-108132839cc8) set the restart for 4 May. The same order bans catching, landing and selling sardines on national holidays. In practice, that means fresh sardines are a late-spring and summer pleasure. Eat them in winter and they may well have come from the freezer, which is worth knowing even if the restaurant does not mention it.

How to eat them: lay one on a slice of bread, peel the flesh from the backbone with a knife and fork (or your fingers), and let the bread soak up the juices. Old hands eat the crisp skin too.

Outside sardine season, tinned fish fills the gap. Portugal's canning tradition is old and proud, and good tinned sardines, mackerel and tuna in olive oil are a staple, not a compromise.

## Clams, barnacles and the shellfish board

### Amêijoas à Bulhão Pato

If you order one shellfish dish in Lisbon, make it this: clams opened in a pan with olive oil, plenty of garlic, white wine or lemon and a fistful of fresh coriander. The real pleasure is the broth at the bottom of the bowl, so keep some bread back for it.

### Percebes

Goose barnacles look like a cross between a claw and a small reptile, and taste of the cleanest sea you can imagine. Twist off the leathery sleeve and eat the pink stalk inside. They are hard to gather, which is why they are expensive. Turismo do Algarve's [cuisine guide](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf) says they are prised off the rocks with special knives, and that seafood here is costly largely because it is caught in hard-to-reach places.

### The marisqueira

A *marisqueira* is a seafood house, and a *cervejaria* is a beer hall that often specialises in shellfish too. Expect steel trays of boiled prawns, crab, clams and lobster, ordered by weight, and cold beer. In Lisbon, [Cervejaria Ramiro](https://www.cervejariaramiro.com/), on Avenida Almirante Reis at the edge of the Baixa, is one of the best-known; its site lists opening hours from Tuesday to Sunday. Finish the Lisbon way with a *prego*, a steak sandwich, as dessert.

## The Algarve: cataplana, octopus and the lagoon

The south has its own seafood culture, shaped by tuna, octopus and the shellfish of the lagoons. Turismo do Algarve's [cuisine guide](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf) is a good primer, and several things stand out.

- **Cataplana.** Named after the hinged copper or brass pan it is cooked in, which seals like a clam so the contents steam in their own juices. The guide says its origins are uncertain but probably go back to the Moorish period. Clams with pork, or mixed fish and shellfish, are the classic fillings. It is usually made for two or more and takes time, so order it early.
- **Octopus.** The guide describes Algarve octopus as big, meaty and tasty, and lists it in salads, cataplanas, and grilled or baked. Octopus salad with coriander and onion is a lovely light lunch.
- **Tuna.** Fresh tuna steaks with onions are a regional dish, and the guide explains the old preserving traditions too, from cured *muxama* to salted *estupeta*.
- **The lagoon.** The guide names the Ria Formosa Natural Park, created in 1987, and the Ria de Alvor as important sources of shellfish such as clams, cockles, razor clams and oysters. The park authority ICNF's [guide to the Ria Formosa](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f61c223d0d89e467) covers its trails and wildlife if you want to see where it all comes from.

For a first look at the raw ingredients, go to a market in the morning. The [Mercado Municipal de Loulé](https://geoparquealgarvensis.pt/patrimonio-cultura/56-patrimonio-arquitetonico/846-mercado-municipal-de-loule-en), opened in 1908 in a Moorish-revival building, is open Monday to Saturday from 7am to 3pm, according to the Geoparque Algarvensis. If you are visiting in the cooler months, our guide to the [Algarve in winter](/stories/algarve-in-winter/) explains which towns stay lively.

## Porto and Matosinhos: the grill by the sea

Porto's seafood capital is not really in Porto at all. Just north, at the mouth of the Leça, Matosinhos has a working port, a fish auction and street after street of restaurants grilling outside their front doors. The municipality's [gastronomy page](https://www.cm-matosinhos.pt/gastronomia) says the Lota de Matosinhos, the fish auction, guarantees a steady supply of fish and shellfish, that the town had 59 canning factories in the early 20th century, and that it now has more than 500 restaurants.

The drill is simple: pick a busy grill, ask what came in that morning, and choose from the display. Sea bream, sea bass, turbot, sole, red mullet and squid are all regulars. In town, the [Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/) is the place for a look at the stalls, open Monday to Saturday and closed on Sundays. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) covers the rest of the northern table.

## Lisbon: from market hall to tasca

Lisbon's seafood runs from humble to grand. In the neighbourhood *tascas*, look for grilled fish of the day, fried horse mackerel (*carapaus*) with tomato rice, and *açorda de marisco*, a garlicky bread soup thick with prawns. For a one-stop introduction, [Time Out Market Lisboa](https://www.timeout.com/time-out-market-lisboa), inside the Mercado da Ribeira building at Cais do Sodré, opened in 2014 and gathers dozens of restaurants and bars under one roof; it lists opening hours of 10am to midnight daily, with later closing from Thursday to Saturday. If you have not yet decided between the two cities, our [Lisbon or Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) comparison helps.

And then there is the fish Portugal does not catch at home. Salt cod deserves its own article, and it has one: read our guide to [bacalhau](/stories/bacalhau/) before you face a menu with twenty versions of it.

## A seasonal cheat sheet

| Season | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Spring | Clams and other shellfish; the sardine fishery reopened on 4 May in 2026 |
| Summer | Grilled sardines at their best, especially at the June festivals; octopus and tuna in the Algarve |
| Autumn | Grilled fish of all kinds; the last of the season's sardines, while the catch limit lasts |
| Winter | Hearty fish stews, cataplana, octopus and bacalhau; treat fresh sardines with suspicion |

## Before you book

- Planning around sardines? Aim for June to September, and check the [Festas de Lisboa](https://www.visitlisboa.com/pt-pt/eventos/festas-de-lisboa-1) programme for the year's dates.
- Check opening days before you set your heart on a restaurant or market: many seafood restaurants close one day a week, and the big markets close on Sundays.
- Ask for the price per kilo and the weight of your portion when ordering shellfish or whole fish.
- Book ahead for well-known seafood restaurants on Friday and Saturday nights.
- If you have a shellfish allergy, learn the word *marisco* and say so clearly; seafood turns up in rice dishes, soups and stews.
