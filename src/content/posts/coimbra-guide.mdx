Stand on the Santa Clara bank of the Mondego in the late afternoon and Coimbra arranges itself for you. The river is wide and slow, the old town rises from it in a stack of pale walls and terracotta roofs, and at the very top, where the hill runs out, sits the university, its tower catching the last of the sun. It is one of the great urban views in Portugal, and most people see it from a train window on the way to somewhere else.

<Figure id="coim-city-view" caption="Coimbra's old town on its hill, with the Mondego below and the hills of the Centro beyond." />

We think Coimbra deserves a night at least. It is a city of students, steep lanes and long histories, and it changes character after dark, when the day-trippers have gone and the cafés fill up. It is also easy to reach: halfway between Lisbon and Porto, and a natural break on any north–south trip, as in our [ten-day Portugal itinerary](/stories/ten-day-portugal-itinerary/).

## A capital, then a university town

Coimbra was once the capital of the Portuguese kingdom, as the [Center of Portugal tourism board](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/coimbra) puts it, and the city wears that early importance in its stone. But what defines it now is learning. CP's [heritage page on Coimbra](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/coimbra-heritage) notes that the university was founded in 1290 by King Dinis, making it the oldest in the country, and that UNESCO inscribed the University of Coimbra – Alta and Sofia as a World Heritage Site in 2013.

That name matters. The listing covers not just the university buildings but the Alta, the old upper town around them, and Rua da Sofia down in the lower town. In other words, a good part of the city is the monument.

## The old university

Everything starts at the top of the hill, in the Paço das Escolas, the former royal palace that became the university's home. Its great courtyard opens on one side to a view across the river, and on the other to the clock tower and the colonnaded Via Latina.

<Figure id="coim-paco-escolas" caption="The Paço das Escolas courtyard, with the university tower on the left." />

The university sells visits to its historic spaces through its own [visitor site](https://visit.uc.pt/en/program-list). The main programmes combine the Royal Palace, the Chapel of St Michael and the Science Museum spaces, with or without the Joanina Library, and there are guided tours of the palace and the Botanical Garden. All programmes are listed as non-refundable.

### The Joanina Library

The library is the reason many people come. According to [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/joanine-library), it was ordered by King João V in 1717, and it is a baroque interior of gilded and painted wood in three rooms, each with its own background colour. The same page explains how the building protects its books: walls more than two metres thick, a teak door, and a colony of bats that comes out after closing to eat the insects, which is why the tables are covered at night.

Plan this part carefully. The university's [visitor information page](https://visit.uc.pt/en/info) says Joanina tickets are for a set date and time, chosen when you buy, and that it is the only space with a fixed slot. Photography and filming are not allowed on the library's noble floor, and flash, tripods and selfie sticks are banned throughout. The same page lists the hours and the days the spaces close, including dates around Christmas and New Year and around the student festivities; the tower was listed as closed when we checked.

## Down through the Alta

From the university, walk down rather than up. The Alta is a tangle of steep, stepped streets and small squares, and it is lovelier on the way down, with the river appearing at the end of a lane every few minutes.

Halfway down is the Sé Velha, the old cathedral. [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/coimbra-old-cathedral-se-velha-de-coimbra) describes it as a treasure of Portuguese Romanesque, with an entrance that looks like a fortress gate, and calls it the only Portuguese cathedral built during the Reconquista that has survived intact. Look inside for the carved wooden altarpiece and the Gothic cloister.

At the bottom of the hill, the lower town is flatter, busier and full of shops. Its anchor is Praça 8 de Maio and the monastery church of Santa Cruz. The [Center of Portugal page on the monastery](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/santa-cruz-de-coimbra-monastery) says it was founded in the reign of Afonso Henriques, Portugal's first king, who chose it as his burial place; his son Sancho I was buried there too. Much of what you see is the later Manueline rebuilding that began in 1507.

<Figure id="coim-santa-cruz" caption="The carved façade of Santa Cruz in the lower town, in evening light." />

## Across the river

The Santa Clara bank is where you get that view back to the city, and it has its own sights. The ruined Gothic monastery of Santa Clara-a-Velha was abandoned to the river: [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/santa-clara-a-velha-monastery) records repeated flooding, the nuns leaving in 1677, and the site reopening in 2009 after restoration with an interpretive centre.

Nearby is [Portugal dos Pequenitos](https://www.portugaldospequenitos.pt/), a park of miniature buildings. The [tourism board](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/portugal-dos-pequenitos) says it was built in 1940, designed by Cassiano Branco, with small-scale regional houses from the mainland and islands alongside famous Portuguese monuments. It is aimed at children, but adults rarely leave without photographs.

<Figure id="coim-mondego-bridge" caption="From the Santa Clara side, the whole of the old town rises above the Mondego." />

## Fado, cafés and where to stay

Coimbra has its own fado, more serenade than lament, and bound up with the student tradition of black capes and late-night singing. The easiest place to hear it is right on Praça 8 de Maio. [Café Santa Cruz](https://cafesantacruz.com/index-en/) occupies a building of recognised historical value next to the church; it says newspapers were writing about it in 1921, when they called it "O Café Manuelino". Its site lists Coimbra fado sessions every day at 6pm and 10pm, with free entry, and no late session on Sundays. It also makes its own sweet, the crúzio, from a recipe dating from the 1950s.

For the night, two very different options. [Quinta das Lágrimas](https://www.quintadaslagrimas.pt/en/) is a hotel in an eighteenth-century palace set in large gardens, and it is tied to the legend of Prince Pedro and Inês de Castro, the most famous love story in Portuguese history. In the lower town, the [Astória](https://www.almeidahotels.pt/en/hotel-astoria-in-coimbra/), which its owners describe as an Art Nouveau and Art Déco building, has been a city landmark for close to a century.

Coimbra is also a good base for eating your way through the Centro: suckling pig from the Bairrada to the north, and the egg-rich convent sweets that run through this part of the country. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) covers the regional dishes worth ordering.

## Getting there and around

Coimbra sits on the main railway between Lisbon and Porto. The [Center of Portugal page](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/coimbra) gives the distances as 198 km from Lisbon and 143 km from Porto. CP's [Coimbra heritage page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/coimbra-heritage) notes that tickets can be bought online up to 60 days ahead, in the CP app or at station ticket offices.

One thing to check when you book is which Coimbra station your train uses. CP's [Coimbra urban trains page](https://www.cp.pt/info/coimbra-trains) explains that local trains connect with the Alfa Pendular, Intercidades and regional services at the main stations, and that Coimbra-B links with the city's buses. The current timetables are on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf). For a fuller picture of the trains and coaches, see our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

Once you are here, walk. The old town is compact but steep, and the cobbles are slippery when wet, so wear shoes with some grip.

## When to go

Coimbra is a year-round city, and term time is when it feels most like itself. The student calendar has peaks of its own: the university's [visitor page](https://visit.uc.pt/en/info) lists closures for the academic festivities in May and October, a reminder that the city is busy and the historic spaces may be shut on those days. Spring and early autumn are comfortable for walking the hill; for a broader view of the seasons, see our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/).

## Before you book

- Buy a Joanina Library ticket for a fixed time slot through the [university's visitor site](https://visit.uc.pt/en/program-list), and build the rest of your day around it.
- Check the [university's opening hours and closure dates](https://visit.uc.pt/en/info) for your visit, especially around Christmas, New Year and the student festivities.
- Check which Coimbra station your train stops at, and how you will get to the centre.
- If you want to hear fado at [Café Santa Cruz](https://cafesantacruz.com/index-en/), remember there is no 10pm session on Sundays.
- Pack comfortable shoes. The walk from the river to the university is short on a map and long on the legs.
