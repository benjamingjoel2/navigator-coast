Somewhere around the end of the first week, a trip through Portugal changes gear. The granite and green of the north give way to the whitewash and cork oaks of the Alentejo, the Atlantic warms up, and the pace of everything, lunch included, slows down. Two weeks is long enough to feel that shift happen, rather than to watch it through a train window.

This route runs the length of mainland Portugal, from Porto to the Algarve, with time for the Douro Valley and the smaller cities in between. It is a fuller, slower version of our [ten-day itinerary](/stories/ten-day-portugal-itinerary/): the same backbone, with extra nights where we always wish we had them.

## How the fortnight fits together

| Days | Base | Highlights |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Porto | Ribeira, São Bento, Gaia, Foz |
| 4–5 | Douro Valley | The riverside railway, Pinhão, quintas |
| 6–7 | Coimbra (via Aveiro) | Aveiro's canals, the old university |
| 8–10 | Lisbon | Alfama, Belém, a day in Sintra |
| 11–12 | Évora | Walled town, Alentejo food and wine |
| 13–14 | Lagos | Cliffs, coves and the far south-west |

Most of it works by train. CP's [timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) lists the services this route leans on: Porto–Régua–Pocinho for the Douro, the Porto–Aveiro urban line, Coimbra–Aveiro regional trains, Lisbon–Évora, and the long-distance trains from Lisbon to Lagos. A hire car earns its keep only at the very end, in the Alentejo and the Algarve.

## Days 1–3: Porto

Give Porto three nights. Its old centre has [UNESCO World Heritage status](https://visitporto.travel/en-GB), and the best way to see it is slowly: down the steep lanes of the Ribeira to the quay, into São Bento station for its tiled hall, across the Dom Luís I bridge to Vila Nova de Gaia, where the Port houses keep their lodges.

<Pair a="tw-porto-ribeira-above" b="tw-porto-river-bridge" captionA="The Ribeira quayside, seen from above." captionB="Looking down the Douro from the bridge at Porto." />

Spend a morning at the [Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/), the city's historic market, which mixes fresh-produce stalls with small shops and restaurants. It is closed on Sundays, and its site carried a storm-closure notice in November 2026, so check before you go. On the last evening, ride out to Foz, where the river meets the ocean. Our [48 hours in Porto](/stories/porto-in-48-hours/) has a street-by-street plan if you want one.

## Days 4–5: Up the Douro

The Douro deserves at least two nights of its own, and this is where a fortnight really pays off. CP's [MiraDouro service](https://cp.pt/info/en/miradouro-train) runs daily in both directions between Porto São Bento and Régua, Pinhão and Pocinho, using vintage carriages with windows that open. Regular regional trains run on the same line.

Base yourself in or near Pinhão and spend the days among the terraces, booking any estate visits ahead. [Quinta do Crasto](https://www.quintadocrasto.wine/en/how-to-arrive/), on the right bank of the river between Régua and Pinhão, says all visits must be booked and confirmed in advance; its [history page](https://www.quintadocrasto.wine/en/history/) traces records of the estate and its wine back to 1615. It suggests Ferrão as the nearest station on the Douro line.

<Figure id="tw-douro-pinhao" caption="Terraced vineyards above the Douro near Pinhão." />

If you are going to taste seriously, do not drive. Our [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/) covers the quintas, the viewpoints and the rules on drinking and driving in more detail.

## Days 6–7: Aveiro and Coimbra

Head back towards the coast on day six. Aveiro is on the Porto–Aveiro urban line listed on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf), and it makes a good half-day stop: a small city laced with canals, where painted moliceiro boats, once used to gather seaweed from the lagoon, now carry visitors past Art Nouveau facades. Try ovos moles, the local sweet of sugared egg yolk in a thin wafer shell.

<Figure id="tw-aveiro-canal" caption="Moliceiro boats on Aveiro's central canal." />

In the afternoon, take a regional train on to Coimbra. CP notes that the university was founded in 1290 and that UNESCO listed the [University of Coimbra, Alta and Sofia, as World Heritage in 2013](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/coimbra-heritage). Give the next day to the old university on top of the hill. According to the [university's visitor information](https://visit.uc.pt/en/info), a non-guided ticket is valid for two days, but the Joanina Library is visited at a specific date and time chosen when you buy, so book that first. When we checked, the page also listed the university tower as closed.

Coimbra is a student city, and it feels like one in the evenings. Our [Coimbra guide](/stories/coimbra-guide/) covers where to eat and where to hear Coimbra's own style of fado.

## Days 8–10: Lisbon and Sintra

From Coimbra, continue south to Lisbon by train. The long-distance Lisbon–Porto timetables on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) show the through services, and CP's [Alfa Pendular page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/alfa-pendular-train) describes its fastest trains, which you can book online.

Three nights let you do Lisbon justice. Spend one day in the old hill neighbourhoods of Alfama and Graça, with their viewpoints, laundry lines and tiled facades. Spend another in Belém, west along the river, for the monastery and the tower. Keep the third for Sintra, whose cultural landscape CP notes was [classified by UNESCO in 1995](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/sintra-heritage); Sintra-line trains run from the city, and the timetable is on CP's site. Buy palace tickets in advance and choose two or three sites rather than trying to see them all.

## Days 11–12: Évora and the Alentejo

From Lisbon, trains head east into the Alentejo, the wide, sunburnt province of wheat fields, olive groves and cork oaks. Évora's historic centre has been a [UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/alentejo-evora), and CP notes that the station is close enough to walk into town.

Two nights here are a luxury, and a good one. Walk the walled town in the early morning before the day-trippers arrive: the Roman temple, the cathedral roof, the whitewashed lanes trimmed with ochre. Then eat properly. Alentejo cooking is generous and unhurried, built around bread, pork, olive oil and herbs, and the region's wines are worth a glass at every meal.

If you want to stay somewhere memorable, [Convento do Espinheiro](https://www.conventodoespinheiro.com/) is a hotel in a restored 15th-century convent in Évora, with its frescoes preserved, a spa and gardens.

<Pair a="tw-evora-temple" b="tw-lagos-piedade" captionA="The Roman temple in Évora." captionB="Boats among the sea stacks at Ponta da Piedade, near Lagos." />

## Days 13–14: Lagos and the south-west

The last leg is the one place where we would pick up a hire car. CP's timetable lists Lisbon–Évora and the Lagos services as separate routes, so by train you would be heading back towards Lisbon to go south. By car, you can drive down through the Alentejo and be on the coast the same day.

Some Portuguese motorways have electronic-only tolls, collected by overhead gantries rather than at booths. [Portugal Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/) explains the options for foreign-registered vehicles, including linking a bank card to the number plate with Easytoll, prepaid Tollcards and Via Verde devices. With a hire car, ask the rental company how tolls are charged before you leave the desk.

Base yourself in Lagos for the final two nights. CP's [Algarve page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/algarve-places) describes the town at the mouth of the river Bensafrim, with a well-preserved old centre and everything within walking distance, and just south, the sandstone cliffs, arches and sea stacks of Ponta da Piedade drop into clear green water. Our [Lagos guide](/stories/lagos-guide/) covers the beaches and boat trips.

On the last day, drive west to Sagres and Cape St Vincent, where the land runs out. The [Pousada de Sagres](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-sagres) looks out over the Sagres fortress and towards the lighthouse at Cape St Vincent, and keeps the 1960s design of its rooms and restaurant.

## When to go

We like this route best in spring and early autumn, when long days on foot in Évora and Coimbra are comfortable and the coast at the end is still inviting. In high summer, start early and plan the middle of the day around shade and lunch.

## Before you book

- Check entry rules on GOV.UK's [Portugal entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/entry-requirements) page, including the EU's Entry/Exit System and the 90-days-in-180 rule.
- Fly into Porto and out of Faro, or the reverse, so you never backtrack the length of the country.
- Book the Joanina Library slot in Coimbra through the [university's visitor pages](https://visit.uc.pt/en/info) before you fix the rest of that day.
- Book quinta visits in the Douro in advance; several estates only receive visitors by appointment.
- Check the current train times on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) for each leg, as timetables change during the year.
- If you hire a car for the south, sort out toll payment using [Portugal Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/) or your rental company before you set off.
