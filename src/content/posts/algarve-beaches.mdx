Stand on the clifftop above almost any beach between Lagos and Albufeira and you see the same thing: rock the colour of toasted bread, worn into arches and stacks, with a pocket of sand at the bottom and water that turns from jade to deep blue within a few metres. It is the Algarve of postcards, and it is real. But it is only one of the Algarve's coasts.

Head east past Faro and the cliffs give way to flat sandbar islands and a lagoon full of channels and saltmarsh. Head west past Sagres and round the corner, and the beaches become long, open and wild, facing the full Atlantic. Three very different kinds of beach, all within a couple of hours' drive of each other.

Knowing which kind you want makes planning far easier. Here is how we think about them.

## The cove coast: Lagos to Albufeira

This is the stretch most people picture. Sandstone and limestone cliffs, eroded by sea and weather, enclose small beaches reached by steps, ramps or paths. Some are tiny and fill quickly; some open out at low tide into a chain of connected coves.

<Figure id="ab-algarve-chapel-headland" caption="A whitewashed chapel on a clifftop headland, with a sheltered beach curving away beside it." />

The charm is the scale. A cove gives you shade under the rock in the afternoon, calm water on settled days, and a sense of being tucked away even when you are not. The trade-off is space. In high summer the popular coves fill early, and the sand can disappear almost entirely at high tide, so check the tide before you commit to a long walk down.

Around Lagos the cliffs are at their most theatrical. Ponta da Piedade, just south of the town, is a headland of pinnacles, grottoes and arches that looks best from the water, and the coves either side of it are reached by long flights of steps. Our [guide to Lagos](/stories/lagos-guide/) covers the town itself and how to base yourself there.

<Pair a="ab-lagos-footbridge-arch" b="ab-algarve-golden-cliffs" captionA="A stone footbridge arch between sandstone stacks on a cove near Lagos." captionB="Weathered golden cliffs drop straight into the sea along the western Algarve." />

Further east, around Carvoeiro and Benagil, the coast is riddled with sea caves. The best known is the cave at Benagil, and it has its own rules. In 2024 the national maritime authority announced a joint edital that [bans landing on or using the sand inside the Benagil cave, and bans swimming or floating into the caves](https://www.amn.pt/Media/Paginas/DetalheNoticia.aspx?nid=5827). It also bans renting kayaks without a guide in the cave area. In practice, that means seeing the caves from a licensed boat or a guided kayak trip, not swimming across from the beach. Our [Benagil sea caves guide](/stories/benagil-sea-caves/) goes into the options in more detail.

### Staying safe under the cliffs

The cliffs that make these beaches beautiful are also the main hazard. The UK government's [Portugal safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) is blunt: falling rocks are a hazard, particularly in the Algarve, you should look out for signs warning of cliff erosion, and the authorities can fine people who ignore them. Do not lay your towel at the foot of a cliff, however inviting the shade, and keep well back from the edge on clifftop paths.

## The lagoon coast: the Ria Formosa and the sandbar islands

East of Faro, the Algarve changes character completely. A chain of low barrier islands runs parallel to the shore, separating the open sea from a sheltered lagoon of tidal channels, mudflats and saltpans. The town-side water is calm and shallow; the sea side of each island is one long, straight beach of fine pale sand, often backed by dunes.

<Figure id="ab-cacela-velha-lagoon" caption="Low tide at Cacela Velha: sandbanks, shallow channels and the sea beyond the bar." />

Getting to these beaches is part of the pleasure. Most involve a short boat crossing, a footbridge over the lagoon, or a walk across a causeway, and the lack of road access keeps them uncluttered. The crowds that do come spread out along kilometres of sand, so even in August you can usually find room by walking ten minutes from the landing point.

They also suit families. The lagoon side is sheltered and the slope into the sea is generally gentle, though the sea side is open water and can still have waves and currents on a breezy day. Children love the boat ride; adults tend to like the absence of cliffs and steps.

Tavira is the natural base for this coast. The [Pousada Convento Tavira](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-tavira), in the historic centre, occupies the rebuilt Convent of Nossa Senhora da Graça; the pousada describes itself as a few minutes' walk from the town's old bridge over the Gilão and about 15 minutes from the beach on Ilha de Tavira. Our [Tavira guide](/stories/tavira-guide/) and our piece on the [Ria Formosa islands](/stories/ria-formosa-islands/) cover crossings, island by island.

A practical note: the islands are exposed. Bring shade, water and more sun cream than you think you need, because there are few trees and the reflected light off pale sand is intense. Some islands have seasonal cafés near the jetties; outside summer, assume you need to bring your own.

## The long sands: the western Algarve and the Atlantic

Round Cape St Vincent and the coast turns north, and so does the mood. The beaches along the western edge of the Algarve face straight into the Atlantic swell. They are long and wide, often set at the mouth of a valley between high cliffs, with dunes and low scrub behind. Water is noticeably cooler than on the south coast, the wind is stronger and the waves are bigger.

<Figure id="ab-bordeira-boardwalk" size="full" caption="A boardwalk through the dunes above Praia da Bordeira, on the Algarve's Atlantic-facing west coast." />

This is surf country. On a good day you will see more boards than parasols, and plenty of people take their first lessons here. It is also wonderful walking country: the coastal paths of the [Rota Vicentina](/stories/walking-the-rota-vicentina/) link several of these beaches, and walking between them on a clear spring morning is one of the great Algarve experiences.

For swimming, though, these beaches demand respect. Waves can be unpredictable and rip currents form easily on open beaches like these. If you are not a confident sea swimmer, stay in the zone the lifeguards are watching, and on a big day consider simply watching the waves.

Sagres sits at the corner where the two coasts meet, so you can pick a south-facing or west-facing beach each morning depending on the wind. The [Pousada Sagres](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-sagres) in the village describes itself as about ten minutes' walk from the beach, with views of the Sagres fortress and the lighthouse at Cabo de São Vicente.

## Reading the flags and the forecast

Wherever you swim, learn the flags. According to the [UK government's travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security), supervised beaches in Portugal have a flag system: a red flag means you must not enter the water, and yellow means it is only safe to paddle at the water's edge. The same advice warns that some beaches have a strong undertow, that you should not try to swim against a rip current, and that you should not dive into unknown water. Take extra care where there are no lifeguards, flags or signs.

Lifeguards are not on every beach all year round, so a warm spring or autumn day can find a beach with no flags and nobody watching. Treat those days with the same caution the advice recommends for any unsupervised stretch.

Two websites help with the planning. [IPMA's coastal forecasts](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) give hourly wave height, swell, wind and water temperature for beaches around the country, which is useful for choosing between a sheltered south-coast cove and an exposed west-coast beach on a given day. And the [Blue Flag programme in Portugal](https://bandeiraazul.abaae.pt/) publishes the beaches awarded each year for water quality, safety and services.

## Matching the beach to the trip

- **Couples and photographers:** the coves around Lagos and the central Algarve. Go early, before the light flattens and the steps get busy.
- **Families with small children:** the sandbar islands off Tavira and Faro, with their lagoon-side calm and long, gently shelving beaches.
- **Surfers and walkers:** the west coast, from Sagres northwards, ideally outside high summer.
- **Out of season:** all three, though the south coast is the more sheltered.

## Before you book

- Decide which coast suits you before choosing a base. Lagos, Tavira and Sagres put you on three different kinds of beach.
- Check tide times before walking down to a small cove; some lose most of their sand at high water.
- Read the beach flags every day, and do not swim when the red flag is up. The [UK government's safety advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/portugal/safety-and-security) explains what each means.
- Keep away from cliff bases and edges, and obey erosion warning signs.
- If you want to see the Benagil caves, book a licensed boat or guided kayak trip; swimming in is [not permitted](https://www.amn.pt/Media/Paginas/DetalheNoticia.aspx?nid=5827).
- Check [IPMA's coastal forecast](https://www.ipma.pt/en/maritima/costeira/) the evening before a west-coast beach day.
