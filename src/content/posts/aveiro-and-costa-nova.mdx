The water in Aveiro is never quite still. It slides in and out of the canals with the tide, rocks the painted boats tied up along the quays, and throws back a wobbling copy of every façade on the bank: yellow, cobalt, rose, a sliver of Art Nouveau ironwork. Then, a few kilometres west, the lagoon gives way to dunes, and a row of houses striped like beach towels stands between the water and the Atlantic.

<Figure id="avr-canal-dusk" caption="Aveiro's central canal at dusk, with a moliceiro moored along the quay." />

Aveiro and Costa Nova make one of the easiest and most cheerful days out in the Centro, and a good overnight stop between Porto and Coimbra. The city is flat and walkable, the sweets are famous, and the beach is close enough for an afternoon.

## The lagoon behind the city

Everything here comes back to the Ria de Aveiro, the lagoon that sits between the city and the sea. The [Center of Portugal tourism board](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/the-aveiro-ria) describes it as 47 kilometres of water running alongside the coast, covering around eleven thousand hectares, and says it began to form in the fifteenth century. Its channels and islands reach into the city itself, which is why Aveiro gets compared, a little hopefully, to Venice.

The lagoon made the city's living. The same page lists the trades that grew up around it: salt, seaweed harvesting, fishing and boatbuilding. Salt in particular shaped the landscape, though the tourism board's page on the [Aveiro salt ponds](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/the-aveiro-salines) notes that only a few working ponds remain, and points to the Troncalhada Eco-Museum as the place to learn how the salt was made.

## Moliceiros on the canals

The boats are what everyone photographs. Moliceiros are long, low, shallow-bottomed boats with high curling prows, and according to [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/aveiro) they were once used to carry seaweed from the lagoon to fertilise farmland. Today they carry visitors instead, on trips around the city canals.

<Pair a="avr-moliceiro-prow" b="avr-moliceiro-bridge" captionA="The painted prow of a moliceiro: bright panels, borders and a scene of local life." captionB="A moliceiro carrying passengers under the trees along one of the city canals." />

Look closely at the prows. Each is painted with bold panels, borders and little scenes of everyday life. It is folk art at the waterline, and no two are quite the same.

A boat trip is the obvious first thing to do and an easy way to get your bearings; the tourism board's [Ria page](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/the-aveiro-ria) lists moliceiro tours as the main way to explore the lagoon. Agree the route and duration before you board.

## Art Nouveau and the old town

Walk the streets beside the main canal and you will start noticing the façades: curving windows, wrought-iron balconies, tiles with plant motifs. [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/aveiro) calls Aveiro a city-museum of Art Nouveau and notes that it belongs to the European Réseau Art Nouveau Network, with a walking route linking the main buildings. You do not need a guide to enjoy it; just look up.

For the deeper history, go to the Museu de Aveiro, in the former Convent of Jesus. The city council's [museum page](https://www.cm-aveiro.pt/visitantes/museus-de-aveiro/museu-de-aveiro-santa-joana) presents it as the Museu de Aveiro / Santa Joana, and the tourism board's [Aveiro page](https://www.centerofportugal.com/destination/aveiro) tells the story behind the name: Princess Joana, daughter of King Afonso V, entered the Jesus Monastery, and the tourism board counts that as an important moment in the town's history.

## Ovos moles

That same convent is where Aveiro's sweet tooth begins. Ovos moles are egg yolks slowly cooked with sugar to a soft, golden cream, sealed inside thin wafer shells moulded into the shapes of clams, whelks and fish. The [tourism board](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/ovos-moles-of-aveiro) traces them to the city's old convents, and says they were the first Portuguese bakery product to gain the EU's Protected Geographical Indication. You can see the registration in the European Commission's [eAmbrosia register](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013759).

They are sold all over the centre, and the tourism board notes they are sometimes packed in small wooden or porcelain pots painted with scenes of the lagoon and its boats. Two places to start:

- **Confeitaria Peixinho**, on Rua de Coimbra in the centre, describes itself as the oldest ovos moles house in Aveiro, dating from 1856 (per its owners, [O Valor do Tempo](https://ovalordotempo.pt/en/brands/confeitaria-peixinho/)).
- **[Oficina do Doce](https://oficinadodoce.com/en/)**, in the Galeria Rossio, runs ovos moles workshops where you can see the traditional method live, sometimes combined with a moliceiro ride or a visit to the salt pans.

Ovos moles are very sweet, so one or two goes a long way. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) puts them in context with the rest of the country's convent sweets.

## Costa Nova: the striped houses

West of the city, a long sandbar separates the lagoon from the open sea, and on it sits Costa Nova. Its houses are the reason for the trip: rows of wooden-fronted buildings painted in bold vertical stripes, red and white, navy and white, yellow and white, each one a little different from its neighbour.

<Figure id="avr-costa-nova-row" caption="The striped houses at Costa Nova, facing the lagoon side of the sandbar." />

They began as something humbler. The [Center of Portugal page on Costa Nova](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/costa-nova) explains that these palheiros were first used by fishermen to store their gear, built from local materials on stakes in the dry sand, with a single undivided room inside. Over time some fishermen turned them into summer homes for several families, and later they became beach houses. The earliest had horizontal planks painted bright red; the stripes came later.

The same page explains the village's name. The lagoon's mouth was opened in 1808, creating a new shore that gave fishermen easier access to the sea, and families from Ílhavo settled here through the nineteenth century. The "do Prado" in its full name, Costa Nova do Prado, refers to a meadow that once lay beside the beach.

<Pair a="avr-costa-nova-door" b="avr-costa-nova-stripes" captionA="Yellow and red stripes meet at a Costa Nova doorway." captionB="Neighbours in yellow and navy on the Costa Nova front." />

The houses are private homes and holiday lets, so admire them from the street. Then cross to the ocean side, where a wide beach runs north and south as far as you can see. The Atlantic here is cold and can be rough; swim only where lifeguards are on duty and follow the flags.

Nearby, at Barra, is the tall red-and-white lighthouse that dominates this stretch of coast. [Center of Portugal](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/barra-lighthouse) calls it the highest lighthouse in Portugal, inaugurated in 1893, and lists free climbs on Wednesday afternoons. Check before you go.

## Getting there

From Porto, Aveiro is the end of one of the city's suburban lines: CP lists the Aveiro line as part of its [Porto urban train network](https://cp.pt/info/en/porto-trains). For faster options from further afield, look up Aveiro on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf). If you are planning a bigger loop, our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) explains the different train types and how to book.

Costa Nova is a short drive from the city. Without a car, ask at your accommodation or the tourist office about the current way out there; with one, park once and walk.

Aveiro is close enough to Porto for a day trip, and if you are basing yourself in the north, it pairs well with the city and with [three days in the Douro Valley](/stories/three-days-in-the-douro-valley/). If you are still choosing between the two big cities, our [Lisbon or Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) guide may help.

## When to go

Aveiro is pleasant in any season, but Costa Nova is at its best when the sun is out and the stripes glow. Summer brings the beach crowds; spring and early autumn give you warm afternoons with more room on the sand. Our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons in more detail.

## Before you book

- Check the current Porto–Aveiro times on [CP's timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf), especially for the return.
- Agree the route and duration of any moliceiro trip before you pay.
- If you want to climb the Barra lighthouse, check the current visiting days on the [tourism board's page](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/barra-lighthouse).
- Book an ovos moles workshop in advance if you want one, via [Oficina do Doce](https://oficinadodoce.com/en/).
- Bring a layer and sun cream for Costa Nova: the wind off the Atlantic is cool even on bright days.
