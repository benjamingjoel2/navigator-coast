Lisbon is a city you understand from above. Down in the Baixa the streets are straight and orderly, but climb any of the hills around it and the place rearranges itself into a sea of terracotta roofs, church domes and washing lines, all tipping towards the wide, bright Tagus. The Portuguese have a word for the spots where the city opens up like this: miradouro, a viewpoint.

There are dozens of them, from grand terraces with kiosks and benches to scraps of railing at the end of a lane. This walk strings together the classics in a single day, starting high in the east, crossing the castle hill and the Baixa, and finishing in the west as the sun drops behind the river. It is hilly, it is mostly on foot, and every climb is paid back in full.

## Before you start: how Lisbon's lifts are running

Lisbon's famous funiculars and its street lift have long been part of any viewpoint walk, so check their status before you plan around them. At the time of writing, Carris lists the [Glória and Lavra funiculars](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/funicular-gloria-and-lavra/) as out of service, the Bica funicular as out of service, and the [Santa Justa Lift](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/lift/) as temporarily closed. The newer [Graça funicular](https://www.carris.pt/en/discover/fleet/funiculars/), linking Mouraria with Graça, appears on the same page with a published schedule. Check Carris's site on the day; on this route we assume you are walking.

The other useful piece of transport is the tram. The [28E](https://www.carris.pt/viaje/carreiras/28e/) runs between Martim Moniz and Campo de Ourique (Prazeres), with stops at Graça, Largo das Portas do Sol, the Sé, Chiado and Estrela, which is a fair summary of this walk. Carris flagged new timetables for the route from 2 November, so look up the current one. Our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) covers tickets and the wider network.

## Morning: Senhora do Monte and Graça

Start at the top. The [Miradouro da Senhora do Monte](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/miradouro-da-senhora-do-monte) is the high point of the walk, and Visit Lisboa calls it one of the calmest spots for looking out over the city. To the left are the church of Graça and the castle; in the distance the river, the ruins of the Carmo and the pastel blocks of the Avenidas Novas. The small chapel beside it dates its foundation to 1147, and tradition places Portugal's first king, Afonso Henriques, here as he gathered his troops to take Lisbon.

Morning is the time to come, before the terrace fills. Bring a coffee, find a bench and let the city wake up beneath you.

A few minutes downhill is the [Miradouro da Graça](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/miradouro-da-graca), set on its own hill beside the castle's. Visit Lisboa describes the shade of the church and its pine trees giving it an air of spiritual refuge, and it has a café where you can sit and look out over the historic quarters and the Tagus beyond. The viewpoint also appears on Visit Lisboa's [Fado Route](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/lisbon-stories/1-fado-route/pois) under the name of the poet Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen, with a view of the castle and the whole of downtown.

Graça, Alfama and Mouraria are worth far more than a walk-through. If you want to linger, our guide to [Alfama, Mouraria and Graça](/stories/lisbon-alfama-mouraria-graca/) explores the three neighbourhoods street by street.

## Late morning: the castle

From Graça, walk down and round to the [Castelo de São Jorge](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/), which crowns the hill above the Baixa. Its walls and towers are a viewpoint in their own right, and the castle sells tickets online. According to its [schedules page](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/), it opens at 9am every day except a handful of holidays, closing at 9pm from March to October and at 6pm from November to February. In winter, the wall walks and towers close for safety at 5.30pm, so do not leave the ramparts until last. The camera obscura is visited only on a guided tour included in the ticket.

Inside the castle walls is [Solar do Castelo](https://lisbonheritagehotels.com/solar-do-castelo/), a small hotel in an 18th-century palace that its operator says stands on the site of the old royal palace kitchens and is the only hotel inside the castle walls. It is an unusual place to wake up if you want the hill to yourself before the gates open.

## Midday: Portas do Sol and Santa Luzia

Drop down through the lanes towards Alfama and you come to two viewpoints almost side by side, at Largo das Portas do Sol and Santa Luzia. Both look east and south over Alfama's tumble of roofs to the river.

<Pair a="mir-santa-luzia-sunrise" b="mir-tram-estrela" captionA="Early light over Alfama from Santa Luzia, with the dome of Santa Engrácia above the roofs." captionB="A yellow tram climbing a cobbled street, heading for Prazeres." />

The [Miradouro de Santa Luzia](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/miradouro-de-santa-luzia) is as much about its tiles as its view. Visit Lisboa points to the panels by António Quaresma near the café and kiosk, showing Praça do Comércio before the 1755 earthquake and the taking of the castle from the Moors. From the terrace you can pick out the dome of Santa Engrácia, the church of Santo Estêvão and the twin towers of São Miguel. Trams rattle past on the street behind, bells ringing.

That big white dome is the [Panteão Nacional](https://www.panteaonacional.gov.pt/), the former church of Santa Engrácia. The building was classified as a national monument in 1910, and a 1916 law decided it would become the National Pantheon. If you want to see it up close, its [visiting page](https://www.panteaonacional.gov.pt/visitar) lists opening from Tuesday to Sunday, 10am to 5pm, between October and March; check the summer hours before you go.

<Figure id="mir-pantheon-dome" size="column" caption="The dome of the Panteão Nacional rising above Alfama's rooftops in the late afternoon." />

For lunch, you are in the right part of town for a long, slow one. If you would rather eat with a view, [memmo Alfama](https://www.memmohotels.com/alfama/), a hotel tucked into the heart of the neighbourhood, has a wine bar and terrace set high above the quarter, overlooking the rooftops and the Tagus.

## Afternoon: down through the Baixa, up to São Pedro de Alcântara

From Alfama, follow the hill down past the Sé into the flat grid of the Baixa. This is the one stretch of the day without a view, so treat it as a breather. The Santa Justa Lift stands over the street here, an iron tower that Carris credits to Raoul Mesnier du Ponsard and dates to 1902. Carris lists it as temporarily closed, but its ironwork is worth stopping beneath to admire.

<Figure id="mir-santa-justa-castle" caption="The ironwork of the Santa Justa Lift, with the Baixa below and the castle walls on the hill beyond." />

Now climb west. With the Glória funicular out of service, you climb on foot up the steep Calçada da Glória, the street its track follows, and at the top you reach the [Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/miradouro-de-s-pedro-de-alcantara), right beside the top of the funicular line. Visit Lisboa describes it as part of a well-kept garden, with a kiosk for a cold drink and an original map in azulejos to help you identify the landmarks. The view runs back across the Baixa to everything you have walked today: Graça, the castle, the hill of Senhora do Monte.

<Figure id="mir-funicular" caption="One of Lisbon's funicular cars on its steep, narrow track; check Carris for which lines are running." />

From here you are on the edge of the Bairro Alto and a short walk from Chiado. Our guide to [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) explains how these hilltop neighbourhoods differ as a base.

## Sunset: Santa Catarina

End the day facing west. Walk through the Bairro Alto and down towards Bica to the [Miradouro de Santa Catarina](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/places/miradouro-de-santa-catarina). Visit Lisboa describes a view that takes in the whole of the Tagus estuary, the bridge and the rooftops, watched over by a large statue of Adamastor, the mythical figure Camões created to symbolise the Cape of Torment, the old name for the Cape of Good Hope. It is a fitting final stop: open, a little scruffy, and pointed straight at the sunset.

If you still have energy after dark, the lanes you walked this morning are where Lisbon's fado lives; our guide to [fado in Lisbon](/stories/fado-in-lisbon/) explains where to hear it.

## Practical tips

- **Shoes.** Lisbon's pavements are polished limestone and basalt calçada, beautiful and slippery, especially after rain. Wear shoes with grip.
- **Pacing.** The walk is a full day on foot with several steep climbs. It is easy to split across two days, east in one and west in the other.
- **Light.** The east-facing terraces, Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol, look best in the morning; Santa Catarina and São Pedro de Alcântara are evening places.
- **Water and shade.** In summer, carry water and plan the climbs for the cooler hours. Our guide to the [best time to visit Portugal](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) covers the seasons.

## Before you book

- Check the status of the funiculars, the Santa Justa Lift and the 28E on [Carris's site](https://www.carris.pt/viaje/carreiras/28e/) before you plan around them.
- Look up the castle's seasonal hours on its [schedules page](https://castelodesaojorge.pt/en/plan-your-visit/schedules/) and buy tickets online to save time.
- Check the [Panteão Nacional's visiting page](https://www.panteaonacional.gov.pt/visitar) for current hours if you want to go inside.
- Save Santa Catarina or São Pedro de Alcântara for sunset, and keep your phone and bag close on crowded terraces.
