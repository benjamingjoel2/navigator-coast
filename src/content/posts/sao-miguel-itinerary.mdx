The first time the cloud lifts over Sete Cidades, it feels like a curtain going up. One moment you are standing on a crater rim in grey mist, the next there are two lakes below you, one greener and one bluer, with a village on the shore and cows grazing to the water's edge. Then the mist rolls back in. On São Miguel, that is how it goes.

The largest island in the Azores is the obvious first one to visit. It has the main airport at Ponta Delgada, the most places to stay, and an extraordinary concentration of volcanic landscape: crater lakes, steaming fumaroles, hot springs and black-rock coast. A week lets you see all of it without rushing, and leaves slack for the days when the weather says no.

## How to plan a week here

The regional tourism board's [São Miguel page](https://mi.visitazores.com/en/node/20) describes an island of two mountain areas separated by a lower platform, and that shape suits a simple plan. Base yourself in Ponta Delgada, or split the week between the city and Furnas, and use a hire car to reach the lakes, trailheads and viewpoints.

The weather is the real planner. IPMA's 1991–2020 [climate normals for Ponta Delgada](https://www.ipma.pt/bin/file.data/climate-normal/cn_91-20_PONTA_DELGADA_OBSERVATORIO.pdf) show mild temperatures all year but frequent rain, and the crater lakes sit high enough to be in cloud when the coast is bright. Treat the days below as a menu rather than a schedule: if the sky is clear, go up to the lakes; if not, head for the hot springs or the coast. Our comparison of [the Azores and Madeira](/stories/azores-or-madeira/) goes into the climate in more detail.

## Day 1: Ponta Delgada

Spend the first day finding your feet in the island's main city. The black-and-white basalt paving, whitewashed churches and harbourfront make for an easy afternoon walk, and the marina is a good place to watch the evening light.

<Figure id="smi-ponta-delgada-marina" caption="Yachts in Ponta Delgada's marina, with the city's church towers behind." />

On the edge of the city, at Fajã de Baixo, the [Arruda pineapple plantation](https://www.ananasesarruda.com/contactos/) opens its glasshouses to visitors. The plantation lists free entry and opening every day, and describes itself as a living museum of the São Miguel pineapple, with more than a century of history, where visitors can follow every stage of the plant's growth.

## Day 2: Sete Cidades

Go west to the island's most famous view. The tourism board recommends seeing the Sete Cidades crater from the Vista do Rei lookout, where the twin lakes, the Green and the Blue, lie side by side, joined by a bridge.

If you want to walk, the marked trails are the best way to feel the scale of the caldera. The official leaflet for [PR04 SMI](https://trails.visitazores.com/sites/default/files/pr04smi-folheto.pdf) starts near Mata do Canário, on the east of the crater ridge, passes the Muro das Nove Janelas aqueduct and the Pico da Cruz viewpoint over the lakes, then descends around Lagoa Azul to the village. Check the [Azores Trails alerts page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/alerts) for closures before you set off, and again on the morning.

Afterwards, drive down to the village on the lake shore for lunch, and come back up to the rim in the late afternoon if the morning was misty.

## Day 3: Lagoa do Fogo, Caldeira Velha and Ribeira Grande

Lagoa do Fogo, in the centre of the island, is the wilder of the great crater lakes. The leaflet for [PR02 SMI](https://trails.visitazores.com/sites/default/files/pr02smi-folheto.pdf) describes it as the only certified walking trail giving access to the lake, which is classified as a nature reserve. It is a linear there-and-back route to the southern shore, starting beside the Parque Escutista dos Lagos, and it passes levadas and old hydraulic works along the Ribeira da Praia on the way up.

<Pair a="smi-lagoa-do-fogo" b="smi-gorreana-tea" captionA="Lagoa do Fogo, deep blue inside its green crater." captionB="Clipped rows of tea bushes on an Azorean hillside." />

On the north side of the mountain, the tourism board picks out Caldeira Velha for its stream of clear water running through ochre channels cut into dark rock, and the Caldeiras of Ribeira Grande, where a thermal building dating back to 1811 sits among trees and fumaroles. Ribeira Grande itself, the north coast's main town, is a good place to finish the day.

## Day 4: Tea and the north coast

Follow the north coast east to [Chá Gorreana](https://gorreana.pt/), near Maia. The estate dates its tea-making traditions to 1883, describes itself as the oldest organic tea plantation in Europe, and invites visitors to tour the plantation and its historic factory, where black and green teas are still processed.

This is a lovely stretch of coast to potter along, with lookouts over the sea and small villages tucked into the valleys. Keep an eye on the clock, though: you will want an early start tomorrow.

## Day 5: Furnas

Furnas is the volcanic heart of São Miguel. The tourism board calls the valley a Mecca of spas, home to dozens of thermal springs, fumaroles and naturally carbonated waters, and you smell the sulphur before you see the steam.

<Figure id="smi-furnas-chapel" caption="The chapel on the shore of Lagoa das Furnas, half hidden in the woods." />

Start at the lake, where pots of cozido are buried in the warm ground. The regional tourism board's [recipe page](https://taste.visitazores.com/en/recipe/cozido-furnas) explains that the pot is placed in ground heated by volcanic steam and cooks slowly for about six hours in its own juices. Our [guide to what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) has more on the island's cooking.

Then soak. [Parque Terra Nostra](https://www.parqueterranostra.com/pt/) is a historic garden whose large thermal pool is fed by a volcanic spring at around 42°C, with water the park describes as rich in iron. Wear an old swimsuit: the water stains. A short walk away, [Poça da Dona Beija](https://pocadadonabeija.com/) is a set of smaller pools in a wooded stream valley, with water at around 39°C. It sells tickets online only and stays open into the evening, which makes it a good last stop of the day.

## Day 6: Nordeste and Povoação

The far east of the island feels remote, and it rewards a day of slow driving. The tourism board notes that the Nordeste lookouts are surrounded by gardens and set up for barbecues, and singles out the Lomba do Cavaleiro lookout over the caldera of Povoação and its seven ridges. Pack a picnic and take the coastal road, stopping wherever the view opens up.

## Day 7: Whale watching and Vila Franca

Finish at sea. [Visit Azores](https://www.visitazores.com/en/experience-the-azores/whale-watching) says whale and dolphin watching is possible throughout the year, and sets out the code of conduct operators must follow, including keeping their distance and never chasing the animals. Our [guide to whale watching in the Azores](/stories/whale-watching-azores/) covers the seasons in more detail. Trips are cancelled when the sea is rough, so book for early in the week if you can and keep the last day as a backup.

Afterwards, drive along the south coast to Vila Franca do Campo to look out at the islet just offshore, which the tourism board singles out among São Miguel's coastal views. If you are moving on to other islands, our guide to [Pico and Faial](/stories/pico-and-faial/) covers the next obvious hop.

## Before you book

- Hire a car; most of the lakes, trailheads and lookouts are not practical by bus.
- Build flexibility into the week: swap days to put the crater lakes on the clearest mornings.
- Check the [Azores Trails alerts page](https://trails.visitazores.com/en/alerts) the night before and the morning of any walk.
- Buy Poça da Dona Beija tickets [online](https://pocadadonabeija.com/) in advance, and check current opening hours on the official sites of any pool or garden you plan to visit.
- Book whale watching with a licensed operator and expect cancellations in rough weather.
- Pack a waterproof, layers and swimwear you do not mind staining in iron-rich water.
