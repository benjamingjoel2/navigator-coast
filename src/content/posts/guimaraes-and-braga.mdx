Guimarães wears its history on its sleeve. On an old stretch of wall in the town centre, big letters spell out *Aqui nasceu Portugal*: Portugal was born here. Walk a few minutes uphill and you reach the castle that gave the claim its power, its granite keep rising out of a grassy hilltop, as solid and plain as a child's drawing of a fortress.

Braga, a short hop away, is a different proposition: a bigger, busier city with an ancient cathedral at its heart and one of the most theatrical sights in Portugal on a wooded hill just outside it. Doing both in a day from Porto is ambitious, but entirely possible if you plan the trains. Here is how.

## Getting there

Both towns are on CP's [Porto urban train network](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/porto-trains), which includes a Braga line and a Guimarães line. The network's [route map](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/mapa-comboios-urbanos-porto) shows both lines starting from Porto São Bento and Porto Campanhã and running north together before they split at Lousado: one branch continues to Braga, the other heads east to Guimarães.

That fork shapes the day. To see both towns by train, you go out on one line, come back towards Lousado and change for the other, so allow time for the connection. Check the current timetables on [CP's timetable page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) before you set your order of play, and see [CP's urban fares page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/prices-porto-urban-trains) for tickets. With a car, linking the two is simpler; our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/) explains how motorway tolls work if you drive.

Our suggestion: Guimarães in the morning, when the old town is quiet, and Braga in the afternoon, finishing with the evening light on Bom Jesus.

## Morning: Guimarães

### The castle and the ducal palace

Start at the top of the town, on the hill that holds the castle and the palace.

The [Guimarães Castle](https://pacodosduques.gov.pt/monumentos/castelo-de-guimaraes/?lang=en) has its roots in the 10th century, when, according to its official history, Countess Mumadona had a fortification built to protect the monastery below from raids. It was rebuilt and enlarged over the following centuries, including under Afonso Henriques, Portugal's first king, and took its current shape by the late 13th century. Its symbolic weight, the official history notes, has grown larger than its actual military role; it was classified as a National Monument in 1881.

<Pair a="gb-guimaraes-arcade" b="gb-castle-keep" captionA="A covered passage opening onto a narrow street in the old town of Guimarães." captionB="The battlements of Guimarães Castle against a cloudy sky." />

Just below it, the [Paço dos Duques de Bragança](https://pacodosduques.gov.pt/monumentos/paco-dos-duques/?lang=en) was built between 1420 and 1433 for Afonso, later the first Duke of Braganza. After centuries of neglect, and a spell as a military barracks in the 19th century, it was rebuilt between 1937 and 1959. Today it is both a museum, with collections of tapestries, furniture, ceramics, paintings and weapons, and the official residence of the Portuguese President in the north of the country.

The palace, the castle and the nearby Church of São Miguel [open daily](https://pacodosduques.gov.pt/planear-visita/bilhetica-horarios/?lang=en) from 10:00 to 18:00, with last admission at 17:30, and the ticket office is in the palace. They close on 1 January, Easter Day, 1 May, 24 June and 25 December. At the time of writing the castle's watchtower was temporarily closed, so check before you go.

### The old town

From the hill, wander down into the historic centre. The Câmara Municipal's [World Heritage page](https://www.cm-guimaraes.pt/municipio/patrimonio-mundial) describes it as an exceptionally well-preserved example of a medieval settlement that grew into a modern city, tied to the emergence of Portuguese national identity in the 12th century. The original listing dates from 2001, and in 2023 it was extended to take in the Zona de Couros, the old leather-tanning quarter.

This is a town for slow walking: granite arcades, narrow lanes, balconies with wooden screens and small squares opening one into the next. Largo da Oliveira is the heart of it, and the best place to sit down with a coffee before the train.

If you have extra time, the Penha cable car climbs from the edge of town to the mountain above it. CP's [Guimarães cable car page](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/guimaraes-cable-car) says it opened in 1995 and was probably Portugal's first; [Turipenha](https://www.turipenha.pt/teleferico/informacoes), which runs it, publishes monthly hours and notes that it can close for bad weather or maintenance.

## Afternoon: Braga

### The cathedral

Braga's old centre is a grid of busy shopping streets, squares and churches. At its heart is the Sé, which Visit Braga describes as [the country's oldest cathedral](https://visitbraga.travel/highlights/monumentos/se-de-braga/). The building in its current form was planned in the 11th century by Bishop Pedro, and it mixes a Romanesque and Gothic exterior with a largely Baroque interior. Look for the ceilings and the pipe organs, and the Treasury-Museum. Visit Braga lists the opening hours on the same page; check them before you go, as they break for lunch.

<Pair a="gb-braga-se" b="gb-braga-arch" captionA="The twin-towered façade of the Sé de Braga." captionB="The Arco da Porta Nova, a Baroque stone arch in Braga." />

### Bom Jesus do Monte

Then head out of the centre to the sight most people come for. The Sanctuary of Bom Jesus do Monte, on a wooded hillside just outside the city, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019. The UN information centre's [report of the listing](https://unric.org/pt/mais-dois-monumentos-de-portugal-integram-lista-do-patrimonio-mundial-da-unesco/) describes a Baroque sacred mount developed over more than 600 years, with a Way of the Cross climbing the slope past chapels, fountains, statues and formal gardens. It singles out the Stairway of the Five Senses as the most emblematic Baroque work on the site, and notes that the church at the top was built between 1784 and 1811.

<Figure id="gb-bom-jesus-stairway" size="full" caption="The zigzag stairway of Bom Jesus do Monte, climbing to the church at the top." />

You can climb the stairs, which is the point, or ride up and walk down. The sanctuary's [funicular](https://bomjesus.pt/bom-jesus/elevador-ou-funicular/) was inaugurated in 1882 and climbs 116 metres along a 267-metre track; the sanctuary says it is the oldest funicular in service in the world to use a water-counterweight system. Hours for the funicular and the basilica are on the sanctuary's [information page](https://bomjesus.pt/informacoes/), which also lists the current bus arrangements from Braga.

Stay for the late light. From the terrace in front of the church, the city spreads out below, and on a clear evening the hills beyond turn blue.

## Where to stay

A day trip is enough to see the highlights, but an overnight lets you see both towns without watching the clock.

- **[Pousada Mosteiro Guimarães](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-guimaraes)** occupies what the pousada describes as an imposing 12th-century Augustinian monastery, a short distance from the historic centre, with views over the town.
- **[Hotel do Elevador](https://www.hoteisbomjesus.pt/hotel-do-elevador-em-braga/)**, at Bom Jesus, stands beside the water-powered funicular and has 22 rooms and a restaurant with a panoramic view over the city.

If you are building a longer route through the country, our [ten-day Portugal itinerary](/stories/ten-day-portugal-itinerary/) shows how the north fits in. And if this is your first trip, start with the [first-timer's guide to Portugal](/stories/first-timers-guide-to-portugal/).

## One day or two?

If you have only one free day from Porto, choose. Guimarães is the more atmospheric old town; Braga has the bigger single sight in Bom Jesus. If you have two days, give each town its own, with a night in between, and the trains become a pleasure rather than a puzzle. Either way, you will come back to [Porto](/stories/porto-in-48-hours/) with a better sense of where Portugal began.

## Before you book

- Check the urban train timetable on [CP's site](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) and plan the change for the second town.
- Confirm the Paço dos Duques and castle [opening days](https://pacodosduques.gov.pt/planear-visita/bilhetica-horarios/?lang=en), especially around 24 June, a city holiday in Guimarães.
- Check the Bom Jesus [funicular hours](https://bomjesus.pt/informacoes/) for the season.
- Wear shoes for granite steps and cobbles; Bom Jesus alone is a serious climb.
- Pack a waterproof outside summer. The north of Portugal is greener than the south for a reason.
