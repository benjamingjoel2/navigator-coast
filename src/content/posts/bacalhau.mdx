Walk into any old grocer's in Lisbon or Porto and you will smell it before you see it: a sharp, clean, faintly ammoniac tang of salt and sea. Then you see it. Stiff, greyish-white slabs stacked like roof tiles on the counter, whole sides of fish hanging from hooks, a stainless-steel guillotine by the till for cutting a piece to order. This is *bacalhau*, salt cod, and the Portuguese have been building their kitchens around it for centuries.

The curious thing is that cod does not swim in Portuguese waters. Every slab on that counter came from the cold seas of the far north. How a fish caught a long way from home became the closest thing Portugal has to a national dish is one of the great stories of European food, and knowing it makes every plate taste better.

## A fish from the far north

For most of its history, Portugal fetched its cod. The [Museu Marítimo de Ílhavo](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/conhecer/o-museu/historia), on the coast near Aveiro, lists cod fishing in the seas of Newfoundland and Greenland as one of the founding themes of its collection. Generations of men from towns like Ílhavo sailed north each year, fished in punishing conditions and salted the catch on board so it would keep for the long voyage home.

<Figure id="bac-atlantic-cod" caption="An Atlantic cod, the fish behind the whole story, swimming in dark water." />

Salt and wind were the whole technology. Split, salted and dried, cod becomes almost indestructible, which made it the ideal protein for a seafaring, largely Catholic country with many meatless days in the calendar. It travelled to the interior, where fresh fish could not, and it kept through the winter.

Today the fishing fleet is a memory and the fish arrives by trade. The Norwegian Seafood Council says on its [Portugal page](https://en.seafood.no/countrypages/portugal/) that Portugal is the largest market for Norwegian clipfish, the salted and dried cod sold here as *bacalhau da Noruega*, and that cod consumption in Portugal consists almost entirely of clipfish. The same page notes a downward trend in consumption, but you would not guess it from a December supermarket.

## How to read a cod counter

Shopping for bacalhau is a small education in itself. A few things worth knowing:

- **It is sold dry and salty.** What you buy is not ready to cook. It needs days of soaking (more on that below) unless you buy it already soaked, *demolhado*, which many supermarkets sell chilled or frozen.
- **Thickness matters.** The thick loin pieces, the *lombo*, are prized for grilling and roasting. Thinner tail and belly pieces are for shredding into dishes like bacalhau à Brás.
- **Look at the colour.** The Codfish History Interpretation Centre's [cooking tips](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/codfish-encyclopedia) advise checking that there are no dark streaks of blood or other stains on the flaps.
- **Cure takes time.** The same tips say good salt cod needs a curing process of several months, at least six.

In Lisbon's Baixa, [Manteigaria Silva](https://www.manteigariasilva.pt/), at Rua D. Antão de Almada, has been trading since 1890 and lists bacalhau among the cheeses, hams and other Portuguese products it sells. In Porto, the restored [Mercado do Bolhão](https://mercadobolhao.pt/) is the place to see what the city cooks with; its site gives market hours of Monday to Friday 8am to 7pm and Saturday 8am to 6pm, and it is closed on Sundays.

## Soaking: the step you cannot skip

If you rent an apartment with a kitchen and want to cook bacalhau yourself, this is the part to plan for. The Codfish History Interpretation Centre's guidance is clear: [soak it for about 48 hours](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/codfish-encyclopedia), changing the water three times, after rinsing the fish two or three times first. Running fresh water is best; failing that, use cold water in the fridge. It also recommends cutting fillets to the same thickness so they desalt evenly.

Two more of its tips are worth stealing. Do not boil the fish: bring water with garlic and a bay leaf to the boil, add the cod, take the pan off the heat and let it rest. And cook it with the skin and bones on, which keeps the flavour and, simmered afterwards, gives you a gelatinous stock to thicken a sauce.

If 48 hours is more forward planning than your holiday allows, buy it pre-soaked, or leave it to the professionals.

## The classics, in brief

Portuguese cooks like to say there are more ways to cook bacalhau than days in the year. Nobody has counted, and it does not matter. These are the ones you will meet on menus most often.

### Bacalhau à Gomes de Sá

A Porto dish, baked in the oven: flaked cod layered with sliced potatoes and onions, plenty of olive oil, then finished with boiled egg and black olives. The [Codfish History Interpretation Centre](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/node/7) credits José Luís Gomes de Sá Júnior, born in Porto in 1851, the son of a cod merchant who kept a warehouse on Rua do Muro dos Bacalhoeiros, down by the river. Legend has it he sold the recipe to a cook at a Porto restaurant. Its recipe soaks the fish in milk, which gives the cod a softer, rounder flavour.

<Figure id="bac-gomes-de-sa" caption="A baked dish of salt cod, potatoes, egg and black olives, in the style of Gomes de Sá." />

### Bacalhau à Brás

The Lisbon favourite and the one most visitors fall for. Shredded cod is fried with onion and matchstick potatoes, then bound with softly scrambled egg and finished with parsley and black olives. Done well it is creamy, salty and very hard to stop eating.

### Bacalhau com natas

Cod baked with cream, onions and potatoes until bubbling and browned on top. Comfort food, and a staple of canteens and family lunches.

### Bacalhau à lagareiro

A thick piece of loin roasted and then drenched in hot olive oil with garlic, served with small potatoes that have been baked and lightly crushed, the *batatas a murro*. This is the dish to order when you want to taste the fish itself.

### Pastéis de bacalhau

Small, torpedo-shaped fritters of cod, mashed potato, egg and parsley, sold in every café and pastelaria as a snack. Northerners and southerners will argue over whether to call them *pastéis* or *bolinhos*. Have one with a glass of white wine or a cold beer at the counter.

## Christmas Eve: the night of the cod

If there is one night when bacalhau is non-negotiable, it is 24 December. The *consoada*, the Christmas Eve supper, centres on the plainest preparation of all: cod poached and served with boiled potatoes, cabbage or kale and eggs, all dressed at the table with olive oil and garlic. The Codfish History Interpretation Centre's [Christmas Eve cod recipe](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/recip/christmas-eve-cod) adds a splash of vinegar to the oil and says the tradition began in the north of Portugal, while admitting its exact origin is hard to pin down.

In the weeks before Christmas, the cod counters get busier and the piles get taller. If you are in Portugal in December, a walk through a market or a big supermarket's bacalhau aisle is a festive sight in its own right. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-portugal/) has more on what the season looks like across the country.

## Where to go deeper

### In Lisbon: the Codfish History Interpretation Centre

On the riverside square of Terreiro do Paço, in the eastern tower, the [Centro Interpretativo da História do Bacalhau](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/introduction-codfish-history-interpretation-centre) tells the story of the cod voyages, from the discovery of Newfoundland to the fishermen who went north. Its site gives opening hours of 10am to 7pm, and the nearest metro is Terreiro do Paço on the blue line. It is an easy hour to fold into a day in the Baixa; if you are still deciding where to base yourself, our comparison of [Lisbon and Porto](/stories/lisbon-or-porto/) may help.

<Figure id="bac-terreiro-do-paco" caption="The Rua Augusta arch on Terreiro do Paço, the square that is home to the Codfish History Interpretation Centre." />

### In Ílhavo: the Maritime Museum

For the full story, go to the coast south of Porto. The [Museu Marítimo de Ílhavo](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/conhecer/o-museu/historia) was founded in 1937 and has been in its modern building since 2001. It now includes an [aquarium of Atlantic cod](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/conhecer/aquario-de-bacalhaus/historia), opened in January 2013, so you can see the live fish that the whole story is about.

<Pair a="bac-aveiro-nets" b="bac-aveiro-boats" captionA="Fishing nets piled on the deck of a boat in Aveiro." captionB="Small boats moored on the water at Aveiro, next door to Ílhavo." />

The museum also runs the [Santo André](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/conhecer/navio-museu-santo-andre/historia), a side trawler built in the Netherlands in 1948 for an Aveiro fishing company and once part of the Portuguese cod fleet. At close to 71 metres long, with a hold for 1,200 tonnes of fish, it gives a vivid sense of the scale of the work. The museum's [main site](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/) lists opening hours of Tuesday to Sunday, 10am to 1pm and 2pm to 6pm, closed on Mondays; check before you go.

<Figure id="bac-ilhavo-museum" caption="The Museu Marítimo de Ílhavo, in its modern building since 2001, with a ship's anchor on the square outside." />

## Eating it well

A few last tips for ordering:

- **Read the whole menu.** Bacalhau often has a section of its own, separate from both fish and meat.
- **Ask what the house does best.** Many restaurants are proud of one particular preparation, and the staff will tell you.
- **Expect generous portions.** A single dish of bacalhau à Brás or com natas is often enough for two light eaters.
- **Pair it with fresh seafood too.** Salt cod is only half of Portugal's fish story; our guide to [Portuguese seafood](/stories/portuguese-seafood/) covers the rest, and our [region-by-region food guide](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) puts both in context.

## Before you book

- If you plan to visit the Ílhavo museum, check its [current hours](https://museumaritimo.cm-ilhavo.pt/) and remember it is closed on Mondays.
- Check the Codfish History Interpretation Centre's [site](https://historiabacalhau.pt/en/introduction-codfish-history-interpretation-centre) for hours and tickets before you go.
- Cooking it yourself? Buy pre-soaked cod, or allow about two days of soaking with the water changed three times.
- Travelling in December? Book Christmas Eve dinner early, or ask whether restaurants are open at all on 24 and 25 December.
- Bringing some home? Check your own country's rules on importing fish products before you pack a slab in your suitcase.
