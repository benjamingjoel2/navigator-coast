Lisbon greets you with a hill. Then another. The city climbs in every direction from the river, its lanes paved with the black-and-white limestone mosaic called calçada, polished smooth by centuries of feet and slick after rain. Porto is steeper still, its old houses stacked up the gorge above the Douro. They are two of the most beautiful cities in Europe, and neither was built with wheelchairs, walking frames or tired knees in mind.

That is the honest starting point. The encouraging part is how much has changed: step-free trams and trains on key routes, airport and rail assistance services, museums with ramps and lifts, and a national scheme that equips beaches for visitors with reduced mobility. Accessible travel in Portugal works best with a little more planning than usual. Here is where to start.

## Know the terrain

In both cities, the historic cores are the hardest ground. In Lisbon that means Alfama, Mouraria, the Bairro Alto and the slopes around the castle; in Porto, Ribeira and the streets climbing to the cathedral. Expect gradients, steps, narrow pavements and cobbles. Flatter, more manageable areas include Lisbon's Baixa grid, the riverside west towards Belém, and the newer avenues; and in Porto, the area around Avenida dos Aliados and the boulevards to the west.

That shapes where you stay. A hotel in a lovely lane can become a trial if every outing starts with a climb, so ask specific questions before booking: how many steps to the entrance, whether there is a lift and how big it is, whether the room has a roll-in shower, and how close a taxi can get to the door. Our guides to [where to stay in Lisbon](/stories/where-to-stay-in-lisbon/) and [where to stay in Porto](/stories/where-to-stay-in-porto/) describe the character of each neighbourhood, and the first question to ask any of them is about the street outside.

## Flying in: airport assistance

The EU's [Your Europe guide to passenger rights](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/transport-disability/reduced-mobility/index_en.htm) sets the ground rules for flights. Assistance is free, and you should tell your airline, ticket seller or tour operator at least 48 hours before travelling. Airlines must carry a recognised assistance dog, medical equipment and two pieces of mobility equipment at no extra cost.

At the airports run by ANA, including Lisbon, Porto, Faro and Funchal, the assistance service is called MyWay. According to [Azores Airlines' explanation of the service](https://www.azoresairlines.pt/en/node/56), on arrival at the airport you go to a designated point of arrival, a phone booth marked with the MyWay logo, and call to say you are there. Staff then help with you and your luggage through check-in, security, border control and boarding, all the way to your seat. Our guide to [arriving at Lisbon airport](/stories/arriving-at-lisbon-airport/) covers the onward journey into the city.

## Trains: CP's assistance service

Portugal's national rail operator, CP, runs an assistance service called SIM, the Integrated Mobility Service. CP's [page on specific mobility needs](https://www.cp.pt/info/w/specific-needs) says the service is provided on all trains and at all stations for passengers who do not use a wheelchair or mobility scooter. For wheelchair and scooter users, it is available only at the stations on CP's published list, so check that both ends of your journey are on it. Requests go through CP's SIM form.

Your Europe notes that for rail travel, you should generally request assistance at least 24 hours ahead, and some countries ask for more. Allow time, and request each leg of a journey separately.

CP has been widening access. Its [inclusive mobility report for 2024](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/institucional/inclusive-mobility-2024) says the number of accessible stations rose from 139 to 216 that year, that it bought ramps allowing wheelchair users to board Porto's urban trains at every station on that network, acquired portable ramps for the Cascais line, and fitted wheelchair lifts to 55 regional electric units. For the bigger picture of trains, metros and coaches, see our [guide to getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

## Metros in Lisbon and Porto

Lisbon's metro is the fastest way across the city, but not every station is step-free. Metro de Lisboa's [guide to using the metro](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/travel/using-the-metro) advises passengers with reduced mobility to check the network map in advance, to make sure both the departure and arrival stations are accessible. It also recommends checking whether lifts and escalators are working before you travel, by contacting its call centre. Wide gates are reserved for people with reduced mobility, and help points at the gates and ticket machines connect you with staff.

In Porto, Portugal's transport regulator, AMT, states in its [2025 report on Metro do Porto](https://www.amt-autoridade.pt/media/5946/2025_relatorio_desempenho_mpsa.pdf) that at the end of 2025 the whole fleet of 120 vehicles was fully accessible to people with reduced mobility. As in Lisbon, check the specific stations you need, as access from the street varies.

## Museums and gardens in Lisbon

Some of Lisbon's best cultural visits are also among its most accessible. The [Gulbenkian](https://gulbenkian.pt/en/useful-information/accessibility/), set in a modernist complex and garden near Avenida de Berna, says its Main Building has ramps indoors and out that allow wheelchairs to circulate in all areas, with stairlifts and wheelchair lifts operated with the help of security staff. It lists adapted public toilets, disabled parking near the lifts in its car park, and accessible routes and equipment in parts of the garden.

By the river at Belém, [MAAT](https://www.maat.pt/en/accessibility), the museum of art, architecture and technology, publishes an unusually detailed accessibility page. It describes ramps and lifts to its galleries, a platform lift beside an alternative entrance at the MAAT Central building, accessible toilets in both buildings, and wheelchairs available on request. It is also candid about the difficulties: one lift is small and may not take larger wheelchairs, and some ramps, including the one to the roof terrace, are steep. That kind of detail is exactly what you need to plan a day. Our guide to [Belém in a day](/stories/belem-in-a-day/) shows what else lies along that flat riverside stretch.

## Porto: Serralves

In Porto, the [Serralves Foundation](https://serralves.pt/en/institucional-serralves/acessibilidades), with its contemporary art museum, Art Deco villa and large park, lists easy-access ramps, a lift, an adapted toilet, wheelchairs, reserved parking for people with reduced mobility and support for electric carts. Audio description of the villa is offered through an app you can download from the Foundation's website. Its page also notes that the wheelchair traction equipment usually available at the museum ticket office is currently under maintenance, so ask before you go if you were counting on it. The park is large and landscaped, so plan your route.

## Accessible beaches

Portugal has a national scheme for this. The [Praia Acessível, Praia para Todos programme](https://business.turismodeportugal.pt/pt/Gerir/reconhecimento-externo/premios-distincoes/Paginas/praia-acessivel-praia-para-todos.aspx), set up in 2005 and promoted by Turismo de Portugal with national partners, awards a flag to bathing areas that meet a set of accessibility conditions. These include easy pedestrian access, accessible parking, a route to the sand by level ground, ramp or mechanical means, walkways on the sand, adapted toilets and a first-aid post.

In 2026, the programme flagged 239 bathing areas, on the coast and inland, and 215 of them have equipment to help people into the water, such as amphibious chairs. Water-access equipment is recommended rather than required, so if you need it, confirm it is available at the beach you have in mind. Turismo de Portugal publishes the list of flagged beaches each year.

## Planning tools

The [TUR4all platform](https://www.tur4all.com/), launched by Accessible Portugal with a Spanish partner, collects accessibility information on hotels, restaurants, museums, monuments and adapted transport, and lets users add comments. It is a useful place to cross-check what a venue says about itself.

Whatever you find online, contact the place directly with specific questions. Measurements of doors and lifts, whether a step is at the entrance, and whether equipment is working today are the details that make or break a visit.

## Before you book

- Ask your airline for assistance at least 48 hours ahead, as [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/transport-disability/reduced-mobility/index_en.htm) advises, and note where the MyWay arrival points are.
- For trains, check CP's [list of stations with SIM](https://www.cp.pt/info/w/specific-needs) for wheelchair users and request help in advance.
- In Lisbon, check the metro map and lift status before travelling, as [Metro de Lisboa](https://www.metrolisboa.pt/en/travel/using-the-metro) recommends.
- Ask hotels about steps, lifts, shower access and taxi access to the door, in writing.
- Check museum accessibility pages, such as [MAAT's](https://www.maat.pt/en/accessibility), for lift sizes and steep ramps.
- If you want a beach day, look for the Praia Acessível flag and confirm water-access equipment is available.
