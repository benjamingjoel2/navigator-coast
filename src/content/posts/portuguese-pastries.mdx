Every Portuguese street has one. A pastelaria with a glass counter running the length of the room, a coffee machine hissing at one end, and trays behind the glass that change as you travel. In Lisbon the custard tarts sit in neat ranks. In Sintra the counter fills with little cheese tarts and long, sugared pillows of pastry. In Aveiro the sweets come in white wafer shells shaped like clams and fish.

The pastel de nata is the famous one, and rightly so. But treating it as the whole story is like visiting Portugal and only seeing Lisbon. The country's sweet tooth runs through convents, market towns and coastal lagoons, and almost every region has a pastry it will defend against all comers.

## How to eat in a pastelaria

The rhythm is simple. Point at what you want, order a coffee, and either stand at the counter or take a table. Mid-morning and mid-afternoon are the busy times, when office workers come in for a bica and something sweet. Nobody expects you to know the names; pointing is perfectly polite.

Look past the obvious, too. The everyday counter staples include the bolo de arroz, a tall rice-flour cake baked in a paper wrapper, and the bola de Berlim, a fat doughnut split and filled with egg custard. These are the pastries locals actually eat on a Tuesday, and they are worth an order alongside whatever regional speciality brought you in.

## Lisbon: the nata and the bolo-rei

Start where everyone starts. Visit Lisboa calls the pastel de nata [Lisbon's most famous cake](https://www.visitlisboa.com/en/p/lisbon-flavours/specialties/pastel-de-nata) and notes that, despite the name, it does not actually contain cream. Its roots, the tourism board says, lie in the tradition of convent-style desserts, and it is best eaten hot with a dusting of cinnamon and a bica on the side.

<Figure id="pas-nata-closeup" size="column" caption="The nata at its best: blistered on top, the custard barely set beneath." />

[Pastéis de Belém](https://pasteisdebelem.pt/en/), at Rua de Belém 84 to 92, says it began making its pastries in 1837 to an old recipe from the nearby Jerónimos Monastery. It is the obvious stop if you are spending a morning in Belém, and our [day in Belém](/stories/belem-in-a-day/) fits it in between the monastery and the river.

In the Baixa, [Confeitaria Nacional](https://confeitarianacional.com/) has been on Praça da Figueira since it opened in 1829. It says it introduced the bolo-rei to Portugal in 1875: the ring-shaped Christmas cake studded with crystallised fruit. The shop also calls its bolo de arroz a signature of the house, which makes it a good place to try the humbler staples too.

## Sintra: queijadas and travesseiros

Take the train to Sintra and the counters change. Two pastries rule here, and they could hardly be more different.

**Queijadas de Sintra** are small, crisp tartlets with a dense, cinnamon-scented filling. Visit Sintra lists the filling as [fresh cheese, sugar, egg yolks, flour and cinnamon](https://visitsintra.travel/en/eat-drink/regional-flavours/queijadas-de-sintra) in a dough of flour and salt, and traces the sweet's origins to the 13th century. Until the mid-18th century, the same page says, queijadas were made at home and even used as a form of payment linked to land rents, before production moved to well-known workshops in the town.

**Travesseiros**, or "pillows", are long rolls of puff pastry filled with egg and almond cream and rolled in sugar. Visit Sintra describes them as made from [puff pastry, egg candy, almonds and sugar](https://visitsintra.travel/en/eat-drink/regional-flavours/travesseiros) and credits their rise to Piriquita, in the old town, where the founder's granddaughter found the recipe in an old cookbook during the Second World War.

[Casa Piriquita](https://piriquita.pt/) says it was founded in 1862, and it lists both the travesseiro and the queijadas de Sintra among its specialities. Visit Sintra places it at Rua das Padarias 1, in the centre of the old town. Expect a queue on busy days.

If you are planning the trip, our [Sintra day trip guide](/stories/sintra-day-trip/) covers the trains and the palaces, so you can build a pastry stop around them.

## Aveiro: ovos moles

Aveiro, on its lagoon south of Porto, has the most beautiful sweet in the country. Ovos moles, "soft eggs", are a rich paste of egg yolk and sugar enclosed in thin wafer shapes. According to Center of Portugal, the regional tourism board, the recipe [comes from Aveiro's old convents](https://www.centerofportugal.com/poi/ovos-moles-of-aveiro) and was handed down after the communities of nuns disappeared. Confectioners shape the wafer into shells, whelks, fish and clams, a nod to the lagoon and the sea.

The same page notes that ovos moles were the first Portuguese bakery product to receive Protected Geographical Indication status, and the name is registered in the EU's eAmbrosia database as [Ovos Moles de Aveiro PGI](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013759).

<Figure id="pas-aveiro-moliceiros" caption="Moliceiro boats on Aveiro's central canal, a short walk from the town's ovos moles counters." />

[Confeitaria Peixinho](https://ovalordotempo.pt/marcas/confeitaria-peixinho/), on Rua de Coimbra in the centre, describes itself as the oldest ovos moles house in Aveiro, in business since 1856. Our guide to [Aveiro and Costa Nova](/stories/aveiro-and-costa-nova/) has the rest of the town, from the canals to the striped beach houses.

## Protected names worth knowing

Portugal guards its sweets the same way it guards its cheeses and wines. Alongside ovos moles, two more pastries from the centre and north carry the EU's protected label:

- **Pastel de Tentúgal.** A pastry of paper-thin dough wrapped around an egg-and-sugar filling, from the village of Tentúgal near Coimbra. It is registered as [Pastel de Tentúgal PGI](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000013984).
- **Pão de ló de Ovar.** A sponge cake from Ovar, on the coast between Aveiro and Porto, registered as [Pão de Ló de Ovar PGI](https://ec.europa.eu/agriculture/eambrosia/geographical-indications-register/details/EUGI00000015919).

When you buy any of these to take home, look for the PGI seal on the box. It is the simplest way to know the sweet was made where it claims to be.

## The convent tradition

Spend enough time at Portuguese pastry counters and you start to notice how much egg yolk is involved. The nata, the ovos moles, the queijada and the Tentúgal pastry all lean on yolks and sugar, and so do countless lesser-known sweets with heavenly names. Visit Lisboa and Center of Portugal both trace their favourites back to the convents, and that convent-sweet thread runs through the whole country.

If you want to see the buildings behind the story, the great monasteries of the centre are a natural pairing: our guide to [Batalha and Alcobaça](/stories/batalha-alcobaca-monasteries/) covers two of the most impressive.

## The Algarve: almonds, figs and carob

Head south and the pastry case turns nutty. Visit Algarve's own cookery guide describes an [Algarve trilogy of dried fruits](https://api.visitalgarve.pt/uploads/1/1/BROCHURASTEMATICAS/Cozinha%20do%20Algarve_EN.pdf), almonds, figs and carob, that turn up in many of the region's sweets.

<Figure id="pas-algarve-market-stall" caption="A market stall in the Algarve, custard tarts on top and regional sweets packed below." />

The same guide calls the little marzipan cakes, moulded into fruits and other shapes, the hallmark of Algarve confectionery. It names fig "cheese", made with dried figs and almonds, among the region's favourites, and describes carob as an ingredient in many Algarve sweets with a taste similar to chocolate. It also crowns the Dom Rodrigo, a nest of sweet egg-yolk threads around an almond filling, wrapped in coloured foil, as the king of Algarve confectionery, found at practically every pastry shop in the region.

For more of what the regions put on the table, our [region-by-region food guide](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) goes from Porto's francesinha to the Azores.

## What to drink with them

A bica is the default, but sweets and fortified wine are an old partnership. The IVDP, which regulates Port, suggests [10-year-old and 20-year-old tawny Port](https://www.ivdp.pt/en/wines/port-wines/choose-and-enjoy/) with traditional egg-and-sugar desserts. An ovos mole with a small glass of old tawny after dinner is a very good argument for that advice.

## Before you book

- Pastry shops are busiest mid-morning and mid-afternoon; go early if you want the first tray out of the oven.
- Check opening days directly with any historic shop you plan to visit; many close one day a week.
- If you are taking sweets home, buy them on your last day. Egg-based pastries do not keep long, and boxed ovos moles and dry sweets travel better than custard tarts.
- Look for the PGI seal on ovos moles, Tentúgal pastries and pão de ló de Ovar.
- Check your home country's rules on bringing food back before you pack a suitcase of sweets.
