North of Lisbon, the motorway to Porto runs inland, fast and forgettable. Turn off it towards the sea and the country changes character. Fishing harbours sit under sandstone cliffs, walled hill towns look out over orchards, and long Atlantic beaches stretch away into a haze of salt spray. This is the Costa de Prata, the Silver Coast, named for the way the light catches the water, and it is one of the most rewarding drives in Portugal.

It is also, compared with the Algarve, still relatively quiet. You can do the whole stretch from Lisbon to Porto in a long day, but that would miss the point. Give it five or six days and you can walk the clifftops, spend a night inside a medieval wall and visit two of the country's greatest monasteries on the way.

## The route at a glance

| Day | Stop | Highlights |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lisbon to Peniche | Fishing harbour, the cliffs of Cabo Carvoeiro |
| 2 | Berlenga Grande, then Óbidos | Island fort and seabirds; a night inside the walls |
| 3 | Óbidos to Nazaré | The lagoon at Foz do Arelho, the bay of São Martinho |
| 4 | Alcobaça and Batalha | Two UNESCO-listed monasteries |
| 5–6 | Aveiro and Ílhavo | Canals, Costa Nova, porcelain, then on to Porto |

A car makes this trip. Public transport does reach parts of the coast (CP's [timetable page](https://cp.pt/info/en/w/trains-timetables-pdf) lists a Lisbon–Caldas da Rainha service on the Oeste line, a short hop from Óbidos), but the beaches and viewpoints between the towns are where the pleasure is. Some Portuguese motorways have electronic-only tolls collected by overhead gantries; [Portugal Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/) explains how foreign-registered cars can pay, and if you are hiring, ask the rental company how tolls are billed. For more on choosing between car and train, see our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

## Day 1: Peniche

Peniche is a working fishing town on what was once an island, now joined to the mainland by sand. Walk out to Cabo Carvoeiro at the tip of the peninsula in the late afternoon, when the cliffs turn amber and the sea below boils around the rock stacks. Paths follow the clifftops around the headland, and this is the first good walk of the trip: wind in your face, gulls overhead, and nothing between you and America.

Eat fish. This is the town for it, and the harbour-side grills are the place to try whatever came in that morning, simply cooked with potatoes and a salad.

## Day 2: Berlenga Grande, then Óbidos

Out to sea from Peniche lies the Berlengas archipelago, a nature reserve, and the boat trip to its main island, Berlenga Grande, is one of the highlights of this coast. The island's granite cliffs are home to nesting seabirds, and a stone causeway leads to the Fort of São João Baptista on its own rocky islet.

Plan this one in advance. According to the [ICNF's BerlengasPass guidance](https://www.icnf.pt/api/file/doc/f41f0eb68959fb3a), the human carrying capacity of the island has been set at 550 people at any one time, and since 1 June 2022, everyone landing on Berlenga must register in advance on the [BerlengasPass platform](https://berlengaspass.icnf.pt/) and pay a small daily fee (with reductions and exemptions for some groups). Book your registration and your boat together. The system is under review: in November 2026 the government [asked the ICNF to simplify it](https://www.portugal.gov.pt/pt/gc25/comunicacao/comunicados/governo-determina-melhorias-nas-berlengas-e-canaliza-receitas-de-visitas-para-conservacao-da-natureza) and to direct part of the revenue to conservation, so check the current rules before you go. Sea crossings are also at the mercy of the weather.

Back on the mainland, drive inland to Óbidos for the night. The little town is wrapped entirely in its medieval walls, and its lanes of whitewashed houses, edged in blue and yellow, are lovely in the evening once the coach parties have left. Walk a stretch of the walls for the view over the rooftops, and try ginjinha, the local sour-cherry liqueur.

To sleep inside the castle itself, the [Pousada Castelo Óbidos](https://www.pousadas.pt/en/hotel/pousada-obidos) has been housed in the town's medieval castle since 1951. If you are travelling in late autumn or winter, the town's Christmas event, Óbidos Vila Natal, runs [from 27 November 2026 to 3 January 2027](https://www.obidos.pt/). Our guide to [Óbidos and Nazaré](/stories/obidos-and-nazare/) has more on both towns.

## Day 3: Foz do Arelho, São Martinho and Nazaré

Head back towards the sea. At Foz do Arelho, the Óbidos lagoon opens into the Atlantic, and a short walk takes you from calm lagoon water on one side to ocean breakers on the other. Further north, São Martinho do Porto sits on an almost perfectly round bay, its narrow mouth turning the water inside into a sheltered pool.

Then on to Nazaré, which has two faces. Down below is the town beach, a long sweep of sand backed by a seafront promenade. Up on the cliff is the Sítio, the old quarter around the sanctuary church, with the best views on this coast. You can climb up on foot or ride the [Ascensor da Nazaré](https://app.cm-nazare.pt/visitar/onde-ir/monumentos-e-patrimonio/poi/ascensor-da-nazare), a funicular inaugurated in 1889 that climbs 318 metres of track up the slope.

From the Sítio, walk out to the fort and lighthouse on the headland, and look north over Praia do Norte. In winter, this beach is famous for its giant waves; our story on [Nazaré's big waves](/stories/nazare-big-waves/) explains when they come and how to watch safely. Stay back from the cliff edges, whatever the season.

## Day 4: Alcobaça and Batalha

A short drive inland brings you to two of Portugal's finest buildings, close enough to see both in a day.

The [Monastery of Alcobaça](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/) was founded in 1153 by Portugal's first king, Afonso Henriques, and its church and cloisters are among the largest and best preserved of the Cistercian order in Europe. It was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1989. Inside the long, bare, luminous nave are the carved tombs of King Pedro I and Inês de Castro, whose love story is one of the great Portuguese legends.

<Pair a="slv-batalha" b="slv-alcobaca" captionA="Gothic pinnacles on the Monastery of Batalha." captionB="The west front of the Monastery of Alcobaça." />

The [Monastery of Batalha](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/) was begun by royal command in 1387, two years after the battle of Aljubarrota, and was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1983. Its stone lace of pinnacles and tracery is astonishing in the late-afternoon light. The monastery's site lists its seasonal opening hours and notes that it closes on 1 January, Easter Sunday, 1 May and 25 December; both monasteries sell tickets online through their websites. Our guide to [Batalha and Alcobaça](/stories/batalha-alcobaca-monasteries/) covers both visits in depth.

Spend the night in either town, or press on north towards the coast.

## Days 5 and 6: Aveiro, Costa Nova and Ílhavo

The last stretch is the Ria de Aveiro, a vast, shallow lagoon behind a long sand bar. Aveiro itself is threaded with canals, where painted moliceiro boats carry visitors past Art Nouveau facades. Out on the sand bar, Costa Nova is famous for its rows of wooden houses painted in bold stripes, and the beach beyond runs north for miles: walk it at low tide, with the dunes on one side and the Atlantic on the other. Our [Aveiro and Costa Nova guide](/stories/aveiro-and-costa-nova/) has more on both.

Between the two lies Ílhavo, home of the Vista Alegre porcelain factory, which dates its brand to 1824. The [Vista Alegre Museum](https://vistaalegre.com/us/t/vaa_visitemuseudavistaalegre_omuseuvistaalegre-1) tells the factory's history through a collection of more than 30,000 pieces, and it has been housed in the old factory buildings since 1964. On the same historic site, the [Montebelo Vista Alegre Ílhavo Hotel](https://montebelohotels.com/montebelo-vista-alegre-ilhavo-hotel/en/home) occupies restored buildings that include the founders' former palace.

From Aveiro it is a short run on to Porto, where the trip ends with the granite city tumbling down to the Douro.

## When to go

Late spring and early autumn are our favourite seasons for walking this coast. Summer brings busier beaches, and winter brings empty clifftops and Nazaré's famous swells. Boat trips to Berlenga depend on the sea, so leave some slack in your plans whatever the month.

## Before you book

- Register on the [BerlengasPass platform](https://berlengaspass.icnf.pt/) before you book a boat to Berlenga, and check the current rules, which are being revised.
- Sort out toll payment through [Portugal Tolls](https://www.pttolls.com/) or your car hire company before you leave Lisbon.
- Check opening hours and buy tickets for [Alcobaça](https://www.mosteiroalcobaca.gov.pt/) and [Batalha](https://www.mosteirobatalha.gov.pt/) online, and avoid the closure days.
- Book a night inside the walls of Óbidos early if your dates fall during Óbidos Vila Natal.
- Pack a windproof layer. Even on sunny days, the wind off the Atlantic on this coast can be strong.
- Respect cliff edges and warning signs everywhere on this coast, especially at Peniche and Nazaré.
