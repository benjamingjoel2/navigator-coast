Walk out of Lagos along the cliff road in the early morning and the town falls quiet behind you. The path runs past pines and prickly pear, the sea below turns from grey to green to an almost improbable turquoise, and then the ground simply stops. Below you, golden rock has been carved by the Atlantic into arches, stacks and tunnels, with small boats threading between them like toys.

That is Ponta da Piedade, and it is the reason many people come to Lagos. But the town itself deserves as much of your time. It is a real place with a working harbour, a walled centre, a fine museum and a hard, important history. It is also the last stop on the Algarve railway, which makes it the natural base for the wild western end of the coast.

## The old town

Lagos sits on the west bank of the Bensafrim river where it meets a wide bay. The historic centre is compact and walkable, a grid of cobbled lanes, whitewashed houses with iron balconies and small squares lined with cafés. Stretches of the old walls still stand, and the streets inside them are at their best early or late in the day, when the light is low and the shutters are open.

<Figure id="lagos-old-town-street" caption="A cobbled lane in Lagos old town, where the houses still keep their shutters and iron balconies." />

Start at the riverfront and drift uphill. You will pass churches, tiled façades and squares where locals sit long over a coffee. Lagos is busy in summer, but even then the back streets have a lived-in feel that the resort strips nearby lack.

The Wednesday market is a good way into local life. The council's [markets page](https://www.cm-lagos.pt/descobrir-lagos/e-util-saber/feiras-e-mercados) lists Viv'o Mercado every Wednesday afternoon at the Mercado de Levante, a market for local and certified organic produce, crafts and food stalls, with later hours in summer than in winter.

## Museu de Lagos and the Igreja de Santo António

If you see only one building in Lagos, make it the **[Núcleo Dr. José Formosinho](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/polos/dr-jose-formosinho/)**, the main site of the Museu de Lagos. It takes in the Igreja de Santo António, a church built for the brotherhood of the town's infantry regiment, whose interior the museum describes as a jewel of Portuguese Baroque gilded woodwork. The collections around it range from archaeology and sacred art to Algarve ethnography and the history of Lagos.

<Pair a="lagos-tiled-facade" b="lagos-piedade-stairs" captionA="A tiled façade on one of central Lagos's squares." captionB="The steps that wind down to the water at Ponta da Piedade." />

The museum is now a network of sites. According to its [own description](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/o-museu/), it groups the Formosinho site, the Rota da Escravatura, the Forte Ponta da Bandeira and other spaces under one organisation. The [visiting page](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/visitar/) lists opening from Tuesday to Sunday with a lunchtime break, sets out current tickets, and notes that the fort is temporarily closed. Check it before you go.

### The Rota da Escravatura

Lagos has a darker story to tell, and it tells it plainly. The museum's [Rota da Escravatura](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/polos/rota-da-escravatura/) explains that in 2009, archaeologists working ahead of an underground car park just outside the old walls found the remains of more than 150 people in a refuse dump of the early modern period. Studies showed they were Africans brought to Lagos as merchandise. The exhibition sets that discovery in the history of the Atlantic slave trade. It is not an easy visit, but it is an essential one.

### Forte Ponta da Bandeira

By the harbour mouth stands the small square fort of Ponta da Bandeira. The museum's [page on the fort](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/polos/forte-ponta-da-bandeira/) calls it one of the best preserved 17th-century fortifications in the Algarve, reached by a drawbridge and with a small tiled chapel inside. It is temporarily closed, but its walls frame some of the best views across the bay at sunset.

## Ponta da Piedade

The headland south of town is Lagos's great natural sight. The council's [coast and beaches guide](https://www.cm-lagos.pt/images/site/cliente/AF_LAGOS_PRAIAS_E_COSTA.pdf) describes golden rock sculpted by wind and tide over thousands of years into formations with local names such as the scales, the giant and the shoe. Boardwalks wind along the clifftop from the lighthouse, which the municipal photo archive records as [first lit on 1 July 1913](https://fototeca.cm-lagos.pt/monumentos-e-patrimonio/farol-da-ponta-da-piedade).

There are two ways to see it, and you should try both. From above, follow the boardwalks for the long views over the stacks. From the water, take a small boat or join a guided kayak trip into the grottoes, which you can only really understand at sea level. The same guide notes that many of the beaches between the town and the headland are linked by tunnels in the rock, best explored on foot at low tide or by small boat.

<Figure id="lagos-camilo-cove" caption="Prickly pear and a small cove on the road out to Ponta da Piedade." />

If the sea grottoes appeal, read our guide to [visiting the Benagil sea cave](/stories/benagil-sea-caves/) further east, where the rules on boats and kayaks are now much stricter. The grottoes off Ponta da Piedade are a calmer alternative for many visitors.

## Beaches

Lagos has two very different kinds of beach, and the council's [beach pages](https://www.cm-lagos.pt/descobrir-lagos/visitar/praias) describe both.

**The coves.** Between the town and Ponta da Piedade, a run of small beaches sits tucked between cliffs. Praia da Dona Ana is reached by a staircase and is known for its blue-green water. Praia do Camilo is a small, shell-shaped cove between the cliffs. Praia do Pinhão, halfway between Lagos and the headland, is reached on foot along a clifftop path from Dona Ana and has no beach facilities or lifeguards. These coves are beautiful and, in high summer, crowded. Go early.

**Meia Praia.** East of the river mouth, Meia Praia follows the curve of the bay for more than four kilometres towards the Ria de Alvor. The council's guide notes it is accessible for people with reduced mobility, has lifeguards in season, and is one of the places where the Arte Xávega, a traditional beach-seine fishery hauled in by hand, is still worked. It is the beach for long walks, families and space.

## Eating in Lagos

Lagos eats like the coast it sits on: grilled fish, clams, octopus and the Algarve's cataplana, the copper pan in which seafood and pork are steamed together. Our guide to [what to eat in Portugal](/stories/what-to-eat-in-portugal/) covers the region's dishes in more detail. In the old town, the simplest rule works best: look for places where the fish is on display and the menu is short.

## Getting there and getting around

Lagos is the western terminus of the Algarve railway. CP announced that from 19 July 2026 the [whole line was electrified](https://www.cp.pt/info/en/w/improved-service-algarve-line), with electric trains running between Lagos and Vila Real de Santo António. The [current regional timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/comboios-regionais-vila-real-s-antonio-lagos-19-julho-2026) lists the stops, including Silves, Faro and Tavira, so an easy day trip east to [Tavira](/stories/tavira-guide/) is possible without a car. For the bigger picture on trains, coaches and tolls, see our guide to [getting around Portugal](/stories/getting-around-portugal/).

West of Lagos, the railway ends and the coast grows wilder. The Rota Vicentina's [region guide](https://rotavicentina.com/en/the-region/) notes that from Lagos, national coaches run by Rede Expressos and regional VAMUS Algarve buses serve Sagres, Cabo de São Vicente, Vila do Bispo, Aljezur and Odeceixe. That makes Lagos a good starting point for [Sagres and Cape St Vincent](/stories/sagres-and-cape-st-vincent/), and for walkers heading north on the [Rota Vicentina](/stories/walking-the-rota-vicentina/).

<Figure id="lagos-cliffs-sunset" caption="Evening light on the cliffs near Lagos, looking west along the coast." />

The old town is best on foot, and you will not want a car inside the walls. If you hire one for trips west, check where you can park before you book your room.

## Where to stay

Base yourself in or near the old town if you want to walk everywhere, including to the train and bus stations. [Lagosmar Hotel](https://www.lagosmar.com/), on Rua Dr. Faria e Silva, is a family-run hotel that describes itself as renovated in 2009 and within walking distance of the town centre, the railway station and the bus station. It suits a car-free trip.

If you want space and the sea, look along Meia Praia, where the long beach is on your doorstep and the old town is a walk or short ride away. For a quieter, more seasonal Lagos, our piece on [the Algarve in winter](/stories/algarve-in-winter/) explains why the town is one of the region's best year-round bases.

## When to go

Lagos is at its liveliest from June to September, when the coves fill and the boat trips run constantly. Spring and autumn bring warm days, quieter beaches and easier booking. In winter the town stays awake, unlike some resorts nearby, and the clifftop walks are at their best on clear, cool mornings.

## Before you book

- Check the museum's [visiting page](https://museu.cm-lagos.pt/visitar/) for current hours and closures; the Forte Ponta da Bandeira is listed as temporarily closed.
- If you are coming by train, look up the [CP Algarve timetable](https://www.cp.pt/info/documents/d/cp/comboios-regionais-vila-real-s-antonio-lagos-19-julho-2026) for your dates.
- Book boat or kayak trips to the Ponta da Piedade grottoes with a licensed operator, and be ready for cancellations in rough seas.
- Plan beach days around the tide if you want to walk between the coves through the rock tunnels.
- In July and August, book accommodation early and reach the small coves first thing in the morning.
